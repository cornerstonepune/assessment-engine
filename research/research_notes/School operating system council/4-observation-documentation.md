# Observation and documentation: methods, products, evidence, and what Cornerstone should borrow

Seat: "observation and documentation", Cornerstone design council. Written 2026-09-26. Every URL below was opened during this research (web fetch or direct download); PDFs were text-extracted locally. Numbers that could not be verified are marked "unverified". Where a domain could not be reached, that is stated in Gaps rather than papered over.

## Key question 1 — Products: unit of observation, framework linkage, parent view, teacher time, AI by 2026, complaints

### Takeaway
Every mainstream product uses the same atom — a media-plus-note "observation" tagged to one or more children and optionally to framework statements — and the 2024–2026 wave of AI features is almost entirely *text-side* (grammar/tone, drafting from typed notes, summaries), with only one UK start-up (TeachScribe) shipping a voice-first capture path. Documented time costs are vendor claims; the one product with a published third-party critique literature (ClassDojo) is criticised for exactly the thing Cornerstone's pilot is not doing — public, numeric behaviour scoring.

### Inferences
- The industry's observation atom (media + note + child tags + optional framework tags + visibility flag) is stable across a decade of products; Cornerstone's extraction step should emit exactly that shape so future export/import and teacher expectations line up.
- Storypark's fact sheet is the most explicit disclosure of what an early-years AI feature sends to a model (first name, age, gender, past stories; never images or other tagged children). Cornerstone's rule "prompts receive images, not names" is stricter than the market leader and should be kept.
- Every vendor's AI is gated on typed input and human approval; the only voice-first product is pre-scale and government-grant-funded (see KQ4). Cornerstone's pilot is therefore ahead of the market, which means there is no adoption evidence to borrow — it must generate its own.
- The ClassDojo literature is a warning about *public, numeric, behaviour-scored* documentation, not about private narrative observation. Cornerstone's affect/wellbeing notes must never become a per-child score visible to peers or displayed in class.

### Gaps
- Time cost per observation is reported only as vendor or customer claims (Transparent Classroom "10 minutes a day"; Educa "cuts administration time in half"; Famly "an entire day each month" for 60 children); no independent time-and-motion study of any product was found.
- Lillio's AI features could not be verified (feature page and Google Play listing did not render); Kinderpedia AI is named but undocumented on the pages reached.
- Seesaw's 2025 product-updates page (403) and Kriyo's corporate site (the kriyo.com domain served unrelated content) could not be read; Kriyo facts come from the App Store only.
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of any 2024–2026 AI documentation feature was found; the one pilot study of digital documentation platforms' effect on educators' work is a small activity-theory pilot (Nuttall, Rooney, Gunn & White 2023, *Technology, Pedagogy and Education*, DOI 10.1080/1475939X.2023.2177720; only the abstract opening was readable) — [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?q=%22Impact+of+Digital+Documentation+Platforms+on+Early+Childhood+Educators%22)

## Key question 2 — Methods: Learning Stories, Reggio documentation, Montessori records, anecdotal/running records, work sampling; reliability and observer bias

### Takeaway
Narrative methods (Learning Stories, Reggio documentation) are valued for making learning visible to families but are criticised inside their own traditions for resting on 3–10 minute observations with no guidance on frequency, while checklist-based systems (Work Sampling, GOLD) show that teacher judgement is valid *when structured and repeated across the year* — and the assessor-variance literature shows that a large share of teacher-rating variance is about the teacher, not the child, which is the strongest argument for confirmation, multiple observations and multiple observers.

### Cited Findings
**Learning Stories (Carr, Te Whāriki, Kei Tua o te Pae)**
- Kei Tua o te Pae contained "177 exemplars from early childhood contexts" with assessment principles, narrative practices and a progression framework; Carr argues for the "consequential validity of narrative assessments"; stories and portfolios are the primary methodology — [Carr 2009, Assessment Matters (NZCER)](https://www.nzcer.org.nz/nzcerpress/assessment-matters/articles/kei-tua-o-te-pae-assessing-learning-reaches-beyond-self-and)
- Learning stories are "structured written narratives of significant learning moments, highlighting children's strengths, interests, abilities and dispositions", built on "noticing, recognising and responding" with multiple perspectives; Carr (2001) acknowledged narrative assessment is "time-consuming"; Blaiklock argued the writing load "is not manageable for teachers on a daily basis"; in practice an informal expectation of one story per child per month emerged and created stress ("it just feels like a stress") — [Niles 2015, He Kupu](https://www.hekupu.ac.nz/article/assessment-practices-within-new-zealand-early-childhood-settings)
- Blaiklock (2008): observations underlying Learning Stories "typically range from 3-10 minutes", often made while the teacher is also working with children or "after the event"; a 10-minute story for a child attending eight hours a day "would represent approximately 1/1000th of his or her time in the centre"; "Publications on Learning Stories do not provide guidelines on where, when, and how often to make Learning Stories"; by contrast running-record guidance (Smith 1998) is to observe "on several occasions" and "for at least 20 to 40 minutes in one session" to avoid "observing an atypical instance"; Carr's "local validity" means "claims of reliability across settings become less plausible" — [Blaiklock 2008 (PDF)](https://oece.nz/wp-content/uploads/Blaiklock_2008.pdf)
- Wanoa & Johnston (2019): learning stories are "the predominant method of assessment" in NZ ECE; reliability = "the degree of consistency with which teachers construct learning stories from observational evidence"; "use of multiple observers to cross-check information is not typically practical in the ECE setting" because of ratios and time, and children "may not manifest certain learning behaviour in the presence of strangers" (Dunn 2000); the workable substitute is a *familiar* observer, informal sharing among staff made explicit in the story (triangulation), the child's own words, and child plus parental feedback — [Wanoa & Johnston 2019, NZ Annual Review of Education (PDF)](https://ojs.victoria.ac.nz/nzaroe/article/download/6333/5496/8635)

**Reggio Emilia pedagogical documentation**
- Wien, Guyevskey & Berdoussis (2011) describe five phases of learning to document: developing habits, going public with recountings, visual literacy, conceptualising documentation as making learning visible, and sharing "visible theories" for interpretation; teachers initially treated documentation as "an 'add-on'"; one teacher's whole-year goal was "to get used to using the tools of documentation—to have camera and tape recorder at hand"; documentation's purpose is collective interpretation that "can sharpen their thinking about children's understanding" and generate "new 'designs' for further learning" — [Wien et al. 2011, ECRP](https://ecrp.illinois.edu/v13n2/wien.html)
- Knauf (2020, *Early Childhood Education Journal*, DOI 10.1007/s10643-019-00979-9): "From the point of view of teachers, however, documentation must compete with numerous other tasks" (NZ and Germany) — [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?q=Knauf+%22Documentation+Strategies%22+New+Zealand+Germany)
- Yilmaz et al. (2021, *Early Childhood Education Journal*, DOI 10.1007/s10643-020-01113-w): challenges of pedagogical documentation studied with 22 Turkish early childhood teachers in a three-year project introducing it as "a teaching, learning, and assessment tool" — [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?q=Turkish+early+childhood+teachers+challenges+implementing+pedagogical+documentation+Yilmaz)

**Montessori record-keeping** — see Transparent Classroom and NCMPS in KQ1: the record is lesson-centric (presented/practised/mastered per material), with observation notes, adjustment checks "near the beginning of the year", and an individual-child observation form covering "a morning work period" — [NCMPS Tools](https://www.public-montessori.org/tools/); [Transparent Classroom](https://www.transparentclassroom.com/)

**Anecdotal records, checklists, event sampling (definitions as used by India's NCF-FS)**
- "An anecdotal record is an attempt to record in detail a specific episode or event that is of particular interest or concern… what children say and do while they are engaged in a particular activity"; checklists record "whether a child has accomplished a listed" outcome and "assume that all children will proceed through the sequence in the same systematic order", so a "'mix and match' approach that combines checklists with another data collection method is preferred"; event sampling "allows for a combination of both" (NCF-FS pp. 174–176) — [NCF for the Foundational Stage 2022 (PDF)](https://ncert.nic.in/pdf/NCF_for_Foundational_Stage_20_October_2022.pdf)

**Work sampling and structured teacher judgement**
- Work Sampling System (Meisels): teachers rate checklist items "in the fall, winter, and spring using a modified mastery scale: Not Yet, In Process, or Proficient", plus portfolios and a summary report; in a Pittsburgh study (17 Title I classrooms, N = 345, K–3) "Over three-quarters of the correlations were between .50 – .75" with Woodcock-Johnson scores and "WSS ratings were a stronger predictor of test scores than demographic variables"; the publisher then reported use with "more than 850,000 children" annually — [Erikson Institute WSS technical summary (PDF)](https://www.erikson.edu/wp-content/uploads/AERA-FCD-WSS-summary.pdf)
- Meisels et al. (1995, *ECRQ* 10(3):277–296), 100 kindergarteners: "the WSS checklist and summary report had very high internal and moderately high interrater reliability" and "accurately predicted the performance of the children on a norm-referenced achievement battery" — [ERIC EJ512440](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ512440)
- GOLD uses 38 objectives and sells an "Interrater Reliability Certification for teachers" so administrators can "ensure a valid and reliable assessment process at scale" — [Teaching Strategies GOLD](https://teachingstrategies.com/product/gold/)

**How reliable is a teacher's judgement, and how many observations/observers?**
- Waterman, McDermott, Fantuzzo & Gadsden (2012, *ECRQ* 27(1):46–54), Head Start and kindergarten cohorts, HLM: assessor variance "was negligible as associated with extramural assessors but substantial for teacher assessors", i.e. "large portions of the variability in teacher-administered assessments have nothing to do with children's unique performances" — [ERIC EJ947506](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ947506)
- Südkamp, Kaiser & Möller (2012, *J. Educational Psychology* 104(3):743–762): 75 studies; mean correlation between teacher judgements and standardised achievement r = 0.63; "informed" judgements (teacher told what is being judged and against what) are more accurate than uninformed ones — [ERIC EJ993888](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ993888)
- Kaufmann (2024, *PLoS One* 19(7):e0307594), 122 studies / 85,878 students: bare-bones r = .58, artifact-corrected ρ = .65; no difference between language (r = .60) and maths (r = .59); "no evidence that teachers' judgment accuracy depended on student gender"; "At least 10% of variance may be associated with common artifacts" — [PMC11271880](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11271880/)
- Kosel, Bauer & Seidel (2024, *Frontiers in Psychology*): experienced teachers were more accurate than novices (M = 3.47 vs 2.42) and used about 5.32 cues per judgement vs 2.52, integrating "deep cues" (interest, certainty, attentiveness) rather than only surface behaviour — [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1278472/full)
- Hintze & Matthews (2004, *School Psychology Review*) studied the generalizability of systematic direct observation by observing 14 students "twice a day, for 10 school days" with momentary time-sampling and G-theory — a benchmark for how much sampling behavioural observation research uses — [ERIC search record](https://eric.ed.gov/?q=Hintze+Matthews+generalizability+systematic+direct+observation+time+setting)
- Laevers reports the inter-scorer reliability of the Leuven Involvement Scale for Young Children (two observers) as ".90 and thus very satisfactory" (self-report by the scale's author) — [Laevers, CEE 2015 (PDF)](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=121630&p=0)

### Inferences
- No source gives a universal "N observations by M observers" rule for young children. The defensible reading of the evidence is: (a) a single 3–10 minute narrative is weak evidence on its own (Blaiklock); (b) systems that *are* validated (WSS, GOLD) accumulate many observations per child over a term and summarise at three checkpoints a year; (c) teacher-specific variance is large (Waterman), so any judgement that will be acted on should rest on several observations, preferably from more than one adult who knows the child, and a "familiar observer" beats an unfamiliar second rater (Wanoa & Johnston).
- Meta-analytic r ≈ .6 means a teacher's global judgement explains roughly 40% of variance in measured attainment; informed, specific judgements do better. Cornerstone's confirmation queue should therefore ask the teacher to confirm a *specific, observable* statement (competency-level) rather than a global level.
- Learning-story practice shows that an informal quota (one story per child per month) becomes a stressor; Cornerstone should not turn "evidence per child" into a quota.

### Gaps
- Lambert, Kim & Burts' measurement study of GOLD (ScienceDirect) returned 403; its inter-rater statistics could not be verified.
- No study was found that quantifies how many *teacher-recorded* observations are needed for a stable per-child competency estimate in early years; the G-theory literature found is on behaviour observation by trained observers.
- NAEYC's DAP assessment position statement (URL 404) could not be cited.

## Key question 3 — The documentation-burden backlash: England's 2021 EYFS reforms, Ofsted/DfE positions, what was found wasteful and what was kept

### Takeaway
England removed the *expectation of physical evidence and statement-level tracking* and kept *assessment as professional judgement plus parent communication*; the DfE's own evaluation found 82–89% of settings shifted time from observation/tracking to children, with ~45–50% doing less internal tracking, at the cost of a real concern that broad goals make SEND progress harder to plot.

### Cited Findings
- Statutory EYFS framework for group and school-based providers (in force from 1 September 2026), para 2.2: "Assessment should not involve long breaks from interaction with children or require excessive paperwork. When assessing whether an individual child is at the expected level of development, practitioners should draw on their knowledge of the child and their own expert professional judgement. Practitioners are not required to prove this through collection of any physical evidence." Para 2.3 keeps the duty to "keep parents and/or carers up to date with their child's progress and development" — [EYFS framework PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a5f8e4fb00f3323bf1a23a1/EYFS_group_and_school_based_from_September_2026.pdf); publication page states the effective date — [gov.uk EYFS framework](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/early-years-foundation-stage-framework--2)
- Development Matters (updated 4 Sept 2023): the guidance "is not designed to be used as a tick list for generating lots of data"; assessment is about "noticing what children can do and what they know. It is not about lots of data and evidence"; "assessment should not take practitioners away from the children for long periods of time"; it "guides but does not replace, professional judgement"; observation checkpoints "can help you to notice whether a child is at risk of falling behind" — [Development Matters](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/development-matters--2/development-matters)
- DfE consultation response (2020): the reforms aimed to "reduce workload so that teachers can spend more time interacting with children"; pilot schools "reported that their workload had reduced due to the reduced expectations for assessment and evidence-gathering"; "in the absence of external moderation and associated evidence gathering and paperwork, teachers found that their workload had reduced, allowing them to focus on teaching"; the "exceeding" judgement was dropped because it "generates additional unnecessary collection of evidence" — [EYFS reforms: government response (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5efc5b343a6f4023cb365a3c/EYFS_reforms_consultation_-_government_response.pdf)
- DfE/IFF evaluation (published Oct 2023; 923 reception leaders surveyed Oct–Dec 2022 plus 60 interviews Feb–Mar 2023): after the reforms "over four in five of these settings had put less focus on observation/tracking and more focus on spending time with the children (reported by 89% of leaders in school-based settings, 88% of leaders in reception settings, 84% in group-based settings, and 82% of childminders)"; "between 45% and 50% of settings reported they were doing less regular tracking of ELGs"; "between 56% and 67% of all settings reported that time spent on other paperwork (not related to assessment) had reduced"; 21% of staff reported new workload challenges (mainly rewriting curriculum, 18%, and changes to assessments, 13%); "An area of concern raised was whether the reforms catered sufficiently for children with SEND, or other additional needs such as English as an additional language", because staff "have found it difficult to plot the progression of children with SEND… against the new framework, as it was too broad" — [EYFS 2021 Reforms Evaluation Report (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6537e5503099f900117f308f/EYFS_2021_Reforms_Evaluation_Report.pdf); summary with sample details — [Schools Week](https://schoolsweek.co.uk/key-findings-from-the-dfes-eyfs-reform-evaluation-report/)
- Ofsted early years inspection toolkit (updated June 2026, v2.0, for inspections from 1 Sept 2026): "Inspectors are required to view specific documentation. This should be limited to documents relating to the setting's statutory requirements or documents it produces as part of its normal business processes. Inspectors do not need information to be presented in any specific format… We do not need leaders to produce documents specifically for an Ofsted inspection of their setting. This would create unnecessary workload." What is evaluated instead: "how practitioners assess what children know, understand and can do… and using this information to plan children's next steps in learning and to monitor their progress" — [Early years inspection toolkit (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68c15c7f8c6d992f23edd81a/Early_years_inspection_toolkit.pdf)
- Ofsted's Jan 2026 early-years inspection FAQ describes evidence gathering as shared observations, discussion with practitioners and talking to parents, and does not ask for tracking data — [Ofsted early years blog, 23 Jan 2026](https://earlyyears.blog.gov.uk/2026/01/23/early-years-inspections-frequently-asked-questions/)
- Vendor account of what was wasteful (12 Apr 2021): "statement-based tracking (ticking off Development Matters examples)", using statements "as checklists", collecting data "primarily to demonstrate progress to Ofsted and Local Authorities"; Famly removed statement linking and tracking graphs in response — [Famly](https://www.famly.co/blog/end-eyfs-tracking-why-ey-software-needs-to-change)

### Inferences
- What was found wasteful: evidence collected to *prove* a judgement (photos/notes per statement), statement-level tick-list tracking, evidence for external moderation, the "exceeding" band. What was kept: ongoing observation as the basis of professional judgement, statutory checkpoints (progress check at two, EYFSP), informing parents, and using assessment to plan next steps.
- The SEND finding is directly relevant to a learner-graph design: a coarse framework loses the resolution needed for children whose progress is small-step. Cornerstone should keep fine-grained competency nodes *available* for children who need them, without requiring that grain for everyone.
- The English lesson for a voice-first system: capture volume is not the goal; the system should reduce the per-observation cost while *lowering* the number of observations the teacher feels obliged to make — the reform's benefit came from doing less, not faster.

### Gaps
- No comparable official evaluation of documentation burden was found for Australia, NZ or India within this research; the NZ critique literature (KQ2) is the closest analogue.
- The Ofsted FAQ page did not contain an explicit paperwork statement; the toolkit is the authoritative source for that.

## Key question 4 — Voice-first capture: products and research 2024–2026, real-classroom accuracy, adoption, Indian-language ASR

### Takeaway
As of September 2026 exactly one early-years product (TeachScribe, UK, Innovate UK-funded) does what Cornerstone plans — speak → transcribe → identify child → auto-tag curriculum — and it publishes engagement claims but no accuracy figures; Indian-language ASR has improved fast (Sarvam Saaras V3/V4 with keyterm prompting; AI4Bharat IndicConformer open-source) but independent audits show systems still fail on code-mixed and vernacular speech, so roster-as-keyterms plus teacher confirmation is necessary, not optional.

### Cited Findings
**Products**
- TeachScribe: educators "press a button on your lanyard mic, speak naturally, and get a complete observation with curriculum goals tagged in seconds"; the AI "transcribes the audio, identifies the child, and maps the activity to relevant EYFS" goals; dashboard shows curriculum "blind spots"; "Part of the DfE AI Tools for Education programme, funded by Innovate UK"; claims "teachers are over 15x more likely to do a voice observation than a written one" with most done during school hours — [TeachScribe EYFS observation software](https://teachscribe.com/eyfs-observation-software/)
- TeachScribe homepage: "Thousands of Observations Recorded"; "Real-world data from Q1 2026" for the in-hours claim; "2 hrs" per teacher per week / "78 hrs" per year returned (vendor estimates); "GDPR Compliant · UK-Hosted Data"; no accuracy metrics, pricing or named pilot sites — [TeachScribe](https://teachscribe.com/)
- NurseryPad (UK): "Practitioners enter what the child did" (text), AI links it "to the relevant EYFS areas of learning and Early Learning Goals" (7 areas, 17 ELGs), auto-populates two-year checks, "no observation is filed without practitioner sign-off"; plans from free (10 observations) to £299/month — [NurseryPad](https://slatewick.co.uk/nurserypad/)
- Nursery in a Box (8 Apr 2026): "One of the most common areas where AI is discussed is in learning observations… a practitioner may record key notes about an activity, and the system helps turn that into a clear, readable observation aligned with EYFS areas. The practitioner still reviews, edits, and approves the final version." — [Nursery in a Box blog](https://www.nurseryinabox.com/blog/ai-in-early-years-practical-uses/)
- Storypark Assist and Famly Sidekick are typed-input only (KQ1 sources).
- Classroom-audio analytics for *teacher* practice (not child observation): TeachFX distinguishes teacher vs student speech and reports "teacher talk, student talk, group talk, and wait time"; both TeachFX's and Edthena's founders opposed use for formal evaluation because it would discourage participation; cameras/microphones raise privacy protocol needs — [Education Next, 26 June 2024](https://www.educationnext.org/next-gen-classroom-observations-powered-by-ai/)
- Edthena "Observation Copilot" (2025) converts administrators' observation notes "into structured feedback reports" aligned to teaching frameworks — [Wikipedia: Edthena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edthena)

**Indian-language ASR**
- AI4Bharat IndicConformer: "Multilingual Conformer-based Hybrid CTC + RNNT ASR model", 600M parameters, all 22 scheduled languages, MIT licence; reported Hindi WER 13.2 on the ARTPARK-IISc Vaani benchmark — [Hugging Face model card](https://huggingface.co/ai4bharat/indic-conformer-600m-multilingual); monolingual variants also released — [GitHub IndicConformerASR](https://github.com/AI4Bharat/IndicConformerASR)
- Sarvam Saaras V3 (released 10 Feb 2026): 22 Indian languages + English; "~19% WER" on IndicVoices for the 10 most-spoken languages (vs ~22% for V2.5); evaluated on Svarah (Indian English, 117 speakers, 65 districts); "handles code-mixed speech without distorting it" — [Sarvam blog: Saaras V3](https://www.sarvam.ai/blogs/asr); product page: "19.31% WER on the IndicVoices benchmark across 10 languages", trained on "1M+ hours of real Indian audio", "<150ms time-to-first-token", diarization, "code-mixing (Hinglish/Tanglish)" — [Sarvam speech to text](https://www.sarvam.ai/speech-to-text)
- Sarvam docs (Saarika v2.5): 11 languages, "Keyterm Prompting", "Speaker Diarization", "Preserves proper nouns and entities"; on the VISTAAR benchmark WER "Across 11 Languages: 18.32%", "English: 8.26%", CER 4.96% — [Sarvam docs: Saarika](https://docs.sarvam.ai/api-reference-docs/getting-started/models/saarika)
- Sarvam Saaras V4 (announced 23 Sept 2026): five output modes ("verbatim transcription, normalised text, code-mixed text, transliteration and translation"); language-ID error 5.22% across 22 languages (2.9% for top 10); "keyterm prompting" lets developers "input names, product terms, and acronyms"; "16.03% WER on IndicContextEval benchmark with L5 keyword-prompting"; no pricing given — [TechStory, 23 Sept 2026](https://techstory.in/sarvam-ai-launches-saaras-v4-with-multilingual-speech-recognition-capabilities/)
- Sarvam (2 Apr 2026) argues WER/CER "were developed primarily for English" and penalise valid script/spelling variants in Indic output ("The transcript is right. The score is wrong."), proposing LLM-WER, LLM-CER, Intent Score and Entity Preservation — [Sarvam blog: evaluating Indic ASR](https://www.sarvam.ai/blogs/evaluating-indian-language-asr)
- Independent audit (Kumar et al., arXiv 30 Nov 2025) of eight ASR systems (Indic Whisper, Whisper, Sarvam, Google, Gemma3n, Omnilingual, Vaani, Gemini) on real clinical interviews in Kannada, Hindi and Indian English found "substantial variability across models and languages", systems "failing on code mixed or vernacular speech", and gaps by speaker role and gender — [arXiv 2512.10967](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10967)
- DISPLACE-M (arXiv, Mar 2026): benchmark of "spontaneous, noisy and overlapping speech" between frontline health workers and care seekers (40 h dev, 15 h eval) scored with diarization error and time-constrained WER — [arXiv 2603.02813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02813)
- SraVaani 1.0 (arXiv, Aug 2026): open ASR for "65 Indian languages and dialects", 31,255 h unlabeled + 31,263 h labeled speech, "lowest word error rate (WER) on a large number of language-dataset pairs" — [arXiv 2608.08235](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08235)

### Inferences
- There is no published real-classroom accuracy figure for teacher-voice-to-observation anywhere; TeachScribe's 15x engagement claim is the only adoption signal and it is a vendor number. Cornerstone should treat its pilot as the first measured instance and log per-item extraction precision/recall against teacher confirmations from day one (this is also what CLAUDE.md rule 7 demands).
- Roster-as-keyterms maps directly onto what Sarvam now sells as "keyterm prompting" (names, acronyms) and onto the L5 keyword-prompting condition in which V4 reports its best WER; child names are the highest-value keyterms because a mis-attributed observation is the worst failure mode.
- Cornerstone teachers will speak Hinglish/Marathi-English; the audit evidence says this is where systems fail, so the pipeline should transcribe in a code-mix output mode, keep the audio for teacher playback at confirmation time, and score itself with entity (name) preservation rather than raw WER.

### Gaps
- Bhashini (bhashini.gov.in) could not be opened (domain blocked to this session); no Bhashini claim is made here.
- The Nursery World article on TeachScribe's government funding returned 403; funding is cited from TeachScribe's own site only.
- No research project (arXiv searches for learning-story/LLM and teacher-voice-note ASR returned zero results) evaluates LLM extraction of per-child observations from teacher speech; the closest published analogues are typed-note structuring (Storypark, NurseryPad) and teacher-talk analytics (TeachFX).
- Sarvam's V4 blog URL was not found; V4 facts rest on one secondary report (TechStory) and should be re-verified against Sarvam's docs before any procurement decision.

## Key question 5 — Wellbeing and socio-emotional observation: frameworks (Leuven, CASEL) and ethical cautions

### Takeaway
The Leuven scales are designed as quick, repeated, *group-level* screening (two-minute scans, 3–4 screenings a year) to change adult practice, not to label a child; the CASEL-aligned policy consensus is that SEL data is for continuous improvement and must be kept away from high-stakes or sorting uses; and the children's-rights literature warns that behavioural data on children accumulates into profiles with few safeguards — so affect notes need the tightest retention, visibility and non-scoring rules in the system.

### Cited Findings
- Leuven Involvement Scale: 5 levels from "no activity… mentally absent" to "total engagement expressed by concentration and absolute absorption"; author-reported inter-scorer reliability .90; the Process-Oriented Monitoring System (POMS) starts with "screening of the levels of well-being and involvement of each of the children based on observations during a longer period (2 or 3 weeks)" and "Screenings take place 3 or 4 times in a year" — [Laevers, Centre for Experiential Education, Feb 2015 (PDF)](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=121630&p=0)
- Practice guidance: "Observe the children individually or as a group for about two minutes then give a score for wellbeing and/or involvement using the five-point scale. Unless children are operating at 4 or 5, learning will be limited… levels will fluctuate throughout the day"; the purpose is to see "how well practitioners tune in" and respond — [Leuven scales practitioner guide (PDF)](https://irresistible-learning.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/The-Leuven-Well-being-and-Involvement-scales.pdf)
- Critique: MacRae & Jones (2023, *International Journal of Qualitative Studies in Education*) question involvement "as a marker of a quality learning environment" and argue for moving "beyond universal and progressive" readings of it — [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?q=MacRae+Jones+philosophical+reflection+Leuven+Scale+involvement)
- CASEL's five competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, responsible decision-making, each defined as "abilities" (e.g. self-management: "manage one's emotions, thoughts, and behaviors effectively in different situations") — [CASEL framework](https://casel.org/fundamentals-of-sel/what-is-the-casel-framework/)
- Penn State/CASEL-linked issue brief (May 2020): educators, parents and students "largely are repelled by, the prospect of SEL assessment being used for high-stakes accountability"; policy should "encourage SEL assessment data to be used for continuous improvement, not for high-stakes accountability" and "Delineate the high-stakes purposes for which such assessments should not be used" — [SEL Assessment to Support Effective SEL Practices at Scale (PDF)](https://files.constantcontact.com/df591f85401/41f7dd15-eac5-4753-9b72-4e436e467969.pdf)
- Lupton & Williamson (2017, *New Media & Society*): children "are becoming the objects of a multitude of monitoring devices that generate detailed data about them"; dataveillance runs "from in utero through to the school years"; "There remains little evidence that specific instruments to safeguard children's rights in relation to dataveillance have been developed or implemented" — [preprint PDF (CORE)](https://core.ac.uk/download/77612283.pdf)
- ClassDojo critiques (KQ1): behaviour reduced "to a number", "balance sheets of behaviour", public ranking — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/732702); [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/classdojo-raises-concerns-about-childrens-rights-111033)
- UNICEF Policy Guidance on AI for Children v3.0 (Dec 2025) lists ten requirements including "Protect children's data and privacy", "Ensure non-discrimination and fairness", "Provide transparency, explainability and accountability" and "Support children's best interests, development and well-being"; informed by a twelve-country study with children and caregivers — [UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/policy-guidance-ai-children)
- Practitioner ethics text (Ontario, College of ECE 2017 standards): consent to observe "needs to be on file for each child"; confidentiality means protecting identities in documentation and storing notes where "only other educators in the child's program can access"; "Documenting what is actually observed (heard and seen), rather than interpreting" — [Observing Early Learning, Canadian edition](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/observingearlylearningcaned/chapter/ethical-guidelines-when-observing-children/)
- Digital documentation research flags the risk directly: Stratigos & Fenech (2021, *Australasian Journal of Early Childhood* 46(2)) note "limited empirical research about the impact of these applications on children's experiences and educators' practices" — [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?q=%22Early+Childhood+Education+and+Care+in+the+App+Generation%22)

### Inferences
- Leuven-style wellbeing/involvement is best treated as a *class-level, time-boxed screening signal* that prompts adult action, not as a per-child longitudinal score in the learner graph.
- The affect/wellbeing note type in Cornerstone's extraction should be (a) descriptive not evaluative, (b) confirmed by the teacher like everything else, (c) excluded from any aggregate that ranks or compares children, (d) subject to a shorter retention default than skill evidence, and (e) visible to parents only through a human (the educator), never as raw feed.
- Safeguarding bypass is consistent with both the professional-ethics texts (concern → immediate human route) and the rights literature (no algorithmic gatekeeping of a child's protection).

### Gaps
- No independent psychometric study of Leuven inter-rater reliability beyond the author's own report was retrievable (a 2011 South African study exists in ERIC but its record could not be opened).
- UNICEF's page did not surface an explicit statement on emotion recognition of children; if the council wants that citation it must come from the full PDF, which was not read.
- CASEL's own assessment-guide site (measuringsel.casel.org) failed TLS in this session; the Penn State brief is used instead.

## Key question 6 — India: what NCF-FS 2022 and the Holistic Progress Card expect teachers to record, and current practice

### Takeaway
NCF-FS 2022 makes observation-and-artefact analysis the only legitimate assessment for ages 3–8, names anecdotal records, checklists, event sampling and portfolios as the tools, explicitly says assessment "should not overly burden the Teacher" (leaving periodicity to teacher autonomy), makes the yearly HPC mandatory and the narrative summary optional — while the CBSE guide operationalises this as observing one or two competencies per week; how Indian private preschools actually record today is documented only through vendor claims (Kriyo, Illumine) because no empirical practice study was reachable.

### Cited Findings
- NCF-FS 2022, p. 172: "Assessment should not contribute to any additional burden for the child… Explicit tests and examinations are completely inappropriate assessment tools for this Stage"; "Assessment should enable recording and documentation. Children's progress should be described and analysed through systematic collection of evidence"; "Assessment should not overly burden the Teacher. The Teacher should have the autonomy to judiciously choose the appropriate tool for assessment and the periodicity in which assessment-related record keeping is maintained… systematic record keeping of children's assessment should be seen as an important part of a Teacher's professional responsibilities" — [NCF-FS 2022 (PDF)](https://ncert.nic.in/pdf/NCF_for_Foundational_Stage_20_October_2022.pdf)
- p. 173: "The two broad methods of assessment that are appropriate for the Foundational Stage are observations of the child and analysing artefacts that the child has produced"; p. 174: "Teachers can use tools such as anecdotal records, checklists, and event sampling to record their observation"; p. 182: the school "should maintain a folder for each child" with a narrative summary per term/year; "If recording and maintaining a narrative summary is found too burdensome, the narrative summary can be considered as an optional additional input, but the HPC… needs to be maintained for every academic year. The Teacher will need to maintain enough records to be able to make the HPC for each child" — same source.
- CBSE implementation guide (adapted from PARAKH): "Teachers can decide to observe one or two competencies to be assessed every week/month"; "Teachers can aim to a minimum of one competency for every curriculum goal across domains"; "Focus on and observe one or two competencies per week"; "Keep a record of those children who require further support, with reason"; attainment levels "beginner, progressing and proficient"; "For the purpose of reporting, assessment can be done any time in a term"; Section IV "Learner's Profile by the Teachers" is a narrative "based on interpretation of multiple sources of information (e.g., anecdotal records, event samples, checklists, portfolios, worksheets)" — [CBSE HPC Teacher Guide (PDF)](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/web_material/Manuals/HPC_TeacherGuide.pdf)
- PARAKH "Understanding the HPC (Foundational Stage)" (NCERT 2023, ISBN 978-93-5292-973-3): self-assessment asks the child to rate "their own level of enjoyment of the activity and the level of difficulty" on a three-level scale; peer assessment mirrors it; the parent component is a column on "learning-teaching resources at home" plus remarks, "filled by the teacher in consultation with the parents during the SMCs/PTA meetings"; performance descriptors "Beginner, Proficient, and Advanced (as defined on pages 28–31)" — [PARAKH: How to fill the HPC, Foundational Stage (PDF)](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/themes/parakh/hpc-files/how-to-fill-pdf/How-to-fill-the-HPC-(Foundational-Stage).pdf)
- PARAKH HPC portal: Foundational Stage domains — Physical, Socio-emotional, Cognitive, Language and Literacy, Aesthetic and Cultural Development, Positive Learning Habits; performance tiers described as "Stream", "Mountain" and "Sky" (e.g. Stream "Follows 2–3 step instructions", Sky "Executes complex, multi-step tasks"); methods include classroom observation with activity rubrics, self- and peer-assessment, and parent observation notes; a "Digital HPC Login" exists (hpc.parakh.ncert.gov.in); 1,204 foundational-stage teachers had completed hands-on exercises at the time of reading — [PARAKH HPC](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/hpc); PARAKH blog (24 Aug 2025) frames HPC as "ongoing observation and teacher reflection" with "narrative and descriptive feedback" — [PARAKH blog](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/blog/hpc-foundational-stage)
- State operationalisation (Meghalaya DERT, 2025): observation steps "Planning… Gather evidence… Reflect and assess"; every anecdotal record "should have some basic information such as name of the child, age, class, date, time, location and the name of the observer/teacher" and "It is useful to record only a single event"; artefacts kept in a "folder/ portfolio" — [Meghalaya Assessment Framework for FS (PDF)](https://megeducation.gov.in/dert/notices_and_circulars/2025/Annexure%20-%20Assessment%20Framework%20-FS.pdf)
- Market practice: Kriyo advertises observations, assessments, learning outcomes, milestones, learning journals and progress tracking to "5,700+ schools" — [Kriyo, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/in/app/kriyo-preschool-daycare-app/id1384125107); Illumine (Bangalore) sells AI-drafted "activity updates and daily reports" and observation capture to "600+ centres" — [Illumine](https://illumine.app/)

### Inferences
- The three official documents use *three different* level vocabularies (Beginner/Progressing/Proficient in CBSE; Beginner/Proficient/Advanced in PARAKH's how-to; Stream/Mountain/Sky on PARAKH's portal). Cornerstone should store the competency-level evidence and *render* whichever descriptor set a report requires, rather than hard-coding one (consistent with CLAUDE.md rule 1: structure in tables).
- The NCF-FS design intent — evidence collected continuously, summarised once a year into an HPC, with the narrative optional — is a near-exact match for "every confirmation feeds a learner graph; the teacher gets a weekly five-line summary": the graph is the "enough records to be able to make the HPC", and the weekly summary is the running narrative NCF-FS lets teachers drop when it is burdensome.
- CBSE's "one or two competencies per week" is a workable default for the extraction step's *attention*: prioritise items that map to the competencies the class is currently focused on, and surface out-of-focus evidence as lower-confidence.

### Gaps
- No empirical study of how Indian private preschools or primaries actually record observations was found (ERIC searches returned policy and infrastructure papers, not documentation-practice studies); current practice is inferred from vendor feature lists only.
- NIPUN Bharat guidelines could not be retrieved (PDF URL returned an HTML page; site returned 503), so Grade 1–3 school-based-assessment expectations are not cited here.
- No data was found on HPC completion rates or teacher time per card; the PARAKH portal count (1,204 foundational teachers trained) is the only adoption number seen.

## Council position

Requested by the brief; each bullet names what to borrow or avoid, from whom, with a confidence level and the evidence behind it.

1. **Borrow the industry's observation atom** — one record = media/transcript segment + descriptive note + child tag(s) + optional competency tag(s) + visibility flag (staff-only vs shareable), exactly as Tapestry/brightwheel/Storypark structure it. Confidence: high. Evidence: [Tapestry](https://tapestry.info/features/online-learning-journals/), [brightwheel](https://help.mybrightwheel.com/en/articles/11899456-log-observations-to-assess-student-development), [Storypark](https://help.storypark.com/en/articles/10137042-educators-storypark-assist).
2. **Borrow England's post-2021 stance on evidence**: never require a photo or note to *prove* a judgement, never track statement-by-statement, and measure success by less documentation time, not more captures. Confidence: high. Evidence: EYFS para 2.2 "not required to prove this through collection of any physical evidence" ([framework](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a5f8e4fb00f3323bf1a23a1/EYFS_group_and_school_based_from_September_2026.pdf)); 82–89% of settings shifted time to children after tracking expectations were removed ([DfE evaluation](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6537e5503099f900117f308f/EYFS_2021_Reforms_Evaluation_Report.pdf)).
3. **Keep fine-grained competency nodes available for children who need them** (SEND/EAL/slow-step learners) even while the default view is coarse — the one documented harm of England's simplification. Confidence: medium-high. Evidence: staff "found it difficult to plot the progression of children with SEND… as it was too broad" ([DfE evaluation](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6537e5503099f900117f308f/EYFS_2021_Reforms_Evaluation_Report.pdf)).
4. **Borrow Storypark's disclosure discipline and go one step further**: publish a fact sheet naming the model vendors, hosting region, retention and the exact fields sent; Storypark sends first name/age/gender and never images — Cornerstone's "images not names" rule inverts that and is stricter; keep it. Confidence: high. Evidence: [Storypark Assist fact sheet](https://help.storypark.com/en/articles/10114616-storypark-assist-fact-sheet).
5. **Treat a single voice note as weak evidence and design the graph to need several confirmations, ideally from more than one familiar adult, before any per-child judgement is rendered** (weekly summary should say "3 confirmed observations from 2 educators" style provenance). Confidence: high on the principle, medium on any specific threshold. Evidence: 3–10 minute stories ≈ 1/1000th of a child's day ([Blaiklock 2008](https://oece.nz/wp-content/uploads/Blaiklock_2008.pdf)); teacher-assessor variance "substantial" ([Waterman et al. 2012](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ947506)); familiar observer plus informal cross-teacher triangulation as the practical reliability route ([Wanoa & Johnston 2019](https://ojs.victoria.ac.nz/nzaroe/article/download/6333/5496/8635)).
6. **Confirm specific, observable statements, not global levels.** Informed, specific teacher judgements correlate better with independent measures; the queue item should read "Aarav counted 7 objects with one-to-one correspondence" not "Aarav is 'progressing' in number". Confidence: high. Evidence: r = .63 with informed > uninformed judgements ([Südkamp et al. 2012](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ993888)); ρ = .65 across 122 studies ([Kaufmann 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11271880/)); WSS checklist-item ratings predicted tests better than demographics ([Erikson WSS summary](https://www.erikson.edu/wp-content/uploads/AERA-FCD-WSS-summary.pdf)).
7. **Avoid quotas and avoid the "one story per child per month" trap**: do not surface per-child evidence counts as targets to teachers; the NZ experience is that informal quotas become stress without improving validity. Confidence: medium-high. Evidence: [Niles 2015](https://www.hekupu.ac.nz/article/assessment-practices-within-new-zealand-early-childhood-settings); [Knauf 2020](https://eric.ed.gov/?q=Knauf+%22Documentation+Strategies%22+New+Zealand+Germany).
8. **Avoid ClassDojo's pattern entirely for affect/behaviour**: no points, no numeric behaviour score, no peer-visible or class-displayed comparison, no raw behaviour feed to parents; affect notes stay descriptive, staff-visible by default, and outside any ranking aggregate. Confidence: high. Evidence: "balance sheets of behaviour" and public ranking critique ([EurekAlert/UniSA](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/732702); [Garlen 2019](https://theconversation.com/classdojo-raises-concerns-about-childrens-rights-111033)); SEL data for "continuous improvement, not for high-stakes accountability" ([Penn State brief 2020](https://files.constantcontact.com/df591f85401/41f7dd15-eac5-4753-9b72-4e436e467969.pdf)).
9. **Borrow Leuven's *procedure*, not its scores**: if wellbeing/involvement is captured, do it as periodic two-minute class scans reported at group level (3–4 times a year) to change adult practice, with shorter retention than skill evidence; do not build per-child wellbeing trajectories. Confidence: medium. Evidence: [Laevers/CEE 2015](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=121630&p=0); [practitioner guide](https://irresistible-learning.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/The-Leuven-Well-being-and-Involvement-scales.pdf); rights caution in [Lupton & Williamson 2017](https://core.ac.uk/download/77612283.pdf).
10. **Borrow Sarvam-style keyterm prompting with the roster, transcribe in a code-mix output mode, and measure the pipeline on name/entity preservation rather than WER**; keep the audio snippet attached to each queue item so the teacher can replay it while confirming. Confidence: medium-high (vendor benchmarks, one independent audit). Evidence: keyterm prompting and 16.03% WER under L5 keyword prompting ([Saaras V4 report](https://techstory.in/sarvam-ai-launches-saaras-v4-with-multilingual-speech-recognition-capabilities/)); "Preserves proper nouns and entities" ([Sarvam docs](https://docs.sarvam.ai/api-reference-docs/getting-started/models/saarika)); systems "failing on code mixed or vernacular speech" ([arXiv 2512.10967](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10967)); WER mis-scores valid Indic variants ([Sarvam eval blog](https://www.sarvam.ai/blogs/evaluating-indian-language-asr)).
11. **Borrow TeachScribe's capture ergonomics and instrument what it did not publish**: push-to-talk during the school day, auto-tagging as a *suggestion*, and a dashboard of coverage gaps — but log extraction precision/recall against teacher confirmations from the first week, because no product or paper has published real-classroom accuracy for voice-to-observation.
