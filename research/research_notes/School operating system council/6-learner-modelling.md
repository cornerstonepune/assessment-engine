# Learner modelling and self-improving learning loops (council seat 6)

Brief for the Cornerstone design council. Date of research: 2026-09-26. Every URL below was opened during this session (directly, or as the JSON record of the same paper from the ERIC / CrossRef / Semantic Scholar APIs, or as a PDF whose text was extracted locally). Numbers that were seen only in search snippets and could not be confirmed on an opened page are marked "unverified". Web search became unavailable partway through, so some well-known papers are cited from their abstract records rather than full text; the Gaps sections say where.

Cornerstone's design, for reference: append-only evidence rows (child, skill, level, correct/blank/wrong/wrong-with-working, misconception tag, channel, observer, confidence, candidate/confirmed/superseded); a nightly per-child skill graph that is a pure function of confirmed evidence; threshold rules today with BKT to be tested offline; FSRS-style spaced revisits; teacher overrides to become golden sets so that decisions graduate from advisory to autonomous only when they beat their golden set.

## KQ1. Mastery models: BKT, DKT, IRT, Elo, knowledge space theory, PFA — data needs, outputs, interpretability, head-to-head evidence

### Takeaway
Across the best-controlled comparisons, well-featured logistic models (PFA/"Best-LR", IRT+) and extended BKT match or beat deep knowledge tracing until a dataset exceeds roughly a million interactions with many learners per item; BKT's classic form lags in predictive accuracy but is the only family whose parameters (prior, learn, guess, slip) are directly readable and whose small-sample behaviour has been quantified (usable mastery predictions from about 25 students x 3 opportunities per skill; stable skill parameters need 250–500 students). Elo-style ratings are the cheapest thing that learns item difficulty from data (about 100–200 students per item pool) and are what the largest primary-maths practice system (Math Garden / Prowise Learn) runs on; Rasch is the only IRT variant whose item calibration is stable at 30–100 responses per item.

### Cited Findings
- BKT origin and form: Corbett & Anderson, "Knowledge tracing: Modeling the acquisition of procedural knowledge", User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction 4(4):253–278, 1995 (1,654 CrossRef citations) — [CrossRef record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/BF01099821). BKT is "a simple Hidden Markov Model ... that tries to estimate a single binary latent variable" per skill using four parameters p(L0), p(G), p(S), p(T) estimated "separately for each distinct skill" — [Slater & Baker 2018, Behaviormetrika preprint](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/behaviormetrika_vfinal.pdf).
- pyBKT (Badrinath, Wang & Pardos, EDM 2021) fits BKT by EM and exposes variants "forgets, multigs (multiple guess/slip classes), multilearn, multiprior, multipair"; fitted output is prior, learns, forgets, guesses, slips; built-in metrics RMSE, AUC, accuracy; C++ backend fits 500 synthetic students in ~1.5 s vs ~1m55s in pure Python — [pyBKT GitHub](https://github.com/CAHLR/pyBKT).
- BKT identifiability: the HMM form of BKT "is, in fact, a function of three parameters and has the functional form of an exponential", which "explains the 'Identifiability Problem' first noted by Beck and Chang [2007]"; the paper derives the constraint that identifies which fitted models are "empirically degenerate" — [van de Sande 2013, JEDM](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1115329.pdf).
- BKT sample-size error (simulation): "about four times as much error in parameter estimations for ns = 25 compared to ns = 500, and about twice as much error ... for np = 3 compared to np = 10"; extreme parameters (e.g., p(T)=1, p(G) or p(S) >= 0.5) occurred "up to 42% of the time" with small samples; "even at a student sample size of 500 and a problem set length of 10, there was still a considerable number of trials that resulted in extreme parameters" for p(G)/p(S) — [Slater & Baker 2018](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/behaviormetrika_vfinal.pdf).
- BKT sample-size recommendations: "If the goal of the BKT model is to predict student mastery, at least 25 students and at least 3 opportunities to practice a given skill seems to be sufficient. At these sample sizes, even large error rates in parameter estimates will not change mastery estimates by more than two or three problems"; error under two problems needs "250 students" (low p(T)) or "500 students" (high p(T)); "to make inferences about properties of the underlying skills, significantly larger sample sizes appear to be necessary"; "to avoid extreme parameter estimates, sample sizes of at least 250 students and 6 problems per skill per student appear warranted". p(L0) is the most error-prone parameter but matters least for mastery; p(T) has "the narrowest window of optimal parameters ... from about p(T) = 0.10 to p(T) = 0.12" — [Slater & Baker 2018](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/behaviormetrika_vfinal.pdf).
- Individualising BKT: "student-specific parameters lead to a tangible improvement when predicting the data of unseen students, and ... parameterizing students' speed of learning is more beneficial than parameterizing a priori knowledge" — [Yudelson, Koedinger & Gordon, AIED 2013 (Semantic Scholar record)](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/978-3-642-39112-5_18?fields=title,abstract); chapter metadata: LNCS, pp. 171–180, 332 citing works — [CrossRef](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/978-3-642-39112-5_18). In a hierarchical-Bayesian follow-up, the fitted weights for per-skill parameters "are two to three times smaller than that of per-student parameters" — [Yudelson, EDM 2016](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED592687.pdf).
- Warning on individualisation: "Individualization increases the number of model parameters and carries the risk of overfitting. The individualized parameters are fitted using only few data points and thus can be significantly influenced by the noise in data"; an alternative is individualisation "on the level of learner groups, e.g., having the same parameters for all learners in one class" — [Pelánek 2017, UMUAI overview](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-overview.pdf).
- DKT: recurrent networks "do not require the explicit encoding of human domain knowledge, and can capture more complex representations of student knowledge", with "substantial improvements in prediction performance"; the learned model "allows straightforward interpretation and discovery of structure in student tasks" — [Piech et al. 2015, arXiv 1506.05908](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.05908). The originally reported gain was "25% gain in AUC ... over BKT on two real-world datasets" — quoted in [Gervet et al. 2020](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1273917.pdf) and [Khajah et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.02416).
- DKT vs extended BKT: on Assistments "classic BKT obtains an AUC of 0.73, better than the 0.67 reported for BKT by Piech et al."; "31.6% of difference in performance reported in [22] appears to be due to the use of a biased procedure for computing the AUC for BKT. Another 50.6% of the difference ... vanishes if BKT is augmented to allow for forgetting"; with exercise-indexed labels "BKT+S and BKT+FSA both obtain an AUC of 0.90, beating DKT" (0.86). Conclusion: "knowledge tracing may be a domain that does not require 'depth'; shallow models like BKT can perform just as well and offer us greater interpretability and explanatory power" — [Khajah, Lindsey & Mozer 2016, EDM (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.02416).
- The nine-dataset comparison (Gervet, Koedinger, Schneider & Mitchell, JEDM 12(3), 2020): "logistic regression - with the right set of features - leads on datasets of moderate size or containing a very large number of interactions per student, whereas Deep Knowledge Tracing leads on datasets of large size or where precise temporal information matters most"; "Markov process methods, like Bayesian Knowledge Tracing, lag behind other approaches" — [JEDM page](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/view/451). Dataset sizes ranged from 182 to 29,018 learners and 189,297 to 6,003,641 interactions (Table 2). Example AUCs: algebra05 Best-LR 0.831, DKT 0.821, PFA 0.769, IRT 0.768, BKT 0.621; assistments09 Best-LR 0.772, BKT+ 0.759, DKT 0.757, IRT 0.692, BKT 0.631; statics DKT 0.829, Best-LR 0.819, BKT+ 0.811, IRT 0.789, BKT 0.732; spanish Best-LR 0.863, BKT+ 0.851, PFA 0.847, DKT 0.832; assistments15 DKT 0.731, Best-LR 0.702 (the classic-BKT figures in these tables are the best previously reported values the authors compiled, not their own re-runs; their re-run "BKT+" variant is the one with confidence intervals). "Best-LR dominates in the low and medium data regimes (fewer than one million interactions), and DKT takes over in the high data regime"; "the number of learners per item and per KC of a dataset better capture its size than the number of interactions it contains"; on datasets with thousands of interactions per learner "DKT plateaus after 1000 student interactions, whereas Best-LR keeps improving" — [Gervet et al. 2020, full text (ERIC)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1273917.pdf).
- Calibration of KT models: "the current best models are severely biased on some datasets – hindering their applicability in adaptive policies and open learner models"; on assistments09 "both models severely overestimate learners when the probability of correct answer is low, and underestimate learners when the probability of correct answer is high. In general, models are calibrated only in the interval containing most of the data"; calibration plots binned by observed frequency "reveal systematic biases, but no standard technique can correct for them" — [Gervet et al. 2020](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1273917.pdf).
- Metric choice: "if the model output is used by an instructional policy that considers absolute values of predictions, it is not meaningful to perform model evaluation using the AUC metric, since it considers only the relative ordering of predictions" — [Pelánek 2017](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-overview.pdf).
- Which family for which knowledge: for practice of facts/vocabulary "the modeling approach should clearly focus on learning and forgetting processes; probably using one of the models from the family of logistic models"; for a tutor "concerned with understanding and sense-making processes" where the purpose is "actionable insight that will be used for a manual redesign", "some version of the BKT model should be relevant and domain modeling is clearly very important"; "it is easier to use 'multiple KCs per item' with logistic models than with BKT"; neural approaches "achieve good predictive accuracy, but at the price of poor interpretability" — [Pelánek 2017](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-overview.pdf).
- PFA vs KT: on four Cognitive Tutor datasets "the PFA model has better LL, BIC, r and A'" than knowledge tracing; KT "had a tendency to predict much worse performance after a failure than did PFA, with the observed average in the data ... much closer to PFA", attributed to KT's assumption "that a single failure (assuming a slip has not occurred) indicates the KC is unlearned"; PFA also handles steps needing multiple KCs, which KT cannot — [Pavlik, Cen & Koedinger, AIED 2009](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED506305.pdf).
- Does interpretability help teachers? In a two-part study (simulation plus N=12 teachers), "decisions based on interpretable KT models achieve mastery faster" in simulation, but with real teachers the number of tasks to mastery "hardly differs between KT models" even though teachers rated interpretable models higher on usability and trust; "teachers do not rely solely on model information" — [Workshop on Epistemics and Decision-Making in AI-Supported Education, AIED 2025 (arXiv 2511.02718)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02718).
- Rasch/IRT sample sizes: for item calibration stability, 30 persons give +/-1 logit at 95% confidence (range 16–36), 50 give +/-1 logit at 99%, 100 give +/-0.5 logit at 95% (range 64–144), 150 give +/-0.5 logit at 99%; "stability to within +/-.3 logits is the best that can be expected for most variables"; at least 8 correct and 8 incorrect responses per item; polytomous items need 10 observations per category. The note covers Rasch only, not 2PL/3PL — [Linacre 1994, Rasch Measurement Transactions 7:4](https://www.rasch.org/rmt/rmt74m.htm).
- IRT sample size across models: with a real 50-item test given to 6,288 students, resampled to test lengths 10/20/30 and samples of 150–5,000, "rather than sample size or test length, the combination of these two variables is important and samples of 150, 250, 350, 500, and 750 examinees can be used to estimate item parameters accurately in three unidimensional dichotomous IRT models, depending on test length and model employed" — [Sahin & Anil 2017, Educational Sciences: Theory and Practice (ERIC EJ1130806)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1130806).
- Elo in education: interpret "student's answer to an item as a match between the student and the item"; the system "is simple, robust, and effective"; it is suited "mainly for adaptive practice or low stakes testing ... but does not provide statistical guarantees on estimated skills (as opposed to well calibrated IRT models used in computerized adaptive testing)"; "the system needs at least 100 students to get good estimates of item difficulty"; Wauters et al. (2012) found "with a sample size of 200 students the data-driven methods provide reliable and highly correlated estimates"; under adaptive item selection "the proportion correct method gives poor estimates" that "do not improve with the increasing number of students", while Elo "gives nearly the same estimates as joint maximum likelihood"; recommended starting point: uncertainty function U(n)=a/(1+bn) with a=1, b=0.05; new items are "(re)set" to difficulty 0 and learned from answers — [Pelánek 2016, Computers & Education (accepted manuscript)](http://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf).
- Math Garden (now Prowise Learn) is Elo-based with a scoring rule on accuracy and response time; items sampled at mean success probability 0.75; 3,648 children completed over 3.5 million problems in ten months; reports "better measurement precision, high validity and reliability" — [Klinkenberg, Straatemeier & van der Maas 2011, Computers & Education (ERIC abstract)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ925823). A decade on: "over a billion responses"; "actively used by over 400,000 primary school children in the Netherlands"; across all games the RMSE of prediction is about 0.28 and about 79% of responses conform to the scoring model (the rest are typing errors, fast guesses); parallel items such as 2x9 and 9x2 keep near-identical ratings over 3.5 years, used as a reliability check; documented misfit: fast-vs-slow responders, guessing, skipping via the question-mark button, and an adaptive-selection feedback loop on the balance-scale task where "the dynamic estimation of the item and user ratings, in combination with local strategies, result in dynamics that reinforce the reward of developing erroneous local strategies", fixed by switching that task to a fixed item order — [Brinkhuis et al. 2018, Journal of Learning Analytics](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1187391.pdf).
- Knowledge space theory / ALEKS: knowledge is modelled as feasible "knowledge states"; for Algebra 1 about 350 concepts generate "millions of empirically feasible knowledge states"; an adaptive assessment "employing Markovian procedures, can gauge a student's knowledge state in a relatively small number of questions (approximately 25-30)"; references Doignon & Falmagne, Learning Spaces (2011) and Falmagne et al., Psychological Review (1990). The page does not say how structures are built or validated — [ALEKS: Knowledge Space Theory](https://www.aleks.com/about_aleks/knowledge_space_theory).

### Inferences
- For a school with tens of thousands (not millions) of interactions per year and 40–80 children per grade, the Gervet threshold ("fewer than one million interactions") puts Cornerstone permanently in the regime where logistic/BKT-family models win; DKT-class models have no predictive case and a clear interpretability and calibration cost.
- Slater & Baker's numbers convert directly into policy: per-skill BKT fitted on one grade (40–80 children) can support "which child should stop practising" decisions to within two or three problems, but the fitted p(G)/p(S)/p(T) should not be read as facts about the skill until 250–500 children have practised it — i.e., several cohorts. Until then, guess/slip should be fixed to published or seed values and only mastery posteriors updated.
- Elo with an uncertainty function is the natural "learned" layer for item difficulty in Cornerstone's item bank: it needs ~100–200 responses per item pool, tolerates new items (start at 0), and is auditable (one number per item). Rasch on the same data gives a comparable difficulty scale once ~30 responses per item exist and offers misfit statistics Elo lacks.
- Cornerstone's three-signal rule (blank / wrong / wrong-with-working) is not native to any of these models, which are binary; partial-credit BKT (hints as partial correctness, cited in Slater & Baker's review) and polytomous Rasch (10 observations per category, per Linacre) are the documented routes, both of which raise data needs.
- Teachers do not act on model internals much (N=12 study), so the "interpretability" that matters is at the level of the decision shown (which skill, which evidence), not the parameterisation.

### Gaps
- Corbett & Anderson 1995 full text was only available as a scanned PDF with no extractable text; details are cited via CrossRef metadata and secondary descriptions.
- The Gervet paper's per-dataset tables were read from extracted text; the bridge06 and squirrel tables were partially garbled and are not quoted numerically beyond what appears above.
- No opened source gives 2PL/3PL-specific minimum samples beyond Sahin & Anil's range statement; the common rule of thumb (hundreds for 2PL, ~1,000 for 3PL) is unverified here.
- Snappet's own adaptive algorithm (Elo-like or otherwise) was not documented in any opened source.

## KQ2. Spaced practice: FSRS and its evidence base; spaced/interleaved retrieval in primary mathematics

### Takeaway
FSRS is a well-benchmarked memory model for adult flashcard review (10,000 Anki users, ~519M reviews; FSRS-7 log loss 0.318, AUC 0.75), but its evidence is entirely from vocabulary/flashcard logs; the school-classroom evidence for spacing and interleaving of maths practice is separate and strong at grade 7 (d = 0.83 in a preregistered cluster RCT), weaker and shorter-lived at grade 3, and depends on scheduling and mixing of items, not on any particular optimiser.

### Cited Findings
- FSRS memory model: three components — Difficulty (D in [1,10]), Stability (S, "the interval when R=90%") and Retrievability (R, probability of recall); "the memory state is represented by Stability (S) and Difficulty (D)"; FSRS-6 has 21 parameters and a trainable forgetting-curve decay — [awesome-fsrs wiki: The Algorithm](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm).
- FSRS benchmark: 10,000 Anki collections, ~519 million reviews (Hugging Face "anki-revlogs-10k"), evaluated with TimeSeriesSplit; FSRS-7 (recency) log loss 0.3178+/-0.0040, RMSE(bins) 0.05715, AUC 0.7522; the best neural model (RWKV-Instant) 0.2660 log loss, AUC 0.8450; older baselines HLR log loss 0.705 (AUC 0.61) and Ebisu v2 0.772 (AUC 0.59); SM-2 and Anki's default scheduler are not in this table — [open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark).
- FSRS ancestry: the SSP-MMC scheduler was built from "220 million students' memory behavior logs with time-series features", uses a Markov memory model and a stochastic-shortest-path optimiser, and showed "a 12.6% performance improvement over the state-of-the-art methods" in the MaiMemo language app — [Ye, Su & Cao, KDD 2022 (Semantic Scholar record)](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3534678.3539081?fields=title,abstract); code and data at [maimemo/SSP-MMC](https://github.com/maimemo/SSP-MMC).
- FSRS in practice (Anki manual): desired retention default 90%; "above 90% the workload increases very quickly, and above 97% the workload can be overwhelming"; the optimiser "requires several reviews to fine-tune the parameters" (no minimum stated); re-optimising "once every month is sufficient"; "Do not change the parameters manually or copy them from someone else" — [Anki manual, deck options](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html).
- Spacing meta-analysis: 839 assessments in 317 experiments from 184 articles; "the ISI producing maximal retention increased as retention interval increased" — [Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer 2006, Psychological Bulletin (ERIC EJ742345)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ742345).
- Spacing in mathematics (college): distributing 10 practice problems across two sessions "virtually doubled" four-week test performance versus massing; solving 3 vs 9 problems in one session made no difference at one or four weeks (overlearning had no benefit) — [Rohrer & Taylor 2006 (ERIC ED505642)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED505642).
- Interleaving RCT: preregistered cluster RCT, 54 grade-7 classes, 4 months, unannounced test one month later: "the interleaved group outscored the blocked group, 61% versus 38%, d = 0.83"; "Teachers were able to implement the intervention without training" — [Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung 2020, J. Educational Psychology (ERIC EJ1237752)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1237752); the IES award page gives 787 students, 14 teachers, 5 schools in suburban Florida — [IES R305A160263](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/efficacy-study-interleaved-mathematics-practice).
- Textbooks are blocked: of 13,505 problems in six representative maths texts, only 9.7% were interleaved — [Rohrer, Dedrick & Hartwig 2020, Educational Psychology Review (ERIC EJ1263191)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1263191).
- Primary-school spacing (grade 3): N=213 third and seventh graders; three practice sets massed in one day vs one per day for three days; "a positive effect of distributed practice ... in Grade 7" at 1 and 6 weeks; "In Grade 3, a positive effect of distributed practice was supported by the data only in the test 1 week after the last practice set" — [Barzagar Nazari & Ebersbach 2019, Applied Cognitive Psychology (ERIC EJ1262987)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1262987).
- Math-fact fluency (grade 3): 43 third graders, four 1-minute probes per day for 20 days; students whose practice was separated by a 3-hour interval "grew significantly more than students who practiced with a 10-min distribution or a massed presentation" — [Powell et al. 2022, School Psychology Review (ERIC EJ1369993)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1369993).
- Spacing for complex procedures (primary): 61 primary students, massed vs distributed problem-solving practice; a spacing effect was found "but only for students with medium initial practice performance" — [Ruitenburg et al. 2025, Applied Cognitive Psychology (ERIC EJ1460829)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1460829).
- Spaced retrieval in a maths course (college precalculus): spaced retrieval improved retention within the course and into the following calculus course — [Hopkins, Lyle, Hieb & Ralston 2016 (ERIC EJ1120461)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1120461); both more practice and more spacing improved within-semester retention — [Lyle et al. 2020 (ERIC EJ1245263)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1245263).

### Inferences
- The classroom-verified mechanism for primary maths is "distribute the same problems over days and mix problem types", which an FSRS-like scheduler implements per skill; but FSRS's parameters were fitted to adult flashcards, and Anki itself warns against transplanting parameters. At school scale, per-child FSRS parameter optimisation is not defensible (hundreds of reviews per child per skill do not exist); a fixed default schedule with a retention target of about 90% is.
- The grade-3 evidence (benefit visible at 1 week, not at 6) suggests revisits for the youngest children should be more frequent than an adult forgetting curve would schedule, and that any "learned" spacing should first be validated on a retention check rather than assumed.
- FSRS's stability/difficulty state per (child, skill) is compatible with Cornerstone's ring B (derived) design: it is a pure function of the evidence rows and can be rebuilt nightly.

### Gaps
- No opened study applies FSRS (or any optimised spaced-repetition scheduler) to primary-school mathematics or to children under 10; the evidence base found is adult flashcard/vocabulary data.
- The FSRS "Optimizing Spaced Repetition Schedule by Capturing the Dynamics of Memory" journal paper and the FSRS-vs-SM-2 comparison repository were not reachable; the SM-2 comparison is therefore not quantified here.
- Effect sizes for Powell 2022 and Ruitenburg 2025 were not in the abstracts opened.

## KQ3. Learning prerequisites and misconceptions from data; how detectors have been validated

### Takeaway
Prerequisite edges can be recovered from performance data only partially even in simulation (at N=150 about 74–90% of adjacencies, with poor edge orientation), and on real logs the discovered structure only partly agreed with experts; misconception prediction from answer data tops out around 68% (which wrong answer) in the largest open competition, LLM-based misconception detection reaches 84% recall but with roughly eight false alarms per true detection at realistic prevalence, and the Baker-tradition detectors show that behaviour/affect models generalise across students and lessons within a system but decay across populations and years.

### Cited Findings
- Causal discovery of prerequisites: treating knowledge components as latent variables with a known Q-matrix, the algorithm was validated on simulated data; "Even in the most difficult (and most realistic) case, where the sample size is 150, the measurement model is impure, and the data is binary, we still recover 74% of adjacencies for Model 1, 76% for Model 3, and 89.5% for Model 2"; adjacency precision "is near perfect in all conditions"; orientation "does not perform quite as well", with a worst true-positive orientation rate of 64.5%; the real application discussed is a single assessment given to about 120 students — [Scheines, Silver & Goldin, EDM 2014](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/nynke/726-2021/week06/HCI%20prereq%20discovery/ScheinesSilverGoldin-EDM2014.pdf). The authors note "expert opinions are not a reliable way to determine effective topic order" as motivation (same source).
- Association-rule discovery of prerequisites: probabilistic association rules over knowledge states inferred by DINA (tests) or BKT (logs); validated on 1,200 simulated students, a 2,933-examinee/28-item/3-skill test, and Cognitive Tutor "Bridge to Algebra 2006-2007" logs (1,146 students, 560 usable); on the log data the rules of one form "completely agree with the structure from human expertise" while the other form "is inconsistent with it"; "the log data is very likely to contain much noise" (skills learned without prerequisites, scaffolding teaching prerequisites), and "confidences of the association rules which are supported by a small proportion of sample will be affected much more" — [Chen, Wuillemin & Labat, EDM 2015](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED560515.pdf).
- Eedi diagnostic-question data (NeurIPS 2020 Education Challenge): MCQs "whose distractors embody misconceptions"; training data for Tasks 1–2: 27,613 questions, 118,971 students, 15,867,850 answers (after removing questions with <50 answers and students with <50 answers); Tasks 3–4: 948 questions, 4,918 students, 1,382,727 answers; Task 1 (right/wrong) winner 77.29% accuracy using feature engineering, target encoding and ensembles; Task 2 (which answer) winner 68.03%; Task 3 (question quality vs 5 expert teachers) winners reached "a maximum of 80% agreement" with one evaluator but 0.60–0.72 with the others, and "all of these solutions are entirely deterministic and do not involve any machine learning component"; Task 4 (choose 10 questions per student adaptively) winner 74.74% — [Wang et al. 2021, PMLR v133](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v133/wang21a/wang21a.pdf); overview at [arXiv 2104.04034](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04034).
- Confidence and hypercorrection on Eedi: 219,826 responses from 7,302 UK students aged 6–16; confidence correlates positively with facility; the platform re-asks "an analogous question about 3 weeks later" after an error, giving "the first evidence in an authentic school mathematics context for the 'hypercorrection effect'" (high-confidence errors are more likely to be corrected) — [Foster, Woodhead, Barton & Clark-Wilson 2022, Educational Studies in Mathematics (ERIC EJ1326555)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1326555).
- LLM misconception detection: on "20,964 real student responses from the Eedi mathematics platform", fine-tuned classifiers detected hidden misconceptions (correct answer, flawed reasoning) at 57% and open-weight reasoning models at 84%, but "At realistic prevalence, false alarms outnumbered genuine detections approximately 8 to 1"; the authors propose a "detect-verify-escalate pipeline" that routes uncertain cases "to diagnostic follow-up questions rather than directly to teachers", a teacher dashboard that filters review queues, and a rubric separating "answer correctness from method validity" — [arXiv 2606.23205, June 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23205).
- Eedi's public research page (vendor) reports "over a decade collecting the novel and granular data needed to accurately diagnose and help resolve student misconceptions", seven public datasets, a gold EDUEvidence certification and an RCT with NWEA; these are vendor claims — [eedi.com/research](https://eedi.com/research).
- Gaming-the-system detector (Cognitive Tutor): A' of 0.80 on training students and 0.73 under leave-one-out cross-validation for distinguishing students who gamed and learned poorly from non-gamers; trained on three lessons and tested on a fourth, average A' 0.85 (training lessons) vs 0.80 (held-out lesson), "not statistically significant" degradation, but "transfer does not appear completely even" — [Baker, Corbett, Roll & Koedinger 2008, UMUAI](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/USER475.pdf).
- Detector rot: detectors trained on 2005 MATHia/Cognitive Tutor data (18,737 labelled clips) tested on 2021 data (594 clips, 31 gaming): decision tree AUC 0.736 old-to-old vs 0.716 old-to-new; random forest 0.784 vs 0.509; neural network 0.649 vs 0.398; XGBoost 0.763 vs 0.333; "a classic decision tree algorithm maintained its performance while more contemporary algorithms struggled to transfer to new data" — [Levin, Baker, Nasiar, Fancsali & Hutt, EDM 2022](https://educationaldatamining.org/edm2022/proceedings/2022.EDM-short-papers.38/index.html).
- Population validity: affect detectors built on urban, suburban and rural ASSISTments students "trained on a population drawn primarily from one demographic grouping do not generalize to populations drawn primarily from the other demographic groupings, even though those populations might be considered part of the same national or regional culture"; models trained on all three "still do not achieve ideal population validity", and "generalize better across urban and suburban students than rural students" — [Ocumpaugh, Baker, Gowda, Heffernan & Heffernan 2014, BJET (ERIC EJ1022850)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1022850).
- Detector quality and predictive validity: sensor-free affect detectors on ASSISTments logs reached A' 0.63–0.82 and kappa 0.23–0.51 (boredom A' 0.632, engaged concentration 0.678, frustration 0.682, confusion 0.736, off-task 0.819, gaming 0.802); applied to a year of logs for 1,393 grade-8 students, engaged concentration during problem solving correlated r = 0.45 (2004–05) and 0.26 (2005–06) with the end-of-year state test, boredom on original problems r = -0.12 / -0.28 but on scaffolding items +0.32 / +0.27 — [Pardos, Baker, San Pedro, Gowda & Gowda 2014, J. Learning Analytics](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/3536-15645-1-ED-v3.pdf).
- Fairness of BKT: using data from a US-scale learning platform, "the performance of these models [BKT knowledge estimates and carelessness detectors] is close to equal across all the demographic and intersectional groups", including transfer to groups unseen in training — [Zambrano, Zhang & Baker, LAK 2024](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/lak24-47-4.pdf). A review of algorithmic bias in education proposes moving "from unknown bias to known bias" with audits across race/ethnicity, gender, nationality, SES, disability — [Baker & Hawn 2022, IJAIED (ERIC EJ1353563)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1353563).

### Inferences
- Learning prerequisite edges from Cornerstone's own data is not viable as a source of truth: with 150 children per grade band even the simulation recall is 74–90% with weak direction, and real logs add the "learned without the prerequisite" noise Chen et al. describe. Data can only flag edges in the school's expert ladder that look wrong (children who master B while failing A), for a human to re-examine.
- Misconception tagging is where a model plus code plus teacher confirmation is essential: even the best data-only "which wrong answer" prediction is ~68%, and LLM detection at 84% recall produces 8 false alarms per hit at realistic prevalence. The published mitigation (route uncertain cases to a diagnostic follow-up item, not to the teacher) fits Cornerstone's "verify before escalate" logic and protects teacher time.
- The detector literature says a model trained on one population, one system, one year should be assumed not to transfer; anything Cornerstone borrows (misconception priors, affect/engagement heuristics) needs local re-validation, and simpler models (trees, logistic) are the safer choice for small labelled sets.

### Gaps
- The Eedi 2024 Kaggle competition ("Mining Misconceptions in Mathematics", MAP@25) page could not be opened (JavaScript-only), and no arXiv paper on its results was found; leaderboard levels are therefore unverified.
- No opened source quantifies how often teacher-assigned misconception tags disagree with each other (inter-rater reliability of misconception tagging in primary maths).
- The Chen et al. paper's figures with per-rule probabilities could not be read from extracted text; only the qualitative agreement is reported.

## KQ4. Efficacy: what ITS/CAL meta-analyses and trials show, under what conditions, and how much was the software versus the humans around it

### Takeaway
Effects range from near zero (K-12 maths ITS vs regular teaching, g = 0.01–0.09; RAND's Cognitive Tutor year-1 null) to large (median 0.66 SD in Kulik & Fletcher; 0.37 SD in Mindspark), and the gap is explained by test alignment, implementation, duration and the human wrapper: the trials with credible standardized-test effects (ASSISTments 0.18 SD; Mindspark 0.37 SD) paired software with teacher reports and training or with 45 minutes of instructor-led small-group time, and none isolates the software's share.

### Cited Findings
- Kulik & Fletcher 2016 (Review of Educational Research 86(1):42–78): 50 controlled evaluations; median effect "0.66 standard deviations over conventional levels, or from the 50th to the 75th percentile"; the improvement "depended to a great extent on whether improvement was measured on locally developed or standardized tests"; six evaluations with nonconventional controls and four with flawed implementations showed small effects — [ERIC EJ1090502](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1090502); [CrossRef](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3102/0034654315581420). The widely quoted split (about 0.7 SD on local tests vs about 0.1 SD on standardized tests) was not visible on any page opened — unverified.
- VanLehn 2011 (Educational Psychologist 46(4)): human tutoring d = 0.79 and intelligent (step-based) tutoring d = 0.76 vs no tutoring; prior beliefs of d = 0.3 / 1.0 / 2.0 for answer-based / intelligent / human tutoring "did not" hold — [ERIC EJ946764](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764).
- Ma, Adesope, Nesbit & Liu 2014 (J. Educational Psychology): 107 effect sizes, 14,321 participants; ITS vs teacher-led large-group instruction g = 0.42, vs non-ITS computer-based instruction g = 0.57, vs textbooks/workbooks g = 0.35; no significant difference vs individual human tutoring (g = -0.11) or small-group instruction (g = 0.05); positive "whether the ITS was used as the principal means of instruction, a supplement ..., an integral component ..., or an aid to homework" and "whether or not the ITS provided feedback or modeled student misconceptions" — [APA PDF](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/features/edu-a0037123.pdf).
- Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper 2013 (J. Educational Psychology 105(4)): 26 reports, 34 samples, 1997–2010; "ITS had no negative and perhaps a small positive effect on K-12 students' mathematical learning, as indicated by the average effect sizes ranging from g = 0.01 to g = 0.09"; effectiveness "for helping students drawn from the general population was greater than for helping low achievers"; the ERIC abstract says effects "appeared to be greater when the interventions lasted for less than a school year" — [ERIC EJ1054449](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1054449). (A search snippet stated the opposite about duration and a low-achiever g = -0.18; both unverified.)
- RAND Cognitive Tutor Algebra I (Pane, Griffin, McCaffrey & Karam, EEPA 36(2), 2014): schools in seven states matched into pairs and randomised for two years; "no effects in the first year of implementation, but ... positive effects in the second year", significant for high schools, not middle schools, magnitude "approximately eight percentile points"; the product used "a personalized, mastery-learning, blended-learning approach" — [ERIC EJ1024233](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1024233). The What Works Clearinghouse (reviewed May 2026) rates both experiments "Meets WWC standards without reservations" but, using its own effect computations (high school: 5,328 students year 1, 5,738 year 2; year-2 intervention 0.08 vs comparison -0.08), finds "no statistically significant positive findings" for high school and an indeterminate effect for middle school — [WWC study 82101](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/study/82101). The year-2 improvement "was not associated with increased fidelity of implementation, but instead with teachers reducing (although not completely eliminating) their use of the activities called for by Cognitive Tutor guidelines for non-computer class time" — [Escueta et al., NBER WP 23744](https://oreopoulos.faculty.economics.utoronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/escueta-et-al-education-technology-nber-wp-23744-2017.pdf).
- Escueta, Quan, Nickow & Oreopoulos (NBER WP 23744, 2017; published as "Upgrading Education with Technology", J. Economic Literature 58(4), 2020): 29 CAL RCTs in developed countries; "only eight report no effect and one turned up negative effects"; "the majority of the studies finding positive effects (15 of 20) were focused on improving math outcomes"; ASSISTments in Maine: 0.18 SD "despite involving less than 30-40 minutes per week", 0.29 SD for students at or below the median; SimCalc: 0.63 and 0.56 SD (grades 7 and 8); Cognitive Tutor: "no effect the first year, but a 0.20 standard deviation impact in the second"; Mindspark (working-paper numbers): 0.36 SD math, 0.22 SD Hindi; reading CAL evidence "scarce"; one null study "specifically mentions that the study was underpowered" — [NBER WP PDF](https://oreopoulos.faculty.economics.utoronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/escueta-et-al-education-technology-nber-wp-23744-2017.pdf). (The final JEL text was not opened; numbers may differ slightly.)
- Mindspark (Muralidharan, Singh & Ganimian, AER 109(4), 2019): lottery among 619 students, mostly grades 6–9, from five public middle schools in low-income Delhi; "lottery winners scored 0.37 sigma higher in math and 0.23 sigma higher in Hindi over just a 4.5-month period"; IV: 90 days of attendance would raise scores 0.6 sigma (math) and 0.39 sigma (Hindi); similar absolute gains but "much greater relative gains for academically-weaker students" — [AEA article page](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20171112). Programme design: 90-minute sessions six days a week, "45 minutes of computer-assisted learning software plus 45 minutes of instructor-led small group instruction (typically 15 students per session)"; fee INR 200/month, cost about INR 1,000 per student per month, projected under USD 2 at scale; grade-6 students averaged 2.5 grade levels behind and grade-9 students 4.5; "The evaluation does not isolate which component (software versus instructor support) drove observed effects" — [J-PAL evaluation summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/disrupting-education-evidence-technology-aided-instruction-india).
- ASSISTments efficacy RCT: 2,850 grade-7 students in 43 Maine schools; the intervention combined "an online homework tool with teacher training"; the tool "(a) provides timely feedback and hints to students as they do homework and (b) gives teachers timely, organized information about students' work"; it "significantly increased student scores on an end-of-the-year standardized mathematics assessment" — [Roschelle, Feng, Murphy & Mason 2016, AERA Open (ERIC EJ1194398)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1194398); effect size 0.18 SD per Escueta et al. above.
- Snappet (Dutch primary adaptive practice) RCT: 79 schools, 1,808 grade-3 students; the tool gave "student feedback, feedback to teachers, and adaptive assignments"; positive effects on achievement and motivation; "achievement effects were higher for high-performing students" — [Faber, Luyten & Visscher 2017, Computers & Education (Twente repository)](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/519ba682-57f7-4028-8224-d82a5347d906). Effect sizes were not in the abstract (unverified).
- What 50 platform RCTs teach (ASSISTments E-TRIALS): 50,752 instances of 30,408 students in 50 randomised experiments; "most tutoring is effective, and ... giving students tutoring instead of showing them the answer is more effective for low knowledge students than high knowledge students"; video-based scaffolding beat text; open-response problems beat multiple choice; "adapting students' assignments based on their prior knowledge ... had a statistically significant positive effect"; motivational messages "have a negative impact"; requiring fewer problems in a row looked better mainly because more students failed to complete longer requirements; the authors caution that "results with effect sizes greater than 0.1 and p-values in range of 10^-3 can likely be trusted", weaker findings less so — [Prihar et al., EDM 2022 (ERIC ED624051)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED624051.pdf).
- Teacher-facing analytics: with Lumilo (teacher smart-glasses over an ITS), "teachers' use of real-time analytics in the classroom had the effect of narrowing the gap in learning outcomes across students of varying prior domain knowledge (Holstein et al., 2018b)", mediated by teachers reallocating time toward students the system flagged as struggling; without it teachers spread time evenly — [Holstein, McLaren & Aleven 2019, J. Learning Analytics (ERIC ED618924 PDF)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED618924.pdf); the 2018 AIED paper exists ("Student Learning Benefits of a Mixed-Reality Teacher Awareness Tool in AI-Enhanced Classrooms", LNCS) — [CrossRef](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/978-3-319-93843-1_12), abstract not retrieved.
- Baker's reading of the field: "the intelligent tutoring systems used at scale today are much simpler" than the original vision; he argues for "educational data mining driving human decision-making as an alternate paradigm ... focusing on intelligence amplification rather than artificial intelligence" — [Baker 2016, IJAIED (ERIC EJ1096875)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1096875).

### Inferences
- The honest planning number for a well-run adaptive-practice-plus-teacher system on standardized outcomes is about 0.1–0.4 SD per year, not 0.66; effects near 0.66 come from aligned local tests. Cornerstone should expect and measure the smaller number.
- In every large trial with a positive standardized effect, teachers were structurally in the loop (reports and training in Maine; 45 minutes of instructor time in Delhi; a blended curriculum for Cognitive Tutor). The literature does not support a claim that software alone produces those gains, and Cognitive Tutor's year-2 finding suggests teacher adaptation, not fidelity to the software's script, mattered.
- Heterogeneity cuts both ways: ASSISTments and Mindspark helped weaker students most; Snappet and the K-12 meta-analysis helped general/higher performers more. A school system must check its own distribution of gains, which at Cornerstone's size will be visible only as descriptive per-child trajectories, not as significant subgroup effects (see KQ6).

### Gaps
- The exact locally-developed vs standardized effect split in Kulik & Fletcher, the low-achiever g in Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper, and Snappet/Faber effect sizes could not be read from any opened page (unverified).
- The JEL 2020 published version of Escueta et al. and the RAND page were not accessible; the NBER working paper and ERIC/WWC records were used instead.
- No opened trial decomposes effects into software vs instructor components; Mindspark's authors explicitly do not.

## KQ5. Learning from humans: human-in-the-loop labelling, active learning, calibration, drift detection, golden-set evaluation, shadow deployment and graduation

### Takeaway
Mature practice is: label where the model is least sure (active learning), evaluate on held-out golden sets with calibration metrics (log loss/RMSE, reliability diagrams) and not AUC alone, deploy new versions in shadow/canary with monitoring for training–serving skew and prediction-quality regression, and re-validate whenever population or content shifts; in education the working examples are crowd-sourced teacher hints validated by RCT before scale (TeacherASSIST), competition-style golden sets (Eedi), and detector audits across populations and years, with teachers reliably found to be the intended decision-makers rather than the model.

### Cited Findings
- Active learning: the learner "actively chooses which data points to label"; main strategies are uncertainty sampling, query-by-committee, expected model change, expected error reduction and density-weighted methods; reported label savings are often large ("80-90% fewer labeled examples" in the survey's examples) but with caveats: sampling bias, noisy oracles, batch-mode selection, variable labelling cost and unclear stopping criteria — [Settles, Active Learning Literature Survey, CMU-CS-09-2, 2009](https://burrsettles.com/pub/settles.activelearning.pdf).
- Calibration: calibration is "the problem of predicting probability estimates representative of the true correctness likelihood"; modern deep networks are poorly calibrated (depth, width, weight decay, batch norm implicated); "temperature scaling -- a single-parameter variant of Platt Scaling -- is surprisingly effective at calibrating predictions" — [Guo, Pleiss, Sun & Weinberger 2017, arXiv 1706.04599](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599). In knowledge tracing specifically, the best models "are not calibrated (reliable) on some tutoring system datasets"; calibration plots binned by predicted probability "would allow us to correct for systematic biases with recalibration techniques", whereas biases visible only when binned by observed frequency cannot be fixed by standard recalibration — [Gervet et al. 2020](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1273917.pdf). AUC is inappropriate when a policy uses absolute predicted probabilities — [Pelánek 2017](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-overview.pdf).
- Production ML tests: 28 tests and monitoring needs in four categories (features and data; model development; ML infrastructure; monitoring), including training/serving skew, data staleness, data invariants, numerical stability and prediction-quality regression, plus canarying and shadow release for safe rollout, with a scoring scheme for production readiness — [Breck et al., "The ML Test Score", IEEE Big Data 2017 (Google Research page)](https://research.google/pubs/the-ml-test-score-a-rubric-for-ml-production-readiness-and-technical-debt-reduction/).
- Concept drift: "unforeseeable changes in the underlying distribution of streaming data over time", handled by detection, understanding and adaptation — [Lu et al., IEEE TKDE 31(12), 2018 (arXiv 2004.05785)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05785). Education-specific drift evidence: the 16-year detector-rot results above ([Levin et al. 2022](https://educationaldatamining.org/edm2022/proceedings/2022.EDM-short-papers.38/index.html)) and population non-transfer ([Ocumpaugh et al. 2014](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1022850)); Math Garden's own monitoring uses prediction accuracy and "parallel item reliability" over years and documents an adaptive-selection feedback loop that had to be switched off — [Brinkhuis et al. 2018](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1187391.pdf).
- Teachers as the labelling and decision layer (TeacherASSIST): in 2017–18, "132,738 distinct problems were assigned inside ASSISTments, but only 38,194 of those problems had on-demand assistance"; teachers then "created 40,292 new instances of assistance for 25,957 different problems in three years", 14 teachers contributing over 1,000 each; two large RCTs found that students receiving another teacher's crowd-sourced hint showed "significant statistical improvement on the next problem performance", i.e., quality "was sufficient ... to take on-demand assistance to scale" — [Patikorn & Heffernan, L@S 2020 (ERIC ED623498)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED623498).
- Human golden sets are themselves noisy: in the Eedi challenge, five expert teachers' pairwise question-quality judgments agreed with the best algorithms at only 0.60–0.80, "because the winning criterion takes the maximum agreement with any of the evaluators" — [Wang et al. 2021](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v133/wang21a/wang21a.pdf).
- Escalation design for uncertain model output: "detect-verify-escalate", routing uncertain misconception detections to a diagnostic follow-up question rather than to the teacher, with a teacher dashboard that filters the review queue — [arXiv 2606.23205](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23205).
- What teachers want from analytics: in co-design with 10 middle-school maths teachers, "teachers tended to prefer 'Seeing students thought processes' over most other superpowers, including 'Seeing students' misconceptions'"; all five interviewed wanted to "See students' thought processes" — [Holstein, McLaren & Aleven 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED618924.pdf).
- Fairness audits as part of validation: near-equal BKT and carelessness-detector performance across demographic and intersectional groups was established by explicit audit — [Zambrano, Zhang & Baker 2024](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/lak24-47-4.pdf); a framework for moving "from unknown bias to known bias" — [Baker & Hawn 2022](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1353563).

### Inferences
- Cornerstone's "golden set" idea is sound but needs three refinements the literature insists on: (1) score graduation on calibrated probabilistic metrics (log loss / RMSE and reliability diagrams per band of predicted probability), not only accuracy/AUC; (2) keep golden sets stratified by grade, channel (worksheet photo vs observation) and item type, because detectors fail on unseen sub-populations; (3) re-run the golden set on every content or cohort change (new grade, new item family), since rot is measured in years and populations, not in model versions.
- Teacher overrides are the highest-value labels but they arrive at the rate teachers correct, which is small; active learning (ask the teacher only about the reads/decisions the model is least sure of, and about a random audit sample) is how the literature stretches a few hundred labels, with the documented risk that the audit sample must stay random to detect drift the model is confidently wrong about.
- "Advisory to autonomous" graduation has a precedent: TeacherASSIST let teacher-written hints flow to strangers' students only after RCT evidence that they improved next-problem performance. Cornerstone can mirror this at smaller scale with shadow mode (system decides, teacher decides, disagreements logged) followed by a pre-set agreement/calibration bar.

### Gaps
- No opened source reports an education deployment with a documented shadow-then-graduate protocol and its thresholds; TeacherASSIST is the nearest analogue.
- Inter-rater reliability of teacher misconception tags, needed to set a realistic graduation ceiling, was not found.

## KQ6. The small-n problem: what 300–1,000 children can statistically support, where to borrow priors, where per-child longitudinal data suffices

### Takeaway
At 40–80 children per grade, Cornerstone can estimate a child's own mastery trajectory and item difficulty well enough for practice decisions (Elo/Rasch after 30–100 responses per item; BKT mastery from 25 students x 3 opportunities), but it cannot estimate misconception prevalence to better than about +/-6–12 percentage points per grade, cannot learn prerequisite edges, cannot fit per-child model parameters, and cannot detect intervention effects smaller than about d = 0.5 (individual randomisation) or d = 0.85 (four classes per arm) — so intervention "learning" must borrow from published trials and use within-child designs, not local A/B tests.

### Cited Findings
- Item calibration: 30 persons per item for +/-1 logit and about 100 for +/-0.5 logit (Rasch); at least 8 correct and 8 incorrect responses per item — [Linacre 1994](https://www.rasch.org/rmt/rmt74m.htm). Under adaptive item selection, simple proportion-correct difficulty "gives poor estimates" that do not improve with more students, while Elo tracks maximum-likelihood estimates; Elo "needs at least 100 students to get good estimates of item difficulty", 200 students give "reliable and highly correlated" estimates across methods — [Pelánek 2016](http://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf). IRT item parameters can be estimated accurately with 150–750 examinees depending on model and test length — [Sahin & Anil 2017](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1130806).
- Skill-model parameters: BKT mastery predictions usable from 25 students and 3 opportunities; stable skill parameters need 250–500 students and 6–10 opportunities; extreme parameters in up to 42% of small-sample fits — [Slater & Baker 2018](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/behaviormetrika_vfinal.pdf). Individualised parameters "are fitted using only few data points and thus can be significantly influenced by the noise in data"; class-level parameters are the compromise — [Pelánek 2017](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-overview.pdf).
- Structure learning: at N=150, 74–90% adjacency recall with weak orientation (simulation) — [Scheines et al. 2014](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/nynke/726-2021/week06/HCI%20prereq%20discovery/ScheinesSilverGoldin-EDM2014.pdf); low-support rules are the most noise-sensitive — [Chen et al. 2015](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED560515.pdf).
- Intervention detection at platform scale required 30,408 students across 50 experiments to yield "multiple statistically significant insights", and even then the authors trust only effects above d = 0.1 with p around 10^-3 — [Prihar et al. 2022](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED624051.pdf); one of the eight null CAL RCTs in the Escueta review "specifically mentions that the study was underpowered" — [Escueta et al.](https://oreopoulos.faculty.economics.utoronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/escueta-et-al-education-technology-nber-wp-23744-2017.pdf).
- Borrowing across populations fails for behaviour/affect detectors even within one US region — [Ocumpaugh et al. 2014](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1022850) — and across 16 years for all but the simplest model — [Levin et al. 2022](https://educationaldatamining.org/edm2022/proceedings/2022.EDM-short-papers.38/index.html); but knowledge estimates (BKT) were found near-equal across demographic groups at national scale — [Zambrano et al. 2024](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/lak24-47-4.pdf).
- Per-child longitudinal data is what the largest primary-maths system relies on: per-child model fit is tracked over years and "the difference between observed and expected responses tends to get centred closely around zero" after an initial phase — [Brinkhuis et al. 2018](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1187391.pdf); Elo's uncertainty function gives "fast coarse estimates after few answers and stability in the long run" — [Pelánek 2016](http://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf).
- Computed for this brief (standard two-sample power formula, alpha 0.05 two-tailed, power 0.80; Wilson intervals; binomial standard errors — computed in this session, not from a publication):
  - n per arm needed: d = 0.1 -> 1,570; d = 0.2 -> 393; d = 0.3 -> 175; d = 0.5 -> 63; d = 0.8 -> 25.
  - Minimum detectable effect with n per arm: 15 -> d 1.02; 30 -> 0.72; 60 -> 0.51; 150 -> 0.32; 300 -> 0.23; 500 -> 0.18.
  - Class-randomised (25 per class, intraclass correlation 0.15): 2 classes/arm -> MDE d 1.20; 4 -> 0.85; 8 -> 0.60; 20 -> 0.38.
  - Misconception frequency 95% CI width: true 10%, n=25 -> [3%, 28%]; n=60 -> [5%, 20%]; n=150 -> [6%, 16%]; n=300 -> [7%, 14%]; n=1,000 -> [8%, 12%]. True 5%, n=60 -> [2%, 14%]; n=300 -> [3%, 8%].
  - Item difficulty (p-value) SE at p=0.5: n=25 -> 0.10; 60 -> 0.065; 150 -> 0.041; 300 -> 0.029; 1,000 -> 0.016.
  - A child's own success rate on one skill, SE at p=0.7: 3 attempts -> 0.27; 5 -> 0.21; 10 -> 0.15; 20 -> 0.10; 40 -> 0.07.

### Inferences
- Per grade (roughly 40–80 children at Cornerstone today), one school year yields item-difficulty SEs of 0.06–0.10 and misconception-frequency intervals 12–24 points wide; these are fine for ordering items and for "this misconception is common here" flags, useless for claims like "prevalence fell from 12% to 8% after we changed the worksheet".
- Pooling across years (cohorts) is the only way the school reaches the 250–500 responses per skill that stabilise BKT parameters and the 100–200 per item that stabilise Elo/Rasch; the item bank and skill ids must therefore stay stable across years, and any re-versioning of an item should start a new rating (as Pelánek does) rather than silently inherit.
- Intervention effectiveness is not learnable locally in the causal sense: a realistic 0.2 SD effect needs ~400 children per arm. What the school can learn is descriptive (trajectories, time-to-mastery, blank/wrong/wrong-with-working mix) and per-child (does this child's spaced revisit hold at 6 weeks). Published priors — spacing and interleaving, tutoring over answer-showing, worked examples for low-prior-knowledge children — should be adopted as defaults and monitored, not re-tested.
- Where to borrow: initial item difficulties by band (school's Easy/Medium/Hard/Advance), BKT guess/slip ranges, spacing defaults, misconception catalogues (external refs already in the design). Where local data is enough: per-child mastery posteriors, per-item Elo/Rasch after the first ~30–100 responses, per-child forgetting evidence after ~20 revisits, and QA of the school's own expert ladder (children who pass B while failing A).

### Gaps
- No published study sizes learner-model calibration for schools of Cornerstone's size; the power and interval figures above are computed, and the ICC of 0.15 is an assumption.
- No source quantifies how many teacher-override labels are needed before a golden set discriminates between two prompt versions; this depends on the base rate of disagreement, which is unmeasured.

## KQ7. Reading children's handwriting: is per-writer adaptation evidenced?

### Takeaway
Writer adaptation is a real but modest lever in modern handwritten-text recognition (about 0.2–0.7 word-error-rate points from a handful of writer samples, with newer methods adapting from unlabeled or in-context examples), and children's handwriting is recognised as a distinct, harder distribution with its own emerging datasets; no study was found that measured per-child adaptation for children's digit recognition in maths worksheets.

### Cited Findings
- Writer adaptation for offline HTR (ResNet + LSTM/Transformer decoders; MAML and writer codes): the meta-learning method "achieved notable improvements: a 1.4 to 2.0 improvement in word error rate (WER)" overall, of which "the improvement due to writer adaptation is between 0.2 and 0.7 WER", using "only a handful of examples from a new writer (e.g., 16 examples)" — [van der Werff, Dhali & Schomaker 2023, arXiv 2307.15071](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15071).
- Related methods: MetaHTR enables writer adaptation "via single gradient step update, all during inference" (2021); MetaWriter personalises "updating less than 1% of its parameters" with unlabeled test data (2025); a 2026 method performs "inference-time writer adaptation without any parameter updates" via multimodal in-context learning; unsupervised synthetic-to-real adaptation to new writers (2019); writer-aware CNNs for Chinese text (2018) — [arXiv search listing](https://arxiv.org/search/?query=writer+adaptation+handwritten+text+recognition&searchtype=all&order=-announced_date_first).
- Classic online writer adaptation: Connell & Jain, "Writer adaptation for online handwriting recognition", IEEE TPAMI 24(3):329–346, 2002 — [CrossRef record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/34.990135) (abstract not retrieved; accuracy figures unverified).
- Children's handwriting as its own distribution: a CNN with stroke identification reached 91% accuracy on the Hijja dataset of Arabic characters written by children (arXiv 2211.02119, 2022); other recent work uses children's handwriting for dysgraphia detection (e.g., 91.8% accuracy, arXiv 2411.13595) and for predicting grade from handwriting dynamics — [arXiv search listing](https://arxiv.org/search/?query=children+handwriting+recognition+dataset&searchtype=all&order=-announced_date_first). A 2026 dataset of Bangla primary-school handwriting notes that existing datasets "frequently overlook the complexity and variability of primary-level learners' handwriting" and was "curated from 500 students, aged 7 to 12 and in grades 2 to 5", 24,420 images over 60 classes including 10 digits — [BPS2025, Data in Brief 2026 (Semantic Scholar record)](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.dib.2026.112700?fields=title,abstract).
- Automatic reading as marking is dominated by errors that look like the response-process noise Math Garden documents (typing errors, fast guesses produce the 21% of responses outside the model) — [Brinkhuis et al. 2018](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1187391.pdf) — i.e., a recognition system's confidence must be separated from the child's competence signal.

### Inferences
- The realistic per-child loop for Cornerstone is not fine-tuning a vision model per child but (a) keeping a small per-child bank of confirmed digit/symbol exemplars that can be supplied as few-shot/in-context examples (16 samples is the documented order of magnitude), (b) tracking per-child confusion pairs (e.g., 1/7, 4/9, 6/0) from teacher corrections to set per-child confidence thresholds, and (c) routing low-confidence reads to the teacher. Expected gains are small in aggregate (fractions of a point of error) but concentrated on the few children whose shapes deviate most, which is where the errors are.
- Because no evidence exists for children's digits specifically, the read-accuracy golden set (per child, per grade) is the only way to know whether adaptation helps here; the literature gives no prior to borrow.

### Gaps
- No opened study evaluates writer adaptation on children's handwritten digits or on maths worksheets; the "allograph-based writer adaptation" figures seen in a search snippet (offline 96.95% -> 97.37%; online 97.55% -> 97.91%) were not confirmed on an opened page — unverified.
- Vendor OCR/vision models' behaviour on Indian primary-school handwriting (Devanagari-influenced digit shapes, pencil on ruled paper) has no published measurement found.

## Council position

1. Adopt now (confidence high): keep the append-only evidence table and deterministic threshold rules as the truth, and add one learned layer — Elo-style ratings with an uncertainty function for item difficulty and per-child skill level, recomputed nightly from confirmed evidence. Evidence: Elo is what 400,000-child Math Garden runs on, needs ~100–200 students per item pool, tolerates new items and is one readable number per item ([Pelánek 2016](http://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf); [Brinkhuis 2018](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1187391.pdf)). Run Rasch alongside for item misfit once items have 30+ responses ([Linacre 1994](https://www.rasch.org/rmt/rmt74m.htm)).
2. Test BKT offline in shadow, per skill, with fixed guess/slip priors (confidence medium-high): mastery predictions are usable from ~25 children x 3 opportunities but fitted parameters are unreliable below 250–500 children, and extreme fits are common; use pyBKT, pool cohorts, and individualise at class level only ([Slater & Baker 2018](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/behaviormetrika_vfinal.pdf); [Pelánek 2017](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/umuai-overview.pdf); [Yudelson 2013](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/978-3-642-39112-5_18?fields=title,abstract)). Prefer a PFA/logistic variant if multiple skills per item or the three-signal coding (blank/wrong/wrong-with-working) must enter the model ([Pavlik 2009](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED506305.pdf)).
3. Never at this scale: deep knowledge tracing or transformer learner models (confidence high). They win only above ~1M interactions with many learners per item, are less interpretable, and are miscalibrated where data are sparse ([Gervet 2020](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1273917.pdf); [Khajah 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.02416)).
4. Spaced revisits: adopt the pedagogy now, the optimiser later (confidence medium). Distribute and interleave practice by default (d = 0.83 at grade 7; grade-3 effect visible at 1 week) with fixed FSRS-style default parameters and a ~90% retention target; do not optimise parameters per child, and validate the schedule with 1- and 6-week retention checks ([Rohrer 2020](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1237752); [Barzagar Nazari & Ebersbach 2019](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1262987); [Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html); [srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)).
5. Never learn prerequisite edges from co-failure as truth (confidence high). Keep the expert ladder; use data only to flag suspicious edges for human review, because recovery is partial and direction unreliable even at N=150 in simulation and noisier on real logs ([Scheines 2014](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/nynke/726-2021/week06/HCI%20prereq%20discovery/ScheinesSilverGoldin-EDM2014.pdf); [Chen 2015](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED560515.pdf)).
6. Misconception tags: model proposes, code verifies where it can, a diagnostic follow-up item verifies where it cannot, teacher confirms (confidence medium-high). Expect 8 false alarms per true detection at realistic prevalence; measure per-tag precision on the golden set; report prevalence only with intervals (at n=60 a true 10% reads as 5–20%) ([arXiv 2606.23205](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23205); [Wang 2021](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v133/wang21a/wang21a.pdf); computed intervals in KQ6).
7. Golden sets and graduation: yes, with calibration metrics and stratification (confidence high). Graduate a prompt or decision only when it beats the golden set on log loss/RMSE and reliability by predicted-probability band, per grade and per channel, after a shadow period in which teacher and system decisions are both logged; re-run on every cohort or content change; prefer simple models for small labelled sets ([Gervet 2020](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1273917.pdf); [Breck 2017](https://research.google/pubs/the-ml-test-score-a-rubric-for-ml-production-readiness-and-technical-debt-reduction/); [Levin 2022](https://educationaldatamining.org/edm2022/proceedings/2022.EDM-short-papers.38/index.html); [Ocumpaugh 2014](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1022850); [Patikorn & Heffernan 2020](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED623498)).
8. Teacher labels through active learning plus a random audit slice (confidence high). Ask teachers to confirm the reads and decisions the model is least sure about, but keep a fixed random sample of confident decisions in the review queue, or drift the model is confidently wrong about goes unseen ([Settles 2009](https://burrsettles.com/pub/settles.activelearning.pdf); [Levin 2022](https://educationaldatamining.org/edm2022/proceedings/2022.EDM-short-papers.38/index.html)).
9. Do not try to learn "what intervention works" from in-school experiments (confidence high). With 60 children per arm the smallest detectable effect is about d = 0.5, and about 0.85 with four classes per arm; realistic effects are 0.1–0.4 SD. Borrow: spacing/interleaving, tutoring over showing answers, worked examples and scaffolding for lower-prior-knowledge children, open response over multiple choice; monitor descriptively per child ([Prihar 2022](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED624051.pdf); [Escueta NBER WP](https://oreopoulos.faculty.economics.utoronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/escueta-et-al-education-technology-nber-wp-23744-2017.pdf); computed MDEs in KQ6).
10. Design the system as teacher amplification, not replacement (confidence high). Every large positive standardized-test result came with teachers structurally in the loop (Maine: teacher reports plus training, 0.18 SD; Delhi: 45 minutes of instructor time alongside 45 of software, 0.37 SD; Lumilo narrowed the prior-knowledge gap by redirecting teacher time), and no trial isolates the software's share ([Roschelle 2016](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1194398); [J-PAL Mindspark](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/disrupting-education-evidence-technology-aided-instruction-india); [Holstein 2019](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED618924.pdf); [Baker 2016](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1096875)).
11. Handwriting: per-child exemplar banks and confidence thresholds now; per-child model adaptation only if the read-accuracy golden set shows a gap (confidence medium-low). Adult-text evidence gives 0.2–0.7 WER points from ~16 samples; nothing measured for children's digits ([van der Werff 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15071); [arXiv listing](https://arxiv.org/search/?query=children+handwriting+recognition+dataset&searchtype=all&order=-announced_date_first)).
12. What "self-learning" should mean in the founder's terms (confidence high): (a) every confirmed assessment, photo, observation and correction updates each child's state and each item's rating that night, deterministically and auditably; (b) every teacher correction becomes a labelled example that scores the current prompt or rule against its predecessor; (c) a new version acts on its own only after it beats the golden set on calibration and precision, in shadow first, per grade and channel; (d) priors for items, skills, spacing and misconceptions are borrowed from the literature and larger populations and re-checked locally. It should not mean model-learned prerequisite edges, per-child fitted parameters, or causal claims about interventions from the school's own data — those are the black box the founder wants to avoid, and the statistics do not support them at this size.
