# Governance, ethics and human oversight for an AI-assisted school system (Cornerstone, Pune) — research notes

Seat: governance, ethics and human oversight. Date of research: 26 September 2026. Every URL below was opened during this session; where a page could not be opened (paywall, 403, PDF that would not render) that is said explicitly and the claim is either dropped or marked "unverified". Legal texts for India were read on the `dpdpa.com` mirror because `meity.gov.in` returned HTTP 403 — those quotations should be checked against the Gazette before being relied on in a compliance document.

Context the brief was written for: a private in-person school, Nursery–Grade 4 (growing), one Postgres holding a longitudinal per-child record (academic, observational, some socio-emotional), models used to read handwriting, transcribe teacher voice notes, draft reports and classify errors, every consequential step gated on a teacher's or coordinator's approval, names in a protected `pii` schema, external AI services receiving pseudonymised tokens. Two internal proposals: gate everything vs gate by exception.

## Key question 1 — India: DPDP Act 2023 s.9, the DPDP Rules 2025, and Ministry of Education / CBSE guidance on AI in schools

### Takeaway
Section 9 requires verifiable parental consent for any processing of a child's (under-18) data, forbids processing "likely to cause any detrimental effect on the well-being of a child", and forbids "tracking or behavioural monitoring of children or targeted advertising directed at children"; the Rules notified on 13 November 2025 exempt educational institutions from the consent requirement and the tracking ban *only* where processing is "restricted to tracking and behavioural monitoring: for the educational activities of such institution; or in the interests of safety of children enrolled", and those children's-data rules take effect 18 months after notification, i.e. 13 May 2027. No Ministry of Education or CBSE guidance governing schools' *own* use of AI (assessment, records, reports) was found; what exists (CBSE Circular Acad-18/2026, 9 April 2026) is a curriculum mandate for classes 3–8 that includes ethics, bias and fairness as content. MeitY's India AI Governance Guidelines (5 November 2025) are soft law: seven "sutras" including "People First", "Accountability" and "Understandable by Design", a call to "require human oversight", and grievance-redressal expectations, with no education-specific text.

### Cited Findings

**The Act (s.9), verbatim**
- s.9(1): "The Data Fiduciary shall, before processing any personal data of a child or a person with disability who has a lawful guardian obtain verifiable consent of the parent of such child or the lawful guardian, as the case may be, in such manner as may be prescribed." — [Indian Kanoon, DPDP Act s.9](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/98869575/)
- s.9(2): "A Data Fiduciary shall not undertake such processing of personal data that is likely to cause any detrimental effect on the well-being of a child." — [Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/98869575/)
- s.9(3): "A Data Fiduciary shall not undertake tracking or behavioural monitoring of children or targeted advertising directed at children." — [Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/98869575/)
- s.9(4): "The provisions of sub-sections (1) and (3) shall not be applicable to processing of personal data of a child by such classes of Data Fiduciaries or for such purposes, and subject to such conditions, as may be prescribed." s.9(5) lets the Central Government notify an age above which a fiduciary that processes children's data "in a manner that is verifiably safe" is exempt from s.9(1) and (3). Note s.9(2) (no detrimental processing) is *not* among the exemptible sub-sections. — [Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/98869575/)
- Rule 10(2) defines "adult" as "individual who completed 18 years", so a child is anyone under 18 for the whole of s.9. — [dpdpa.com mirror, Rule 10](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule10.html)

**Other duties in the Act that the exemption does not lift**
- s.8(3): where personal data "is likely to be — (a) used to make a decision that affects the Data Principal; or (b) disclosed…" the fiduciary must ensure its "completeness, accuracy, and consistency". s.8(5) security safeguards; s.8(6) breach notification to the Board and each affected principal; s.8(7) erasure when the purpose is no longer served or consent is withdrawn, "unless retention is necessary for compliance with any law". s.8(1)–(2): the fiduciary is responsible for compliance irrespective of processors, and may engage processors only "under a valid contract". — [dpdpa.com mirror, s.8](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-2/section8.html)
- s.12 (right to correction and erasure): "A Data Principal shall have the right to correction, completion, updating and erasure of her personal data for the processing of which she has previously given consent…"; on a request the fiduciary must correct inaccurate or misleading data, complete incomplete data and update outdated data; s.12(3) requires notifying entities the data was shared with and taking reasonable steps to have them correct it; s.12(5) allows refusal where alteration is infeasible/disproportionate or retention is required by law. — [dpdpa.com mirror, s.12](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-3/section12.html)
- Penalties (s.33 and Schedule): the mirror summarises maxima of ₹250 crore and ₹200 crore for the gravest classes, with a specific ₹200 crore ceiling associated with children's-data obligations; the mirror's own breakdown is internally inconsistent (it assigns ₹200 crore to s.8(5) security failures, which other summaries place at ₹250 crore), so treat the exact mapping as **unverified** until read in the Gazette. — [dpdpa.com mirror, s.33](https://dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-8/section33.html)

**The Rules (notified 13 November 2025) and their phasing**
- The final Rules were notified on 13 November 2025; the phased timeline reported is: immediate (13 Nov 2025), 12 months (13 Nov 2026), 18 months (13 May 2027) for "full enforcement of substantive obligations, including consent and children's data rules". — [Consently (industry briefing)](https://www.consently.in/blog/verifiable-parental-consent-dpdp-rules-2025-edtech-gaming)
- Rule 1 (commencement): "Rules 1, 2 and 17 to 21 shall come into force on the date of their publication in the Official Gazette"; "Rule 4 shall come into force one year after the date of publication of this Gazette"; "Rules 3, 5 to 16, 22 and 23 shall come into force eighteen months after the date of publication of this Gazette." Rules 10 (verifiable parental consent), 12 (children's exemptions) and 13 (Significant Data Fiduciaries) are therefore in the 18-month tranche. — [dpdpa.com mirror, Rule 1](https://dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule1.html)
- Rule 10(1): fiduciaries must adopt technical and organisational measures to ensure "verifiable consent of the parent is obtained before the processing of any personal data of a child", and must do due diligence that the consenting person is an adult, using either "reliable details of identity and age" already held, or details/"virtual token" voluntarily provided and issued by an "authorised entity" (including Digital Locker service providers under the IT Act 2000). Four illustrative cases are given (child self-identifies and parent verifies via existing account; via Digital Locker token; parent opens account with existing details; parent opens account with government identity/token). — [dpdpa.com mirror, Rule 10](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule10.html)
- Rule 12(1)–(2): s.9(1) and (3) "shall not be applicable to processing of personal data of a child by such class of Data Fiduciaries as are specified in Part A of Fourth Schedule, subject to such conditions as are specified in the said Part", and likewise for purposes in Part B. — [dpdpa.com mirror, Rule 12](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule12.html)
- Fourth Schedule Part A, educational institutions row (condition column): "Processing is restricted to tracking and behavioural monitoring: for the educational activities of such institution; or in the interests of safety of children enrolled with such institution." Crèche/day-care row: "restricted to tracking and behavioural monitoring in the interests of safety of children entrusted in the care". Transport row: "restricted to tracking the location of such children, in the interests of their safety, during the course of their travel". Clinical establishments: "restricted to provision of health services to the child… to the extent necessary for the protection of her health." — [dpdpa.com mirror, Fourth Schedule](https://dpdpa.com/schedule/schedule4.html)
- Fourth Schedule Part B (exempt purposes): exercise of legal powers/duties in the child's interests; subsidies/benefits/services under s.7; creating a user account limited to e-mail communication; restricting access to information detrimental to the child; confirming that a data principal is not a child (Rule 10 compliance). — [dpdpa.com mirror, Fourth Schedule](https://dpdpa.com/schedule/schedule4.html)
- Legal commentary on the draft: the educational exemption lets institutions process "without verifiable parental consent if necessary for academic purposes, safety, or institutional operations"; tracking is permissible "only for two key reasons: first, to support the educational activities of the institution, and second, to ensure the safety of children enrolled", and "any processing of data or behavioral tracking for purposes outside of these educational or safety-related reasons (such as advertisements) is prohibited"; "Data Fiduciaries are not permitted to carry out any data processing activities that have a detrimental effect on the well-being of a child." (Written while the draft was open for consultation; the final wording above matches.) — [Pacta, Impact of DPDP Rules on Educational Institutions](https://www.pacta.in/post/impact-of-dpdp-rules-on-educational-institutions-part-ii-of-a-3-part-series)
- Critique of the final Rules: the exemption permits "real-time location tracking of children without verifiable parental consent in the interest of children's safety", "real-time location" is undefined, and (Chitra Iyer, quoted) "Live monitoring does not create safety. True safety comes from prevention, stronger institutional practices, and better accountability"; the boundary of "education" is broad enough to include "any learning institution, including vocational training centres". — [MediaNama, Nov 2025](https://www.medianama.com/2025/11/223-dpdp-rules-tracking-children-parental-consent/)
- Rule 6 (security safeguards): "encryption, obfuscation, masking or the use of virtual tokens"; access control; "visibility on the accessing of such personal data, through appropriate logs, monitoring and review, for enabling detection of unauthorised access, its investigation and remediation"; "retain such logs and personal data for a period of one year, unless compliance with any law for the time being in force requires otherwise"; backups; and "appropriate provision in the contract" with any processor. — [dpdpa.com mirror, Rule 6](https://dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule6.html)
- Rule 13 (Significant Data Fiduciaries only): annual DPIA and audit; "A Significant Data Fiduciary shall observe due diligence to verify that technical measures including algorithmic software… are not likely to pose a risk to the rights of Data Principals." — [dpdpa.com mirror, Rule 13](https://dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule13.html)

**Ministry of Education / CBSE / MeitY, 2024–2026**
- CBSE Circular Acad-18/2026 (9 April 2026, "Computational Thinking and Artificial Intelligence (CT&AI) for classes 3rd to 8th"): "CBSE has released the curriculum framework for CT&AI, for students of classes 3rd to 8th, with the aim of creating AI-literate learners by 2030. The curriculum mandates CT + AI + Ethics + Unplugged Learning." The AI component "deepens understanding of the AI project lifecycle, data-driven decision-making and ethical considerations such as bias and fairness, enabling students to critically analyse how data and models influence outcomes." Other AI courses are "discontinued for class IX, session 2026-27 onwards"; NCERT is to provide CT&AI modules for classes 9–12. The circular is about what pupils learn, not about how schools may use AI on pupils. — [CBSE Circular Acad-18/2026 (PDF)](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/web_material/Circulars/2026/18_Circular_2026.pdf)
- The Ministry of Education announced (30 October 2025) AI and computational thinking from Class 3 in 2026-27 (classes 3–8) and 2027-28 (classes 9–10), framed as "AI for Public Good"; an expert committee chaired by Prof. Karthik Raman (IIT Madras) is reviewing the draft with NCERT; teacher training runs through NISHTHA. — [STEMpedia blog](https://thestempedia.com/blog/cbse-proposes-new-ai-curriculum-in-academic-year-2026-27-for-class-3-onwards-ncert-to-review/) (secondary; the article does not cite a circular number)
- School Education Secretary Sanjay Kumar: "We need to move fast so that students and teachers are properly aligned with this technology"; a pilot exists for teacher training in lesson planning with AI tools. — [Careers360](https://news.careers360.com/centre-introduce-ai-in-school-curriculum-from-class-3-onwards-from-2026-27-session)
- India AI Governance Guidelines (MeitY, 5 November 2025): seven sutras — "Trust is the Foundation, People First, Innovation over Restraint, Fairness & Equity, Accountability, Understandable by Design, and Safety, Resilience & Sustainability"; "Understandable by Design: Provide disclosures and explanations that can be understood by the intended user and regulators"; recommendations include "Establish a robust AI incidents mechanism…" and "Require human oversight and other safeguards to mitigate loss of control risks especially in sensitive sectors"; entities "deploying AI systems should establish accessible and effective grievance redressal mechanisms as part of their accountability obligations"; the document notes harms "to the long-term mental development and well-being of children" and lists the Ministry of Education only as one agency among many — no education-specific guidance. — [India AI Governance Guidelines (PIB PDF)](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc2025115685601.pdf)
- Chambers' reading of the same document: "Humans should, as far as possible, have final control over AI systems" and "human oversight is essential to maintain accountability"; organisations should explain systems via "Visualizations; Model extraction; Feature importance", "Inform individuals that they are interacting with an AI system at the time of interaction", and "enable the users to report AI-related harms and ensure resolution… within a reasonable timeframe"; India relies on the DPDP Act, IT Act and Consumer Protection Act rather than a new AI statute; proposed bodies: AI Governance Group, Technology & Policy Expert Committee, AI Safety Institute; "no mention of education or children" beyond the above. — [Chambers and Partners](https://chambers.com/articles/meity-unveils-india-s-approach-towards-regulating-artificial-intelligence)

### Inferences
- Observation logs and learner models are "behavioural monitoring" in plain meaning. Cornerstone can rely on the Part A exemption from consent and from the s.9(3) ban only for processing that is demonstrably "for the educational activities of such institution" or child safety. Everything else about a child (photos for newsletters, sharing with vendors for product improvement, model training on pupil work, marketing) falls back on s.9(1) verifiable parental consent through a Rule 10 mechanism — so the school should build a Rule 10 flow anyway, and keep a per-data-element register that states which purpose (educational activity / safety / consented) covers it.
- s.9(2) is not exemptible: any feature "likely to cause any detrimental effect on the well-being of a child" is unlawful regardless of consent or exemption. A persistent "low ability" flag, a socio-emotional label that follows a child, or a ranking shown to parents are the obvious candidates for that test (see Key question 4 for the evidence that labelling harms).
- s.8(3) makes accuracy a legal duty precisely where the system is used "to make a decision that affects" the child — which is what an error classification feeding a prescription, or a report line, is. The confirm queue is therefore not just good practice; it is the accuracy control the Act requires, and the s.12 correction right is the contestability control.
- The 13 May 2027 date gives Cornerstone roughly eight months from now to have the consent mechanism, retention/erasure logic (s.8(7)), one-year access logs (Rule 6), processor contracts (s.8(2), Rule 6) and correction workflow (s.12) live. Nothing prevents doing it earlier; s.9(2) and the constitutional duty of care exist now.
- Pseudonymised tokens sent to external models are still personal data in the fiduciary's hands; the vendor is a processor and needs a "valid contract" with security provisions. Cross-border transfer rules (s.16) were not fetched — check them for any vendor hosted outside India.
- A small school is unlikely to be designated a Significant Data Fiduciary, but Rule 13(3) ("algorithmic software… not likely to pose a risk to the rights of Data Principals") is the clearest statement in Indian law of the bar a data-processing algorithm is expected to meet, and is a sensible voluntary standard.

### Gaps
- MeitY's own site returned HTTP 403; the Rules text was read on a third-party mirror. The Gazette PDF should be checked, especially the Schedule to s.33 penalty mapping (marked unverified).
- No MoE/CBSE/NCERT guidance on schools' *operational* use of AI (assessment, record-keeping, reports to parents) was found through the pages reached; the web-search budget was exhausted before a targeted search for a 2025–26 DoSEL "responsible AI in schools" advisory could be run. Treat "none exists" as unconfirmed.
- Internet Freedom Foundation analysis of the final Rules could not be reached (404 on the tag page); the critique above is MediaNama's.
- s.16 (cross-border transfer), s.13 (grievance redressal) and the Board's procedures were not fetched.

## Key question 2 — EU AI Act: education as high-risk, the obligations, the 2026–2027 timeline, and what it sets as the global bar

### Takeaway
Annex III point 3 makes AI systems that "evaluate learning outcomes, including when those outcomes are used to steer the learning process", assess "the appropriate level of education", or decide admission/assignment high-risk; the provider duties are risk management, data governance, automatic logging, transparency to deployers, human oversight designed against automation bias, and accuracy/robustness, while deployers must assign oversight to trained, empowered people, keep logs for at least six months, inform the persons affected, and (for public bodies and public-service providers) do a fundamental-rights impact assessment. Emotion inference by AI in education institutions is *prohibited* outright since 2 February 2025. The Annex III obligations were postponed by the Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027. Cornerstone is outside the Act's territorial scope, but the Act's structure is the most detailed public specification of what "human oversight" and "transparency" must mean in an education AI system.

### Cited Findings
- Annex III point 3 (Education and vocational training), verbatim: "(a) AI systems intended to be used to determine access or admission or to assign natural persons to educational and vocational training institutions at all levels; (b) AI systems intended to be used to evaluate learning outcomes, including when those outcomes are used to steer the learning process of natural persons in educational and vocational training institutions at all levels; (c) AI systems intended to be used for the purpose of assessing the appropriate level of education that an individual will receive or will be able to access…; (d) AI systems intended to be used for monitoring and detecting prohibited behaviour of students during tests…" — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Annex III](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/)
- Recital 56 rationale: such systems "may determine the educational and professional course of a person's life", may violate "the right to education and training as well as the right not to be discriminated against", and can "perpetuate historical patterns of discrimination, for example against women, certain age groups, persons with disabilities, or persons of certain racial or ethnic origins"; proctoring is "particularly intrusive". — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Recital 56](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/recital/56/)
- Article 5(1)(f) (prohibited, applies from 2 February 2025): "the use of AI systems to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the market for medical or safety reasons". Article 5(1)(b) also prohibits systems that "exploit any of the vulnerabilities of a natural person or a specific group of persons due to their age, disability or a specific social or economic situation" to distort behaviour causing significant harm. — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Article 5](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/)
- Article 14 (human oversight): systems "shall be designed and developed in such a way, including with appropriate human-machine interface tools, that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons"; measures must enable the overseer to (a) "properly understand the relevant capacities and limitations" and monitor for anomalies; (b) "remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output" (automation bias), "in particular for high-risk AI systems used to provide information or recommendations for decisions to be taken by natural persons"; (c) "correctly interpret the high-risk AI system's output"; (d) "decide… not to use the high-risk AI system or to otherwise disregard, override or reverse the output"; (e) "intervene in the operation… through a 'stop' button or a similar procedure". For biometric identification (Annex III 1(a)) no action may be taken unless verified by "at least two natural persons". — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Article 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- Article 12 (record-keeping): "High-risk AI systems shall technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system", for traceability appropriate to the intended purpose; for biometric systems the log must include "the period of each use", the reference database, input data leading to a match, and "identification of the natural persons involved in the verification of the results". — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Article 12](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/12/)
- Article 13 (transparency to deployers): operation must be "sufficiently transparent to enable deployers to interpret a system's output and use it appropriately"; instructions must state intended purpose, accuracy/robustness levels, known limitations, "performance regarding specific persons or groups", input-data specifications, and "the human oversight measures… including the technical measures put in place to facilitate the interpretation of the outputs". — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Article 13](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/13/)
- Article 26 (deployers): "Deployers shall assign human oversight to natural persons who have the necessary competence, training and authority, as well as the necessary support" (26(2)); ensure input data is "relevant and sufficiently representative" (26(4)); monitor and report risks/serious incidents (26(5)); keep automatically generated logs "for a period appropriate to the intended purpose… of at least six months" (26(6)); "inform the natural persons that they are subject to the use of the high-risk AI system" (26(11)); use provider information to carry out a GDPR Article 35 DPIA (26(9)). — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Article 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)
- Article 27 (fundamental rights impact assessment, for public bodies and private providers of public services): must describe the processes, period and frequency of use, "categories of natural persons and groups likely to be affected", specific risks of harm, "the implementation of human oversight measures", and measures if risks materialise "including the arrangements for internal governance and complaint mechanisms". — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Article 27](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/27/)
- Article 86 (right to explanation): a person subject to a decision taken by a deployer on the basis of an Annex III system that "produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects" them has the right to "clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision taken"; the page lists 2 August 2026 as its application date. — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Article 86](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/86/)
- The Commission's summary of the seven high-risk requirements: "adequate risk assessment and mitigation systems"; "high quality of the datasets feeding the system to minimise risks of discriminatory outcomes"; "logging of activity to ensure traceability of results"; "detailed documentation"; "clear and adequate information to the deployer"; "appropriate human oversight measures"; "high level of robustness, cybersecurity and accuracy". The page states the AI Omnibus "entered force on 27 July 2026 as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744" and that the Annex III date is now 2 December 2027. — [European Commission, Regulatory framework for AI](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)
- Timeline as currently published: entry into force 1 August 2024; prohibitions 2 February 2025; GPAI obligations 2 August 2025; most remaining obligations (including Article 50 transparency) 2 August 2026; Annex III high-risk 2 December 2027; Annex I high-risk 2 August 2028. — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Implementation timeline](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/implementation-timeline/)
- "The text in force today is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI", which "moved four application dates and changed nothing else": Annex III from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027 (+16 months), Annex I from 2 August 2027 to 2 August 2028 (+24 months). — [regulation-ai.eu](https://www.regulation-ai.eu/en/ai-act/)

### Inferences
- Map Cornerstone's own steps onto Annex III 3(b): reading a worksheet, marking it, classifying the error, updating a child-skill state and generating a prescription is exactly "evaluate learning outcomes… used to steer the learning process". If the school were in the EU it would be a *deployer* of a high-risk system and, as a private school providing a public service, would arguably owe a fundamental-rights impact assessment. Adopting Articles 12, 13, 14, 26 and 86 voluntarily is the cheapest way to make "not a black box" concrete and auditable.
- Article 5(1)(f) is the sharpest line for the socio-emotional part of the record: an AI must not infer a child's emotions. A teacher may write an observation; a model may transcribe or tidy it; no model should be asked "how does this child feel" from a face, a voice or a page. In DPDP terms the same conduct is "behavioural monitoring", exempt only if it is an educational activity or safety measure and never if "likely to cause any detrimental effect on the well-being of a child".
- Article 14(4)(b) is the only statute that names automation bias as a design requirement. The evidence base behind it is in Key question 5; Cornerstone should write the same sentence into its own design rules.
- Article 26(6) (logs ≥ 6 months) and DPDP Rule 6 (access logs 1 year) together suggest a retention floor of one year for the audit trail; the Article 12 logging fields for biometric verification (period of use, inputs, "identification of the natural persons involved in the verification") are a good template for what a confirm-queue event should record.

### Gaps
- EUR-Lex pages for Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 would not render through the fetch tool; the regulation number, the 27 July 2026 entry-into-force date and the new dates rest on the Commission's policy page and regulation-ai.eu (which agree). One of the fetched summaries mis-stated the "original" Annex III date as 2 December 2025; the correct original date, 2 August 2026, is what regulation-ai.eu and the Article 14 page state.
- Whether Article 86 was itself re-timed by the Omnibus is not confirmed; the Article 86 page shows 2 August 2026.
- Articles 9 and 10 (risk management, data governance) were not read in full; only the Commission's summary was.

## Key question 3 — UNESCO 2023 guidance and 2024 competency frameworks; OECD; US FERPA and COPPA (2025 rule)

### Takeaway
UNESCO's 2023 Guidance for generative AI in education proposes a "human-centred" approach, an age floor of 13 for independent use of GenAI, mandatory data-privacy protection, institutional validation of tools before use and seven regulatory steps spanning data protection through GenAI-specific frameworks; its September 2024 competency frameworks (15 teacher competencies, 12 student competencies) put "human-centred mindset" and "ethics of AI" first and insist AI "should complement, not replace" teachers. The OECD AI Principles (2019, amended May 2024) require that those adversely affected by an AI system can "challenge its output" on "plain and easy-to-understand information on the factors, and the logic", and require traceability of datasets, processes and decisions. FERPA gives parents the right to inspect, to request amendment and to control disclosure of education records; the FTC's amended COPPA Rule (published 22 April 2025, effective 23 June 2025, compliance by 22 April 2026) adds voiceprints and other biometrics to personal information, requires separate consent for third-party disclosure, a written security programme and a written retention policy, and *rejected* the proposed school-authorisation exception for ed-tech.

### Cited Findings

**UNESCO**
- Guidance for generative AI in education and research, published 7 September 2023: proposes "a human-agent and age-appropriate approach to the ethical validation and pedagogical design processes", requires "mandating the protection of data privacy", and recommends "setting an age limit for the independent conversations with GenAI platforms". — [UNESCO, Guidance page](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research)
- The press release states the Guidance "sets an age limit of 13 for the use of AI tools in the classroom and calls for teacher training", that "a recent UNESCO global survey of over 450 schools and universities showed that less than 10% of them had institutional policies and/or formal guidance concerning the use of generative AI applications", and quotes Audrey Azoulay: "Generative AI can be a tremendous opportunity for human development, but it can also cause harm and prejudice. It cannot be integrated into education without public engagement, and the necessary safeguards and regulations from governments." — [UNESCO press release, 7 Sept 2023](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-governments-must-quickly-regulate-generative-ai-schools)
- UN News on the same document: it recommends "considering an age limit of 13 for their use in the classroom", "guardrails on data protection and user privacy", and immediate steps including "Validating AI systems for student use through educational institutions" and requirements on GenAI providers. — [UN News, Sept 2023](https://news.un.org/en/story/2023/09/1140477)
- The seven regulatory steps run "from endorsing data protection regulations to building GenAI-specific frameworks and reflecting on long-term implications"; the guidance proposes "a minimum of 13 for independent use of GenAI platforms, with acknowledgment that the EU's GDPR sets the threshold at 16", and states "generative AI in education should serve human capabilities. It should not replace them." — [Med Kharbach summary (secondary)](https://medkharbach.com/generative-ai-in-education-unesco-guidance/); UNESCO's own page confirms the categories but the fetched text did not enumerate the seven steps — [CEDEFOP resource page](https://www.cedefop.europa.eu/en/tools/vet-toolkit-tackling-early-leaving/resources/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research-unesco)
- AI competency frameworks for teachers and students, launched at Digital Learning Week, September 2024: the teacher framework has five aspects across three progression levels (15 competencies) — "Human-centred mindset, Ethics of AI, AI foundations and applications, AI pedagogy, AI for professional development"; the student framework has four aspects across three levels (12) — "Human-centred mindset, Ethics of AI, AI techniques and applications, AI system design"; "AI tools should complement, not replace, the vital roles and responsibilities of teachers", and AI "should support human decision-making and intellectual development, rather than undermine or replace it". — [UNESCO, What you need to know about the frameworks](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/what-you-need-know-about-unescos-new-ai-competency-frameworks-students-and-teachers)

**OECD**
- OECD AI Principles: adopted May 2019, updated May 2024; five values-based principles — inclusive growth; human rights and democratic values including fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; accountability. — [OECD.AI, AI Principles](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles)
- Principle 1.3 (transparency and explainability): AI actors should provide information "to foster a general understanding of AI systems, including their capabilities and limitations", make "stakeholders aware of their interactions with AI systems, including in the workplace", where feasible provide "plain and easy-to-understand information on the sources of data/input, factors, processes and/or logic that led to the prediction", and information enabling "those adversely affected by an AI system to challenge its output". — [OECD.AI, Principle 1.3](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/ai-principles/P7)
- Principle 1.5 (accountability): "AI actors should ensure traceability, including in relation to datasets, processes and decisions made during the AI system lifecycle, to enable analysis of the AI system's outputs and responses to inquiry", and apply "a systematic risk management approach to each phase of the AI system lifecycle on an ongoing basis". — [OECD.AI, Principle 1.5](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/ai-principles/P9)

**United States**
- FERPA protects "education records"; parents have the right to inspect and review, to request amendment of inaccurate information, and to control disclosure of personally identifiable information, with exceptions such as "school officials with legitimate educational interests"; rights transfer to the student at 18 or on entering post-secondary education; it applies to agencies and institutions receiving US Department of Education funds. — [US Dept of Education, Student Privacy: What is FERPA?](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/what-ferpa)

The FTC "rejected the proposed school-authorization exception for ed tech" to avoid conflict with FERPA. — [Latham & Watkins client alert](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule) (the Federal Register page itself redirected to an anti-bot page and could not be read)

**Adjacent soft law that sets the "child" bar**
- GDPR Recital 38: "Children merit specific protection with regard to their personal data, as they may be less aware of the risks, consequences and safeguards concerned", in particular for marketing and "personality or user profiles". — [gdpr-info.eu, Recital 38](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-38/)
- GDPR Article 22: a right "not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing… which produces legal effects… or similarly significantly affects" the person; where allowed, safeguards must include the right "to obtain human intervention", "to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision". — [gdpr-info.eu, Article 22](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-22-gdpr/)
- GDPR Recital 65 (erasure): the right "is relevant in particular where the data subject has given his or her consent as a child and is not fully aware of the risks involved by the processing, and later wants to remove such personal data". — [gdpr-info.eu, Recital 65](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-65/)
- UK ICO Children's code (15 standards): "The best interests of the child should be a primary consideration"; DPIA; transparency "in clear language suited to the age of the child"; "Do not use children's personal data in ways that have been shown to be detrimental to their wellbeing"; "Settings must be 'high privacy' by default"; "Collect and retain only the minimum amount of personal data you need"; "Switch options which use profiling 'off' by default (unless you can demonstrate a compelling reason…)"; if parents can monitor a child, "provide an obvious sign to the child when they are being monitored"; "Do not use nudge techniques". — [ICO, Code standards](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/code-standards/)
- UNICEF Policy guidance on AI for children: the current page (version 3.0, December 2025) lists ten requirements including "Ensure regulatory frameworks, oversight and compliance", "Protect children's data and privacy", "Ensure non-discrimination and fairness", "Provide transparency, explainability and accountability for children", "Support children's best interests, development and well-being". (The 2021 version 2.0 listed nine; that count comes from search snippets only and is unverified here.) — [UNICEF Innocenti, Guidance on AI and children](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/policy-guidance-ai-children)
- UK DfE "Generative AI: product safety standards" (published 22 January 2025; updated 19 January 2026 with standards on cognitive development, emotional and social development, mental health and manipulation): products should be "recording input prompts and responses", "track and report when learners offload thinking to the system", "enforce hard limits that cannot be bypassed by the learner… until reset by a teacher or administrator", "conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment… during the full life cycle", "not collect, store, share or use personal data for any commercial purposes, including further model training and fine-tuning, without confirmation of appropriate lawful basis", "mitigate the potential for cognitive deskilling, or long-term developmental harm to learners", "only show a full solution after a genuine learner attempt", detect "signs of learner distress" and raise "a safeguarding flag to the institution's safeguarding lead", and avoid "sycophancy and flattery" and "dark patterns". — [GOV.UK, Generative AI: product safety standards](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-ai-product-safety-standards/generative-ai-product-safety-standards)
- UK DfE "Generative AI in education" (updated 12 August 2025): "Any content produced requires critical judgement to check for appropriateness and accuracy"; "The quality and content of any final documents remains the responsibility of the professional who produced it and the organisation they belong to, regardless of the tools or resources used"; "It is recommended that personal data is not used in generative AI tools"; where it is, "ensure the data subjects (pupils and parents or legal guardians) understand that their personal data is being processed using AI tools" and "seek agreement"; pupils "should only be using generative AI in education settings with appropriate safeguards in place, such as close supervision". — [GOV.UK, Generative AI in education](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-artificial-intelligence-in-education/generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-in-education)

### Inferences
- Across UNESCO, OECD, GDPR, the ICO code, UNICEF and the DfE the same five soft-law demands recur: best interests of the child as the primary consideration; profiling off by default and never for detriment; plain-language transparency scaled to the audience (including the child); a human who owns the output; and a right to challenge and correct. These are exactly the design demands the founder has stated informally; they can be cited as the external bar.
- The COPPA change most relevant to Cornerstone is the addition of voiceprints to "personal information": teacher voice notes are adult data, but any recording of a child's voice (reading aloud, oral assessment) is biometric-class data under the US rule and would be "behavioural monitoring" under DPDP s.9(3) — do not record children's voices without a clear educational purpose, a retention limit and a parent-facing explanation.
- UNESCO's age-13 floor and the DfE's "close supervision" standard both cut the same way for Nursery–Grade 4: no child at Cornerstone should be an *end-user* of a generative model. Models in this system act on the child's work for adults, not with the child.

## Key question 4 — Documented harms: labelling and tracking of children, bias in automated grading and speech recognition, "permanent record" effects, parental surveillance, inBloom; harms specific to young children

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced harm is the one Cornerstone's learner model is most exposed to: grouping and labelling by measured attainment yields "0 months" average progress with "small negative impact" for low attainers (EEF/QRF, 58 studies, effects "similar… for both primary and secondary"), driven by lower teacher expectations, misallocation of disadvantaged pupils, poorer teaching for low groups, rigidity of movement and "negative self-fulfilling prophecies". Algorithmic risk labels have failed in practice (Wisconsin's DEWS: "high risk" wrong about three-quarters of the time, false-alarm rates 42 points higher for Black than white students, no effect on graduation; Ofqual 2020: ~36–40% of A-level grades downgraded by a cohort-statistics algorithm and reversed within four days). Automated scoring and speech recognition carry measured subgroup bias (e-rater ±1 point by nationality/ethnicity on a 6-point scale; ASR word error rates 35% vs 19% for Black vs white speakers; children's speech, especially ages 4–8 and code-switched Indian children's speech, is recognised worse than adults'). inBloom showed that a well-funded, lawful student-data platform collapses when parents are neither notified nor asked. Young children are specifically more exposed: expectancy effects in the original Pygmalion study were significant only in grades 1–2, ASR error is highest at ages 4–7, and reading gaps compound ("Matthew effects").

### Cited Findings

**Labelling, setting, expectancy**
- EEF/QRF Toolkit, setting and streaming: "0 months progress on average"; low attainers "small negative impact", middle "negligible", high "small positive"; evidence security "very limited" (58 studies, small share recent, large share non-randomised); cost "very low". Mechanisms: "Disadvantaged pupils may suffer from lower teacher expectations which increases their chances of being placed in lower sets"; pupils in lower groups "are more likely to be taught by less experienced and qualified teachers"; "Pupils from disadvantaged backgrounds are more likely to be misallocated to lower sets"; recommends "flexible practices that allow pupils to move between sets"; setting may create "negative self-fulfilling prophecies" and hurt "attitudes and engagement of low attaining pupils"; "Similar effects have been found for both primary and secondary age pupils". — [Queen Rania Foundation mirror of the EEF Toolkit entry](https://qrf.org/en/teaching-and-learning-toolkit/setting-and-streaming) (the EEF's own page returned 403)
- Pygmalion (Rosenthal & Jacobson 1968): teachers were told ~20% of randomly chosen pupils were "intellectual bloomers"; "first- and second-graders showed statistically significant gains" — only the youngest grades did. Raudenbush's 1984 meta-analysis of 18 experiments: "when teachers had gotten to know their students for two weeks, the effect of a prior expectancy induction was reduced to virtually zero". A 2005 review (Jussim & Harber) concludes effects are "usually small and temporary", with expectancy effects "stronger for stigmatized groups". — [Wikipedia, Pygmalion effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pygmalion_effect) (secondary; the Jussim & Harber abstract itself is paywalled — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1207/s15327957pspr0902_3?fields=title,abstract,year,venue,authors,citationCount) confirms title/venue only)
- Matthew effects in reading (Stanovich 1986, Reading Research Quarterly 21(4):360–407): "early success in acquiring reading skills usually leads to later successes in reading… while failing to learn to read before the third or fourth year of schooling may be indicative of lifelong problems"; a 2014 review found "mixed empirical evidence", with Matthew effects for primary-school skill development and compensatory patterns where skills have ceilings. — [Wikipedia, Matthew effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_effect)
- Wisconsin Dropout Early Warning System (DEWS): in 2020-21 over 32,000 students (15% of grades 6–9) were labelled "high risk"; the label was wrong "nearly 75% of the time" about non-graduation; false-alarm rates were 42 percentage points higher for Black and 18 points higher for Hispanic students than for white students; inputs included race, free/reduced lunch status, test scores, discipline and disability; a UC Berkeley study found the label had "no statistically significant effect" on on-time graduation; educators got little training ("They just handed us the data and said, 'Figure it out.'"); a 2021 internal DPI equity analysis found DEWS unfair, and nothing was done or disclosed. — [The Markup, "False Alarm", 27 April 2023](https://themarkup.org/machine-learning/2023/04/27/false-alarm-how-wisconsin-uses-race-and-income-to-label-students-high-risk)
- Ofqual 2020: the standardisation algorithm used centre historical performance and teacher rank order; roughly 36–40% of A-level grades were downgraded from teacher assessments, 3% by two grades; state-school pupils were downgraded while small independent-school cohorts were treated differently; results 13 August 2020, reversal to teacher grades 17 August 2020; Ofqual's chief regulator and the DfE Permanent Secretary resigned; officials had been warned. — [Wikipedia, 2020 UK exam grading controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_Kingdom_school_exam_grading_controversy)

**Automated scoring bias**
- At least 21 US states used NLP scoring on standardised tests; in 18 of them only 5–20% of essays got human verification. ETS's e-rater research: Chinese students averaged 1.3 points higher than expert human graders on a 6-point scale (attributed to "pre-memorized shell text"), African American students 0.81 points lower; language learners under-scored across studies from 1999 to 2018; Les Perelman's BABEL nonsense essays scored 4/6; Emily Bender: algorithms "pick up bias… and amplify it". — [Vice/Motherboard, 20 Aug 2019](https://www.vice.com/en/article/flawed-algorithms-are-grading-millions-of-students-essays/)
- Bridgeman, Trapani & Attali (2012), as summarised in a peer-reviewed EDM paper: "human and e-rater scores were incomparable across some subgroups", "students' essays from China tend to get higher scores from e-rater than from humans", and "rankings of subgroups would be different if machine scores were substituted for human scores". — [EDM 2022, Individual Fairness Evaluation for AES](https://educationaldatamining.org/edm2022/proceedings/2022.EDM-long-papers.18/); original paper metadata — [Semantic Scholar](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1080/08957347.2012.635502?fields=title,abstract,year,venue,authors,citationCount)

**Speech recognition bias**
- Koenecke et al. (PNAS, March 2020): systems from Amazon, IBM, Google, Microsoft and Apple, on more than 2,000 speech samples, misrecognised 35% of words from Black speakers vs 19% from white speakers; over 20% of Black speakers' samples vs under 2% of white speakers' were transcribed with ≥50% errors; the gap persisted when speakers were "matched by gender and age and when they spoke the same words"; attributed to training data of "English as spoken by white Americans". — [Stanford News, 23 March 2020](https://news.stanford.edu/2020/03/23/automated-speech-recognition-less-accurate-blacks/)
- Child speech: adults ~5% WER in real-world settings vs 11–18% for all children, 15–21% for ages 6–10 and up to 35% for kindergarteners (4–6); performance "begins to stabilize after age 10" and aligns with adults "around ages 12–13"; "children's articulation interacts with dialectal variation, leading to compounded errors". — [The Learning Agency, Closing the Child Speech Recognition Gap](https://the-learning-agency.com/guides-resources/closing-the-child-speech-recognition-gap-evidence-limitations-and-paths-forward/) (practitioner guide; the underlying studies were not opened)
- Indian, code-switched, children's speech (HiACC corpus, Data in Brief 2025): 24 adults (19–42) recorded in a lab vs 20 children (10–14) recorded "in school classrooms" with playground/vehicle noise; Whisper-medium 16% WER adults vs 18% children; Wav2Vec2-XLS-R 38% vs 40%; MMS-1B 31% vs 36%; "standard models trained on monolingual data underperform by approximately 42% WER on our test set". — [PMC, HiACC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12329218/)

**"Permanent record", surveillance, and the inBloom collapse**
- inBloom: $100 million from the Gates Foundation and Carnegie Corporation; Louisiana withdrew May 2013, Jefferson County (Colorado) November 2013, New York April 2014; shutdown announced 21 April 2014; parents objected that it was designed to "facilitate the sharing of children's personal and very sensitive information with data-mining vendors, with no attention paid to the need for parental notification or consent" (Leonie Haimson); New York legislated to bar the education department "from contracting with outside companies to store, organize, or aggregate student data"; CEO Iwan Streichenberger: "building public acceptance for the solution will require more time and resources than anyone could have anticipated." — [Education Week, April 2014](https://www.edweek.org/technology/inbloom-to-shut-down-amid-growing-data-privacy-concerns/2014/04)
- Nine states were original partners; Louisiana, Colorado and North Carolina pulled out, Massachusetts paused, Illinois made it voluntary, Kentucky, Delaware and Georgia said they never intended to share personal data; fields included "student names, addresses, grades, test scores, economic, race, special education status, disciplinary status"; New York planned sharing "without any parental notification or consent"; the privacy policy said inBloom "cannot guarantee the security of the information stored". — [Parent Coalition for Student Privacy, inBloom background](https://studentprivacymatters.org/background-of-inbloom/)
- ClassDojo (behaviour-points app): the company claims use in "95% of pre-kindergarten through eighth grade schools in the United States" and 180 countries; a 2017 LSE critique raised "student data protection and mental health" concerns, argued such apps normalise "surveillance", and that "behavior is individualized to discount larger social and environmental influences"; teachers reported more "aggressive emails from parents due to convenience of the app". — [Wikipedia, ClassDojo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ClassDojo) (secondary; the 2014 New York Times report could not be fetched; Williamson's 2017 peer-reviewed critique "Decoding ClassDojo: psycho-policy, social-emotional learning and persuasive educational technologies" exists but its abstract is paywalled — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1080/17439884.2017.1278020?fields=title,abstract,year,venue,authors,citationCount))
- The "permanent record" concern is codified rather than only argued: GDPR Recital 65 on erasure of data consented to as a child; DPDP s.8(7) erasure when the purpose is served and s.12 correction/erasure; the ICO code's data-minimisation and "detrimental use" standards (sources in Key questions 1 and 3).

**Harms specific to young children**
- Expectancy effects were significant only in grades 1–2 in the original Pygmalion experiment. — [Wikipedia, Pygmalion effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pygmalion_effect)
- ASR error is highest at ages 4–7 "due to developing articulation and vocabulary". — [The Learning Agency](https://the-learning-agency.com/guides-resources/closing-the-child-speech-recognition-gap-evidence-limitations-and-paths-forward/)
- Early-childhood development: Dr Dana Suskind (University of Chicago): "AI use will not lead to critical thinking… all of those experiences take human presence, messy interaction and productive struggles"; AI companions are "deferential" and deny children productive conflict; the Moxie robot shutdown caused documented distress; Common Sense Media (2025, children up to 8): 29% had used AI for schoolwork, 24% for stories/art, 10% of 5–8-year-olds had chatted with chatbots; Suskind distinguishes acceptable uses ("robots listening to children read", parental productivity) from replacement of human interaction. — [Hechinger Report, 9 July 2026](https://hechingerreport.org/dont-let-ai-raise-your-kids/)
- Teacher-side harm from unrevised AI output: a randomised trial (Alp Sungu, Wharton; Angela Duckworth) with 193 teachers and 2,800+ students in a Turkish private-school chain, spring 2025: students rated AI-assisted classes "less enjoyable, less interesting and less important"; overall achievement unchanged, but students of weaker teachers declined in exam performance and confidence — Sungu's reading is that weaker teachers used AI material without revision while stronger ones treated it as a first draft. — [Hechinger Report, 13 July 2026](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-ai-in-teaching/)
- The DfE's January 2026 additions name "cognitive deskilling, or long-term developmental harm", "emotional dependence", and "sycophancy and flattery" as harms to design against, and require systems to "only show a full solution after a genuine learner attempt". — [GOV.UK, product safety standards](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-ai-product-safety-standards/generative-ai-product-safety-standards)
- India's own AI Governance Guidelines flag harm "to the long-term mental development and well-being of children" as a priority risk. — [PIB PDF](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc2025115685601.pdf)

### Inferences
- Cornerstone's `child_skill_state` and `class_card` are functionally a "set" if they are shown as a standing verdict about a child. The EEF mechanisms translate directly: the danger is not the number but (a) the teacher's expectation moving, (b) the child staying where the model put them because nobody re-derives it, (c) disadvantaged or non-standard children being mis-read more often (accent, handwriting, language). The design counters are: show *evidence* not *verdict*; make derived states recomputable and expiring (Ring B is TRUNCATE-able, which is the right architecture); force periodic re-derivation; never surface a rank.
- Wisconsin is the closest analogue to a "gate by exception" system that surfaces model labels to teachers: the labels were trusted, un-validated for subgroups, and did no measurable good. Any risk or confidence score at Cornerstone must be validated *before* it is shown and its positive predictive value published to the people who see it.
- Ofqual is the "gate everything" failure in reverse: a technically defensible algorithm applied to individuals via cohort statistics with no individual contestability produced an outcome the public would not accept. A parent-facing report line derived from a model without a per-child evidence trail carries the same risk.
- inBloom's lesson is about *consent architecture*, not data protection law: it was lawful under FERPA and still collapsed. For Cornerstone, "notice and a choice" for anything beyond the core educational purpose, delivered before the fact, is the trust mechanism.
- Speech: teacher voice notes are adult speech, but Pune teachers speak Indian English and code-switch with Marathi/Hindi; vendor ASR error rates are measured mainly on US/UK English. Transcripts must be treated as drafts the teacher confirms, and error rates should be measured per teacher/language, not assumed.

### Gaps
- The 2014 New York Times report on ClassDojo and Williamson's 2017 paper could not be read; ClassDojo evidence is therefore secondary.
- The EEF's own toolkit page was blocked; the mirror is an official partner site with identical content, but figures like "58 studies" were not cross-checked against the EEF technical appendix.
- The Children's Commissioner for England report "Who knows what about me?" (2018) was reached only as a landing page; its often-quoted data-point estimates are **unverified** and not used.
- No study was found (within the tool budget) on error rates of vision models on *young children's handwriting* specifically; this is the harm channel closest to Cornerstone's cell-reading step and is a measurement Cornerstone will have to make itself.

## Key question 5 — Human oversight that works: automation bias and complacency, medical AI second-reader and confidence-triage designs, alert fatigue, the "rubber-stamp" failure mode, and designs that keep a human genuinely deciding

### Takeaway
The human-factors evidence is consistent: people over-rely on imperfect automation (automation bias produces both omission and commission errors), it "occurs in both naive and expert participants, cannot be prevented by training or instructions", and in clinical decision support 6–11% of initially correct decisions are switched to wrong ones when the system is wrong. Explanations make it worse, not better ("explanations increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness"), while cognitive forcing (decide first, delayed advice) reduces over-reliance at the cost of being disliked. Alerting everyone about everything produces 90% override rates. Policies that simply require a human in the loop "provide a false sense of security" and "legitimize" bad algorithms (Green, 41 policies). Yet a *designed* triage works: in the MASAI randomised trial, sending low-score mammograms to one reader and high-score ones to two, with AI marks as decision support, cut screen-reading ~44% while detecting more cancers and reducing interval cancers 12%, and a 2026 meta-analysis of 13 studies found triage "consistently lower[s] radiologist workload and recalls without compromising sensitivity, whereas replacing a second reader may simply shift effort to arbitration". The conditions for that success are the design pattern: prospectively validated thresholds, the human still reading the flagged set fully, calibrated confidence, and outcome monitoring.

### Cited Findings

**Automation bias and complacency**
- Parasuraman & Manzey (Human Factors, 2010): complacency and automation bias are "different manifestations of overlapping automation-induced phenomena, with attention playing a central role"; "automation bias results in making both omission and commission errors when decision aids are imperfect"; complacency arises "under conditions of multiple-task load, when manual tasks compete with the automated task"; "automation complacency is found in both naive and expert participants and cannot be overcome with simple practice"; "automation bias occurs in both naive and expert participants, cannot be prevented by training or instructions". — [TRID record with abstract](https://trid.trb.org/View/1085175)
- Goddard, Roudsari & Wyatt (JAMIA 2012), systematic review of 74 studies (from 13,821 screened): "The proportion of decisions which demonstrated this ranged from 6% to 11% of cases in prospective empirical studies" (correct decisions changed to incorrect after wrong advice); a meta-analysis of four healthcare studies found "erroneous advice was more likely to be followed in the CDSS groups than in the control groups and when in error the CDSS increased the risk of an incorrect decision being made by 26%". Mediators: "trust in the DSS increased reliance", "workload, task complexity, and time constraint". Mitigators: "training and emphasizing user accountability", and design factors — "the position of advice on the screen, updated confidence levels attached to DSS output, and the provision of information versus recommendation". — [PMC, Goddard et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/)
- Bansal et al. (CHI 2021): complementary performance was defined as team accuracy exceeding both human alone and AI alone; "While we observed complementary improvements from AI augmentation, they were not increased by explanations"; "explanations increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness". — [arXiv 2006.14779](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14779)
- Buçinca, Malaya & Gajos (CSCW 2021): three cognitive forcing interventions "significantly reduced overreliance compared to the simple explainable AI approaches", but "people assigned the least favorable subjective ratings to the designs that reduced the overreliance the most", and the benefit accrued more to participants "higher in Need for Cognition". — [Harvard EECS paper page](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2021/bucinca2021trust.shtml)
- Lai et al., survey of "over 100 papers" of empirical human–AI decision-making studies, organised by decision task, AI assistance elements and evaluation metrics. — [arXiv 2112.11471](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.11471) (abstract only)
- Steyvers et al. (PNAS 2022) model human–AI complementarity "while taking into account the unique ways human and algorithmic confidence is expressed" — the value of combination depends on how confidence is calibrated on each side. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1073/pnas.2111547119?fields=title,abstract,year,venue,authors,citationCount)
- Mozannar & Sontag (ICML 2020), "learning to defer": jointly learn "a classifier and a rejector" so the model predicts where it is reliable and defers to the expert where the expert is better. — [arXiv 2006.01862](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01862)
- Tian et al. (2023): "RLHF-LMs produce conditional probabilities that are very poorly calibrated"; asking the model to verbalise confidence outperforms its token probabilities, "often reducing the expected calibration error by a relative 50%" on TriviaQA/SciQ/TruthfulQA — i.e. even the better method leaves substantial miscalibration, and the result is benchmark-specific. — [arXiv 2305.14975](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14975)

**Alert fatigue**
- Meta-analysis of 16 studies: "the overall prevalence of alert override by physicians was 90% (CI95% 85–95%)", with alerts fired on ~13% of prescriptions. — [Felisberto et al. 2024, Health Informatics Journal (Semantic Scholar record)](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1177/14604582241263242?fields=title,abstract,year,venue,authors,citationCount)
- Systematic review of 23 studies on overridden CPOE alerts: override rates 46.2%–96.2% (dose alerts 82–96.8%); overrides judged appropriate 29.4%–100% depending on type (drug–drug interaction 0–95%, geriatric 14.3–57%); recommendation: "alert tiering by severity" — hard stops for the high-risk, low-appropriateness categories, non-interruptive display for the rest. — [PMC, Appropriateness of Overridden Alerts](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7400042/)

**The rubber-stamp critique**
- Ben Green (Computer Law & Security Review, 2022): surveyed 41 policies requiring human oversight of government algorithms (40 in the SSRN draft); two flaws — "evidence suggests that people are unable to perform the desired oversight functions", and the policies "legitimize government uses of faulty and controversial algorithms without addressing the fundamental issues", providing "a false sense of security" and letting "vendors and agencies… shirk accountability"; proposes institutional oversight: agencies must "critically consider whether it is appropriate to use an algorithm at all" and "conduct preliminary evaluations of whether people can effectively oversee the algorithm" with evidence before deployment, followed by public review. — [AI Law Blawg reproduction of the abstract](https://ailawblawg.com/2022/03/16/green-on-the-flaws-of-policies-requiring-human-oversight-of-government-algorithms/); [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.clsr.2022.105681?fields=title,abstract,year,venue,authors,citationCount)
- Co-design study of oversight roles (2025): overseers need to understand their role, see how the AI decides, make a "meaningful contribution" and collaborate; poorly designed oversight work risks "undetected errors with serious consequences" and disengagement, reducing humans "to passive validators"; twelve design considerations built on the SMART work-design model. — [arXiv 2510.19512](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.19512)

**Medical AI: triage and second-reader evidence**
- MASAI (randomised, 105,000+ Swedish women, April 2021–December 2022; final results The Lancet, 30 January 2026; lead author Kristina Lång, Lund): AI risk score triaged low-risk exams to single reading and high-risk to double reading, with AI marks as detection support; interval cancers 1.55 vs 1.76 per 1,000 (−12%); screen-detected share of cancers 81% vs 74%; false positives 1.5% vs 1.4%; 16% fewer invasive, 21% fewer large, 27% fewer aggressive cancers in the AI arm; interim analysis had shown a 44% reduction in screen-reading workload. Caveats: one country, one device, one AI system, experienced radiologists, no ethnicity data. — [ecancer news on the Lancet paper](https://ecancer.org/en/news/27721-ai-supported-mammography-screening-results-in-fewer-aggressive-and-advanced-breast-cancers-finds-full-results-from-first-randomised-controlled-trial)
- Meta-analysis (2026) of 13 studies, ~1.03 million exams, 2017–2024: AI *replacing one reader* (6 studies): detection RR 0.98 (0.92–1.05), recall RR 1.12 (0.90–1.39), "−50%" initial reads but "+270% to +305%" arbitration cases; AI *triage/gate-keeper* (5 studies): detection RR 1.02 (0.98–1.07), recall RR 0.89 (0.82–0.96), "44–70%" fewer initial reads without an arbitration surge; *concurrent overlay* (2 studies): sensitivity RR 1.31 (0.90–1.91), no staffing change. Conclusion: "Triage-based approaches consistently lower radiologist workload and recalls without compromising sensitivity, whereas replacing a second reader may simply shift effort to arbitration"; deployment should use "workflow-aware metrics and prospective threshold validation". — [PMC, AI in breast cancer screening: integration strategies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12818982)

**Statutory encoding of the same lessons**
- EU AI Act Art 14(4)(b): overseers must "remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output". — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Article 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- India AI Governance Guidelines: "Require human oversight and other safeguards to mitigate loss of control risks"; per Chambers' reading, the guidelines also concede that where "direct human oversight is ineffective" (high-speed trading) automated circuit breakers are the right control — i.e. oversight is a means, evaluated for effectiveness, not a ritual. — [PIB PDF](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc2025115685601.pdf); [Chambers](https://chambers.com/articles/meity-unveils-india-s-approach-towards-regulating-artificial-intelligence)

### Inferences
- "Gate everything" as written reproduces the alert-fatigue setting: a teacher confirming hundreds of cell readings a day is the physician facing alerts on 13% of prescriptions — the override/accept rate will drift to ~100% and the 6–11% commission-error rate will pass straight through with a human signature on it. That signature then *legitimises* the error (Green). A gate that everyone clicks is worse than no gate, because it removes the incentive to fix the model and the audit trail says "human approved".
- "Gate by exception" as written reproduces Wisconsin/Tian: it trusts a confidence number that (for RLHF models) is poorly calibrated, and it lets high-confidence errors flow into the record unseen. It is only safe where MASAI's conditions hold: the score has been prospectively validated against ground truth on *this* population, the threshold is set from that data, the flagged set is fully read by a human, a random slice of the unflagged set is also read, and outcomes are monitored.
- The strongest single design lever in the literature is *information versus recommendation* plus *decide-before-you-see* (Goddard; Buçinca): show the teacher the crop of the child's handwriting and ask them to type the digit, then show the model's reading and the diff, for a sampled fraction — rather than showing the model's answer with a green tick. Teachers will dislike it (Buçinca), so budget it (a fixed, small, visible fraction) rather than impose it everywhere.
- The medical analogue of Cornerstone's cheapest safeguard is *code as the second reader*: where an answer can be recomputed (arithmetic, lookup), disagreement between the model's read and the recomputed truth is free disagreement sampling; where it cannot (a hand-written working, a note about a child), the second reader must be a person.
- Accountability is a mitigator only if it is real: each confirm should carry the reviewer's identity, dwell time and what they changed (Art 12's "identification of the natural persons involved in the verification" is the template), and those statistics should be looked at — an accept rate near 100% with sub-second dwell is the signature of a rubber stamp, and should trigger tightening of the queue automatically.

### Gaps
- Goddard's 6–11% and 26% figures come from clinical decision support with physicians; there is no equivalent measurement for teachers reviewing model-read children's work. This is exactly the "preliminary evaluation of whether people can effectively oversee the algorithm" Green calls for, and Cornerstone should run it (a blind gold-set study of teacher acceptance of seeded wrong readings) before choosing thresholds.
- The Lancet Oncology 2023 interim MASAI paper (exact 44.3% / 36,886 fewer readings) could not be opened (403); "44%" is from the ecancer summary of the final paper.
- The AI & Society (2025) review of automation bias mitigations and Alfrink et al.'s "Contestable AI by design" (Minds & Machines 2023) both redirected to a Springer login and could not be read.
- Ofqual's marking-consistency metrics (the noisiness of *human* marking of extended responses, a useful baseline for "how good must the human gate be") were reached only as a landing page; figures are not quoted.

## Key question 6 — Transparency and contestability: what "explainable" means for a teacher and a parent, the right to correction, audit logs, model and prompt versioning, and "glass box" precedents

### Takeaway
The regulators converge on a concrete, non-technical definition: an explanation is *rationale* ("the reasons that led to a decision, delivered in an accessible and non-technical way"), *responsibility* ("who is involved… and who to contact for a human review"), *data* ("what data about them… were used"), *fairness*, *safety and performance*, and *impact* (ICO/Alan Turing Institute), delivered both as process-based and outcome-based explanations; the OECD adds that the affected person must be able to "challenge its output" from "plain and easy-to-understand information on the… factors, processes and/or logic"; the EU AI Act adds provider documentation of accuracy and "performance regarding specific persons or groups", lifetime event logs, deployer log retention ≥ 6 months, and a right to "the main elements of the decision taken"; DPDP gives a right to correction with downstream notification. The educational "glass box" precedent is the open (and negotiated) learner model — the model of the child is shown to the child/teacher, can be disputed, and disputing it improves both accuracy and self-assessment.

### Cited Findings
- ICO / Alan Turing Institute, six explanation types: rationale — "the reasons that led to a decision, delivered in an accessible and non-technical way"; responsibility — "who is involved in the development, management and implementation of an AI system, and who to contact for a human review"; data — "what data about them, and what other sources of data, were used in a particular AI decision"; fairness — "steps taken… to ensure that the decisions it supports are generally unbiased and fair"; safety and performance — "steps taken… to maximise the accuracy, reliability, security and robustness of its decisions"; impact — "how you have considered the effects that your AI decision-support system may have on an individual". Process-based explanations "are about demonstrating that you have followed good governance processes and best practices"; outcome-based ones "are about clarifying the results of a specific decision… in plain, easily understandable language". — [ICO, What goes into an explanation?](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/explaining-decisions-made-with-artificial-intelligence/part-1-the-basics-of-explaining-ai/what-goes-into-an-explanation/)
- OECD 1.3: "plain and easy-to-understand information on the sources of data/input, factors, processes and/or logic that led to the prediction" and enabling "those adversely affected by an AI system to challenge its output"; 1.5: "traceability, including in relation to datasets, processes and decisions made during the AI system lifecycle, to enable analysis of the AI system's outputs and responses to inquiry". — [OECD P7](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/ai-principles/P7); [OECD P9](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/ai-principles/P9)
- EU AI Act: Art 13 — instructions must disclose accuracy, limitations, "performance regarding specific persons or groups" and the "technical measures put in place to facilitate the interpretation of the outputs"; Art 12 — "automatic recording of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system"; Art 26(6) — deployer keeps logs "at least six months"; Art 26(11) — "inform the natural persons that they are subject to the use of the high-risk AI system"; Art 86 — right to "clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision taken"; Art 27 — impact assessment must describe "complaint mechanisms". — [Art 13](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/13/); [Art 12](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/12/); [Art 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/); [Art 86](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/86/); [Art 27](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/27/)
- GDPR Art 22 safeguards: "obtain human intervention", "express his or her point of view", "contest the decision". — [gdpr-info.eu, Art 22](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-22-gdpr/)
- DPDP s.12: correction of "inaccurate or misleading" data, completion, updating, erasure; s.12(3) — notify every recipient of the data and take reasonable steps to have them correct it, and tell the principal who those recipients are. s.8(3): accuracy/completeness/consistency where data is "used to make a decision that affects the Data Principal". Rule 6: access logs retained one year. — [s.12](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-3/section12.html); [s.8](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-2/section8.html); [Rule 6](https://dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule6.html)
- India AI Governance Guidelines: "Understandable by Design: Provide disclosures and explanations that can be understood by the intended user and regulators"; grievance channels "clearly" timed and fed back systematically. — [PIB PDF](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc2025115685601.pdf)
- UK DfE product standards: "recording input prompts and responses"; DfE guidance: the professional who produced a document is responsible for it "regardless of the tools or resources used", and pupils/parents must "understand that their personal data is being processed using AI tools". — [Product safety standards](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-ai-product-safety-standards/generative-ai-product-safety-standards); [GenAI in education](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-artificial-intelligence-in-education/generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-in-education)
- ICO Children's code: privacy information "concise, prominent and in clear language suited to the age of the child"; "provide an obvious sign to the child when they are being monitored"; "Provide prominent and accessible tools to help children exercise their data protection rights and report concerns." — [ICO Code standards](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/code-standards/)
- Open learner models (Bull & Kay, via Bull et al.): five purposes — "Overcome concerns about holding electronic data on learners without their access"; "improve the accuracy of the learner model by allowing user input to correct it"; encourage reflection; support planning; increase trust "by showing information underlying teaching decisions". Negotiated learner models let learners "challenge claims, warrants and backings" through moves of agreement, disagreement, evidence provision and requests for explanation; students in negotiated conditions showed "significantly greater improvement" in self-assessment accuracy than inspection-only groups; samples were small (n = 7–25). — [PMC, Negotiated learner modelling](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6302918/)
- Wisconsin (contrast case): teachers received labels with no explanation of inputs and "minimal training"; the state knew of unfairness and did not disclose it — the absence of responsibility, data and fairness explanations in practice. — [The Markup](https://themarkup.org/machine-learning/2023/04/27/false-alarm-how-wisconsin-uses-race-and-income-to-label-students-high-risk)

### Inferences
- "Transparent" for Cornerstone can be specified as a checklist per artefact (a marked cell, an error classification, a report line, a socio-emotional note): (1) *provenance* — the source capture (sheet id, crop), the model and prompt version that read it, the eval score of that version, the code that verified it, the person who confirmed it and when; (2) *rationale* — one plain sentence a Grade 2 parent can read ("The answer 47 was read from the sheet; the correct answer is 45; the working shows the tens were not exchanged"); (3) *data* — which captures and observations the line rests on, each clickable; (4) *fairness and performance* — the published error rate of that step, disaggregated where it matters (handwriting style, language of the note); (5) *responsibility* — who to ask, and how to dispute; (6) *impact* — what this line changes (a prescription, nothing, a conversation). Items 1, 3 and 5 are Ring A facts the schema already holds; items 2, 4 and 6 are screens.
- Prompt-as-row with version and eval score (Cornerstone's Rule 2) is the Article 13 "instructions for use" for a prompt: it gives every artefact a citable performance figure. The missing piece in most systems is *disaggregated* performance ("specific persons or groups"): the eval gold set must be stratified by the things that vary at Cornerstone (grade, handwriting maturity, teachers' languages).
- Contestability must be a workflow, not a mailbox: a parent's or teacher's dispute creates a new evidence row that supersedes (append-only — Rule 4), is acknowledged with a timeline, triggers re-derivation of Ring B, and, per DPDP s.12(3), propagates to anyone the data was shared with. The open-learner-model literature says the dispute itself has educational value; the socio-emotional part should be disputable by the parent and, at a suitable age, the child.
- A parent should never meet a model's sentence without a human's name next to it. The DfE's rule ("the responsibility of the professional who produced it… regardless of the tools") is the right social contract and is what the confirm queue produces if every confirm is attributed.

### Gaps
- No education-sector regulator has published a worked example of a parent-facing AI explanation for young children; the ICO taxonomy and the open-learner-model literature are the closest.
- Alfrink et al.'s contestability framework could not be read (login redirect); the contestability design above rests on OECD 1.3, GDPR Art 22, EU Art 86, DPDP s.12 and the negotiated-learner-model evidence.
- No source was found on prompt versioning as a governance control specifically; the inference rests on EU Art 12/13 and OECD 1.5 traceability language.

## Council position

Each bullet: the rule or pattern, the confidence the seat attaches to it, and the evidence it rests on.

1. **Treat DPDP as the floor and build to it by 13 May 2027, not later.** Every data element about a child is tagged with the lawful basis it rests on — "educational activity" or "safety" (Fourth Schedule Part A) or a Rule 10 verifiable parental consent; anything not covered by an educational or safety purpose (photos, sharing beyond the school, vendor improvement, any use of pupil work to train a model) needs the consent flow, so build it now. Keep the s.8(3) accuracy duty, s.8(7) erasure, s.12 correction with s.12(3) downstream notification, Rule 6 one-year access logs and processor contracts, and s.9(2) ("no processing likely to cause any detrimental effect on the well-being of a child" — not exemptible) as named acceptance criteria. *Confidence: high on the rules; medium on exact wording until the Gazette text is checked (the MeitY site was unreachable, a mirror was used).* — [s.9](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/98869575/); [Fourth Schedule](https://dpdpa.com/schedule/schedule4.html); [Rule 1](https://dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule1.html); [Rule 10](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule10.html); [s.8](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-2/section8.html); [s.12](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-3/section12.html); [Rule 6](https://dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule6.html)

2. **No model infers a child's emotions, temperament or "risk"; socio-emotional observations are teacher-authored, model-transcribed at most.** The EU prohibits AI emotion inference in education institutions outright (Art 5(1)(f), in force since 2 February 2025); DPDP calls the same thing "behavioural monitoring", permitted for a school only as an educational activity or safety measure and never if detrimental; the ICO code says profiling is off by default for children. A model may clean up a voice note; it may not be asked what the child felt. *Confidence: high.* — [Art 5](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/); [ICO code](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/code-standards/)

3. **Adopt the EU AI Act's high-risk duties voluntarily as the definition of "not a black box".** Cornerstone's marking-to-prescription chain is Annex III 3(b) ("evaluate learning outcomes… used to steer the learning process"). Write Articles 12 (lifetime event logs), 13 (documented accuracy and limitations, disaggregated by group), 14 (oversight designed against automation bias, with override and stop), 26 (trained, empowered, named overseers; logs ≥ 6 months; tell the people affected) and 86 (explanation of "the main elements of the decision") into `workflows.json`-level acceptance tests. The Annex III obligations themselves apply in the EU from 2 December 2027 (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744), so this is ahead of the global bar, not behind it. *Confidence: high on content; medium on the omnibus regulation number, confirmed by two secondary pages but not EUR-Lex.* — [Annex III](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/); [Art 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/); [Art 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/); [Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)

4. **Decide the gate by decision class, and write the classes down.** Class A — *code-verifiable, reversible, low-stakes* (a digit read from a cell whose correct value code recomputes; a QR lookup): gate by exception. Class B — *judgement-laden or record-forming* (error-to-misconception classification, anything that changes `child_skill_state`, a transcribed observation, a report line): gate everything, with the human entering or choosing, not ticking. Class C — *irreversible or child/parent-facing* (a report sent home, a change to a child's placement, a socio-emotional note): gate everything plus a second person (coordinator), mirroring Art 14(5)'s two-person rule for the highest-stakes decisions. The DPDP s.8(3) accuracy duty attaches wherever data is "used to make a decision that affects" the child — Classes B and C. *Confidence: medium-high.* — [Art 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/); [s.8](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-2/section8.html)

5. **Gate-by-exception is permitted only where confidence has been measured on Cornerstone's own children, and the threshold set from that measurement.** RLHF-model probabilities are "very poorly calibrated"; verbalised confidence is better but still leaves half the error. So: no exception gate until `engine eval` for that prompt version reports calibration (predicted vs observed accuracy by confidence bin) on a gold set stratified by grade, handwriting maturity and language; thresholds re-validated on every prompt version; the exception rate itself a governed number reviewed monthly. This is the breast-screening lesson — triage works with "prospective threshold validation", and Wisconsin is what happens without it. *Confidence: high.* — [Tian et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14975); [Meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12818982); [The Markup](https://themarkup.org/machine-learning/2023/04/27/false-alarm-how-wisconsin-uses-race-and-income-to-label-students-high-risk)

6. **Design the confirm queue against automation bias, not merely for throughput.** Concretely: (a) confidence-sorted, lowest first, with the model's answer *hidden until the teacher has entered theirs* for a fixed, visible sample (cognitive forcing: decide-then-compare); (b) a random slice of high-confidence items injected blind into the same queue every day (spot-check of the confident); (c) every disagreement between model and code, or between two independent reads, routed to a person (disagreement sampling); (d) "information, not recommendation" where possible — show the crop and the recomputed truth, not a green tick; (e) a review-rate budget per teacher per day, sized so accept-rates stay well below the 90% override regime of clinical alerting, with tiering so only Class B/C items interrupt. Expect teachers to prefer the lazier design; the evidence says the effortful one is the one that works, so make its cost small and visible rather than removing it. *Confidence: medium-high (strong lab and clinical evidence; no classroom replication).* — [Goddard et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/); [Buçinca et al.](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2021/bucinca2021trust.shtml); [Bansal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14779); [Felisberto et al.](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1177/14604582241263242?fields=title,abstract,year,venue,authors,citationCount); [PMC override review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7400042/)

7. **Measure the gate, or it is a rubber stamp.** Every confirm records who, when, dwell time, what changed (Art 12's "identification of the natural persons involved in the verification" is the template). Publish per-teacher and per-step accept rates and blind-gold agreement. An accept rate near 100% with sub-second dwell tightens the queue automatically (more blind seeds, more decide-then-compare). Before relying on any gate, run Green's "preliminary evaluation of whether people can effectively oversee the algorithm": seed known-wrong readings into a teacher's queue and measure how many pass. *Confidence: high.* — [Green](https://ailawblawg.com/2022/03/16/green-on-the-flaws-of-policies-requiring-human-oversight-of-government-algorithms/); [Art 12](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/12/); [Parasuraman & Manzey](https://trid.trb.org/View/1085175)

8. **No standing labels; evidence, not verdicts.** Derived child states are recomputable, expire, and are shown as the evidence behind them (the sheets, the cells, the notes) rather than as a level; no rank, set or "risk" score is displayed to any teacher or parent unless its positive predictive value on Cornerstone's own children has been measured and is shown beside it; forced periodic re-derivation so a child is never "where the model put them" by default. This is the EEF mechanism list (teacher expectations, misallocation, rigidity, self-fulfilling prophecy) turned into constraints, and it is also the s.9(2) "detrimental effect" test in practice. *Confidence: medium-high.* — [EEF/QRF](https://qrf.org/en/teaching-and-learning-toolkit/setting-and-streaming); [Pygmalion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pygmalion_effect); [The Markup](https://themarkup.org/machine-learning/2023/04/27/false-alarm-how-wisconsin-uses-race-and-income-to-label-students-high-risk)

9. **Transparency means six things on every artefact, in plain words, with a name.** Provenance (capture, model + prompt version + its eval score, verifying code, confirming person); rationale in one parent-readable sentence; the data it rests on, clickable; the step's measured error rate, disaggregated; who is responsible and how to dispute; what the line changes. The prompt row with version and eval score is the Article 13 "instructions for use"; the parent never sees a model's sentence without a teacher's name beside it ("the responsibility of the professional who produced it… regardless of the tools"). *Confidence: high.* — [ICO explanation types](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/explaining-decisions-made-with-artificial-intelligence/part-1-the-basics-of-explaining-ai/what-goes-into-an-explanation/); [OECD 1.3](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/ai-principles/P7); [Art 13](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/13/); [DfE](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-artificial-intelligence-in-education/generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-in-education)

10. **Contestability is a workflow with a clock.** A parent's or teacher's dispute creates a superseding evidence row (append-only), is acknowledged with a stated timeline, triggers re-derivation, and propagates to anyone the data was shared with (s.12(3)); the teacher's view of the child is an open learner model — inspectable and disputable — and the negotiated-model evidence says the act of disputing improves accuracy and the learner's self-assessment. *Confidence: medium-high.* — [s.12](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-3/section12.html); [OECD 1.3](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/ai-principles/P7); [Art 86](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/86/); [Open learner models](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6302918/)

11. **Minimise, pseudonymise, expire, and ask before going beyond the core purpose — the inBloom rule.** Names stay in `pii`; models receive tokens and crops, never names or faces; no pupil data trains a vendor model; retention schedules and erasure on leaving; children's voices are not recorded without an educational purpose, a limit and a parent-facing explanation (voiceprints are now biometric personal information under COPPA); anything beyond the educational/safety purpose gets notice and a choice before the fact. inBloom was lawful and still collapsed because parents were neither told nor asked. *Confidence: high.* — [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/technology/inbloom-to-shut-down-amid-growing-data-privacy-concerns/2014/04); [Parent Coalition](https://studentprivacymatters.org/background-of-inbloom/); [Latham & Watkins on COPPA](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule); [ICO code](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/code-standards/)

12. **Verdict on the two proposals: neither, as written; gate by decision class.** "Gate everything" fails on the evidence about attention: under load, confirmation becomes a 90%-override ritual that passes the 6–11% of model errors through with a human signature and legitimises them. "Gate by exception" fails on the evidence about confidence: it trusts a number that is poorly calibrated and lets confident errors form the record unseen — Wisconsin's labels, Ofqual's grades. The defensible design is: exception-gating for code-verifiable, reversible, low-stakes reads *once calibration is measured on Cornerstone's own children*, with a standing blind audit sample and a monthly-reviewed exception rate; full, forced-choice gating for anything judgement-laden or record-forming; two people for anything that reaches a parent or moves a child. Measure the gate as hard as the model, and treat a near-100% accept rate as a defect, not a success. The precedent that this hybrid is safe is a 105,000-woman randomised trial in which validated triage reduced human reading ~44% while catching more disease; the precedent that skipping the validation is unsafe is a state-wide school system that labelled 32,000 children with a tool that was wrong three times in four. *Confidence: medium-high — the human-factors and medical evidence is strong and consistent; its transfer to teachers reading young children's work has not been measured, which is why bullets 5 and 7 make Cornerstone measure it.* — [ecancer/MASAI](https://ecancer.org/en/news/27721-ai-supported-mammography-screening-results-in-fewer-aggressive-and-advanced-breast-cancers-finds-full-results-from-first-randomised-controlled-trial); [Meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12818982); [Goddard et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/); [Green](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.clsr.2022.105681?fields=title,abstract,year,venue,authors,citationCount); [The Markup](https://themarkup.org/machine-learning/2023/04/27/false-alarm-how-wisconsin-uses-race-and-income-to-label-students-high-risk); [Ofqual 2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_Kingdom_school_exam_grading_controversy)
