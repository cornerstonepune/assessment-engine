# Assessment and measurement products and methods for school-age children: what Cornerstone should borrow and avoid

Seat: assessment and measurement, School operating system council. Written 26 September 2026. Every URL below was opened during this research (web fetch, or download plus text extraction); where a vendor page or a paywalled journal could not be opened, the claim is marked as such or moved to Gaps. Numbers a source did not itself state are marked "unverified". Focus is ages 3–10 (Nursery to Grade 4), with notes on what changes for older grades.

Method notes for the report writer: the EEF website, Gradescope's help centre, Wiley, SAGE and Springer article pages returned 403s; for those, abstracts were obtained through the ERIC record, the CrossRef API or the Semantic Scholar API (record URLs given). Several technical manuals were downloaded as PDFs and text-extracted; page-level quotes come from those extractions. The PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan 2024 national report is a scanned PDF with almost no extractable text, so its headline numbers are taken from PARAKH's own web pages and clearly-labelled secondary summaries.

## Key question 1 — Interim/adaptive assessments (MAP Growth, Star, i-Ready, mCLASS/DIBELS/Acadience, ACER PAT, e-asTTle, Cambridge CEM): what they give a teacher, and the documented criticisms

### Takeaway
The big interim products all rest on the same machinery — a Rasch/IRT vertical scale, computer-adaptive item selection, three-to-four administrations a year, and norm-referenced growth (percentiles or student growth percentiles) — and their reliability at the whole-test level is excellent (0.90–0.98). Their weakness for a school like Cornerstone is exactly where the school's own prototype is strong: sub-scores for a single skill are much less reliable (down to 0.75 in MAP Growth's instructional areas), tests take 25–60 minutes of a young child's attention per subject, kindergarten classification accuracy and validity evidence are consistently weaker than for older grades, and none of them tells a teacher *which mistake* a child made.

### Cited findings

**NWEA MAP Growth (USA; K–12)**
- Scale and adoption: MAP Growth is a computer-adaptive assessment in mathematics, reading, language usage and science, marketed as serving "13+ million students" in "35900+ schools" and "4500+ districts" — [NWEA MAP Growth product page](https://www.nwea.org/map-growth/). The 2025 technical report says "the NWEA test delivery platform supports nearly 77 million test events each year" — [MAP Growth Technical Report for 2024–2025 (PDF, April 2026)](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/MAP-Growth-Technical-Report-2025.pdf).
- The RIT scale runs from about 100 to 350, is claimed to be equal-interval ("a 10-point increase from 120 to 130 represents the same learning growth as an increase from 210 to 220"), is grade-independent, and a student's RIT is "the difficulty level of questions that [the student] answers correctly about 50% of the time" — [NWEA blog, 4 Feb 2025](https://www.nwea.org/blog/2025/how-the-map-growth-rit-scale-offers-valuable-insights-into-student-growth/).
- Test design: items are calibrated with the Rasch model; tests are "grade banded (e.g., K–2, 2–5, 6+)" on "a common vertical scale in each" subject; "MAP for Primary Grades was renamed MAP Growth K–2" — [2025 technical report](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/MAP-Growth-Technical-Report-2025.pdf).
- Testing time: "MAP Growth 2–12 assessments are untimed and take approximately 45–60 minutes per content area. MAP Growth K–2 assessments are also untimed, and students typically take 25–40 minutes per content area. MAP Growth can be administered up to four times a year (fall, winter, and spring, with an optional fourth …)" — [2025 technical report](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/MAP-Growth-Technical-Report-2025.pdf).
- Reliability: marginal reliabilities for Math and Reading were "generally mid-to-high 0.90 across most grades, with lower values in kindergarten (e.g., Math: 0.92–0.94; Reading: 0.90–0.94) and higher values in upper grades (often 0.97–0.98)". For instructional-area (sub-domain) scores "the average marginal reliability was between 0.86 and 0.91. Even the lowest observed marginal reliability estimates were 0.75 or higher. The lower reliability estimates were seen for instructional areas with small numbers of items." — [2025 technical report, §7.6.1](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/MAP-Growth-Technical-Report-2025.pdf).
- Precision: "A MAP Growth test event is designed to have a target CSEM of about 3.5 RIT points"; observed CSEMs for K–3 Math were 3.2–3.4 RIT across score deciles — [2025 technical report, §7.5.1, Table 7.5](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/MAP-Growth-Technical-Report-2025.pdf).
- Norms: "Data used to produce the 2025 MAP Growth national norms were sampled from 116 million scores of 13.8 million students across 30,000 schools spanning 6 testing terms from fall 2022 to spring 2024" — [2025 technical report](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/MAP-Growth-Technical-Report-2025.pdf).
- Accommodations for young/emergent readers exist as designated features: text-to-speech ("audio support, spoken audio") and a "Human reader (human read aloud, read aloud)" — [2025 technical report, Tables 9.5–9.6](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/MAP-Growth-Technical-Report-2025.pdf).

**Renaissance Star Math (USA/UK; K–12)**
- Design: "the Star Math test administers 34 items of varying difficulty based on the student's responses"; the second-generation item bank "grew to over 6,000 items"; items are "field tested and calibrated to estimate their Rasch difficulty parameters"; the test "takes an average of less than 25 minutes"; kindergarteners (n = 88,566 in 2018–19) averaged 13.24 minutes — [Star Assessments for Math Technical Manual – Florida (PDF)](https://flfast.org/content/contentresources/en/SMRPTechnicalManual_FL.pdf).
- Reliability, Table 11 (2018–19, Unified scale): generic reliability 0.90 at K, 0.89 at grade 1, rising to 0.94 at grade 12, 0.98 overall; but *alternate-form* reliability (two administrations ~90–100 days apart) is 0.71 at K, 0.75 at grade 1, 0.80 at grade 2, 0.82–0.85 in grades 3–12 — [Star Math technical manual, Table 11](https://flfast.org/content/contentresources/en/SMRPTechnicalManual_FL.pdf).
- Growth reporting: "The growth model used by Star Assessments is Student Growth Percentile (SGP; Betebenner, 2009)… a norm-referenced quantification of individual student growth derived using quantile regression" — [Star Math technical manual](https://flfast.org/content/contentresources/en/SMRPTechnicalManual_FL.pdf). The Unified Score Scale, introduced 2017–18, links Star Math to the Star Reading Rasch scale so both report on one scale — same source.
- Independent review: the US National Center on Intensive Intervention rates Star Math's reliability "Convincing evidence" for grades 1–11 (internal consistency, n = 131,103), administration "20 minutes per student", item bank "more than 6,000 items", but "Data unavailable" for validity of slope (i.e., whether growth on the test tracks real growth) — [NCII progress-monitoring tool chart: Star Math](https://charts.intensiveintervention.org/progressmonitoring/tool/?id=549288c34c6b39c5).
- Vendor page: grades K–12, "approximately 20–30 minutes", domains Numbers and Operations, Algebra, Geometry and Measurement, Data Analysis — [Renaissance Star Math](https://www.renaissance.com/products/assessment/star-assessments/star-math/).

**Curriculum Associates i-Ready Diagnostic (now "i-Ready Inform"; USA; K–12)**
- Design: "Computer adaptive testing and the Rasch Item Response Theory model form a strong foundation"; "Students receive 54–72 items per subject • Students typically take 30–60 minutes per subject… with grades K–3 tending towards the shorter end of the range"; scores are reported as scale scores ("a single continuum"), placement levels ("what grade level of skills to focus on") and norms; the sample report shows a standard error of ±15.5 scale-score points — [The Science Behind i-Ready's Adaptive Diagnostic (SETDA-hosted PDF, 2014)](https://www.setda.org/ls2013/wp-content/uploads/sites/8/2014/12/Adaptive-Diagnostic-Science-SETDA.pdf).
- Adoption and teacher outputs: "more than 14 million students"; grades K–12; teachers get placement data, "Grouping recommendations", and two growth targets, "Typical Growth" and "Stretch Growth" — [i-Ready Inform product page](https://www.curriculumassociates.com/programs/i-ready-assessment/diagnostic).
- Independent review: NCII rates classification accuracy for kindergarten only "Partially convincing" (AUC 0.75 fall, 0.80 spring) versus "Convincing" for grades 1–8 (AUC 0.87–0.95); validity is "Partially convincing" for K–2 and "Convincing" for 3–8; 45 minutes per student; $6.00 per student per year — [NCII screening tool chart: i-Ready Diagnostic](https://charts.intensiveintervention.org/screening/tool/?id=7534542c4f422f85).

**Amplify mCLASS / DIBELS 8th Edition / Acadience (USA; K–8 literacy, one-minute probes)**
- What it is: mCLASS is "the only digital provider of DIBELS 8th edition assessments", using "one-minute measures", teacher-administered by direct observation; teachers receive "Data-driven instructional recommendations", skill-based groups and "Over 500 evidence-based activities" — [Amplify mCLASS](https://amplify.com/programs/mclass/).
- Administration (California DoE description): recommended for K–2; one-on-one; required K–1 measures are Phonemic Segmentation Fluency, Letter Naming Fluency, Nonsense Word Fluency and Word Reading Fluency; kindergarten takes "up to 4 minutes (DIBELS)"; licences "include three benchmark screenings" a year; scoring is automatic — [CDE mCLASS overview](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ci/cl/mclassinfooverview.asp).
- Reliability: the technical manual examined five kinds of reliability. Concurrent alternate-form reliability for PSF was "in every case .80 or above" (median .86 in kindergarten). But *delayed* alternate-form reliability is much weaker for the youngest measures: "PSF median correlations were .49 in kindergarten, .54 in first grade"; LNF .81 in K and .76 in grade 1; NWF-CLS .71 in K to .83 in grade 2 — [DIBELS 8th Edition Technical Manual (Univ. of Oregon, 2020, PDF)](https://dibels.uoregon.edu/sites/default/files/DIBELS8-TechnicalManual_04152020.pdf).
- Cut points are chosen "to maximize sensitivity while maintaining specificity at or above .80" — same manual. The 2020 supplement added "data collected in 2019-2020 on nearly one million additional students" from the DIBELS Data System and mCLASS — [DIBELS 8 Technical Manual Supplement (PDF)](https://dibels.amplify.com/docs/techreports/UO_Dibels8_Technical_Manual_Supplement%20072020.pdf).
- Documented criticism: in 1,518 urban first-graders, DIBELS Oral Reading Fluency predicted later comprehension better than the other subtests, "including a retell fluency task designed to measure comprehension", and vocabulary explained why fluent readers could still comprehend poorly; the article "is followed by a commentary from S. Jay Samuels entitled 'The DIBELS Tests: Is Speed of Barking at Print What We Mean by Reading Fluency?'" — [Riedel 2007, Reading Research Quarterly (CrossRef record)](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1598/RRQ.42.4.5).
- Acadience Reading K–6 (same lineage) is described only generically as "standardized", "reliable and valid" and "fast to administer" on its product page; no numbers are given there — [Acadience Reading K–6](https://acadiencelearning.org/acadience-reading/k-grade6/).

**ACER Progressive Achievement Tests (Australia; primary–secondary)**
- "PAT tests within a learning area are all equated onto a common scale using Rasch scaling"; scales are interval; but "a Reading score cannot be compared to a Maths score, as 'the units of the scale will have different meanings for each scale'"; "scales are divided into achievement bands that include written descriptions of what students are typically able to do at that band", which teachers can use "to group students of similar abilities" — [ACER: Scale scores and achievement bands](https://schoolsupport.acer.org/hc/en-au/articles/22255011145241-Scale-scores-and-achievement-bands).
- Interpretation guide: "Based on analysis of the data using the Rasch model, this scale enables student achievement and question difficulties to be located on the same scale"; norm tables give scale score, percentile rank and stanine "and the margin of error for each scale score"; "Error margins are larger for very high and very low scores"; "each PAT assessment has a unique scale" — [Interpreting ACER Test Results (PDF)](https://www.acer.org/files/PATM-Interpreting-Scores.pdf).
- Philosophy: "A student who receives a 'D' grade every year may believe they are making no progress at all when, in reality, they may be progressing as much as a student who consistently receives an 'A' grade"; conventional and adaptive PAT "use the same scales" — [ACER progressive achievement](https://www.acer.org/au/pat/progressive-achievement). "Two students achieving approximately the same scale score… have demonstrated similar ability and understanding, regardless of the year level or the test items" — [ACER: What are Progressive Achievement scales?](https://schoolsupport.acer.org/hc/en-au/articles/28696551910169-What-are-Progressive-Achievement-scales).

**New Zealand e-asTTle**
- "e-asTTle uses the 'Rasch model' when testing in Reading and Mathematics"; it reports curriculum sub-levels because "a curriculum level score alone did not show progress within a level. Three sublevels for each curriculum level were developed – Basic, Proficient and Advanced" (e.g., 3B, 3P, 3A; only writing uses level 1, other subjects report <2B); "the high and low scores on any given test have the highest SEM"; teachers read an Individual Learning Pathway with measurement error shown as ± next to the score; the example shows results tracked "from Years 4 to 10" — [e-asTTle workshop and support booklet 2024–25 (PDF)](https://www.datocms-assets.com/56414/1726795170-e-asttle-support-booklet-24-25.pdf).

**Cambridge CEM (UK/international; ages 4–11)**
- BASE (ages 4–5) "takes just 20 minutes", is "adaptive", is delivered in "one-to-one sessions" and covers literacy, numeracy, communication, language and personal/social/emotional development — [Cambridge Insight primary assessments](https://www.cem.org/primary-assessments).
- Cambridge Primary Insight (ages 5–11) replaced InCAS; five adaptive modules (reading, spelling, mathematics, arithmetic, developed ability) of "approximately 20 minutes per module", "student-led", "no marking required", with statements comparing the child "to other learners of the same age" — [Cambridge Primary Insight](https://www.cem.org/primary-insight).

**Validity for very young children (cross-cutting)**
- BERA's expert panel (Goldstein, Moss, Sammons, Sinnott, Stobart, 4 July 2018) on England's Reception Baseline: "just a few month's difference in age in the early years produces pronounced developmental differences"; value-added calculations built on it "will be highly unreliable"; it would "produce results with little predictive power and dubious validity"; and "assessment of very young children is hard to justify when it is not being used to support a child's learning" — [BERA, A baseline without basis](https://www.bera.ac.uk/publication/a-baseline-without-basis).
- England nonetheless made the RBA statutory: it has been mandatory "since 2021", administered "within the first 6 weeks of starting reception", in early mathematics and early literacy/communication/language, with pupils responding by touchscreen, verbally and with manipulatives; its stated purpose is "school-level progress measures", first to be published in summer 2028 — [GOV.UK Reception baseline assessment guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/reception-baseline-assessment).

### Inferences
- Whole-test reliability of 0.90+ is achieved with 34–72 adaptive items per subject; the moment a product reports a *sub-skill*, reliability falls to the 0.75–0.91 range (MAP) — the same statistical fact Cornerstone will meet when it reports a single skill from one week's worksheet. The products handle it by reporting sub-scores as indicative bands, not as mastery verdicts.
- The one-minute, one-on-one probe (DIBELS/mCLASS, BASE) is the format the field converged on for ages 4–7, not the 30–60-minute screen test. But even the probes are unstable over weeks at that age (PSF delayed alternate-form 0.49–0.54), which argues for repeated short measures rather than any single reading.
- Growth in every product is reported normatively (percentiles, SGPs) against a large national sample; no product tries to report growth across subjects on one scale, and ACER says explicitly that reading and maths units are not comparable.
- "Teaching to the test" is asserted in the criticism literature but I found no direct causal study for interim assessments in this research (see Gaps); the Samuels/Riedel exchange shows the milder documented risk — that a speed-of-decoding probe gets treated as if it measured reading.

### Gaps
- No vendor page opened gave the recommended i-Ready testing frequency (widely described as three times a year; unverified here).
- The RAND-style or WWC-style evidence that MAP/Star/i-Ready *use* improves learning (as opposed to measuring it reliably) was not located; NCII rates the instruments, not their instructional effect.
- Direct quantitative evidence on "teaching to the test" caused by interim assessments was not found in the sources opened.
- Cambridge CEM publishes no reliability numbers on the pages opened; ACER's reliability figures live in per-test teacher manuals that were not opened.
- Amplify does not state mCLASS user counts on the page opened.

## Key question 2 — Diagnostic and misconception-based assessment (Eedi/Diagnostic Questions, ASSISTments, MATHia, ALEKS, Mindspark/ASSET, ASER): how they tag misconceptions, what they do with a wrong answer, how reliable the diagnoses are

### Takeaway
Two very different traditions exist. The *misconception* tradition (Eedi/Diagnostic Questions, Educational Initiatives' Mindspark) attaches a named error to each wrong option and now has plausible causal evidence (Mindspark 0.37σ in 4.5 months in an AER randomised trial; Eedi d = 0.30 at 24 months in a smaller independent RCT), but its diagnoses are known to be noisy — students with the right answer can hold the misconception, distractor-to-misconception tagging is hard enough to have needed a 2,587-label Kaggle competition, and confidence data show wrong answers held with high confidence are a different beast from guesses. The *mastery-modelling* tradition (ALEKS, MATHia/Cognitive Tutor, ASSISTments) treats a wrong answer as evidence against a knowledge state, not as a labelled misconception, and its field data show teachers overriding the software's mastery judgements in ~15% of sections, with worse outcomes when they do.

### Cited findings

**Eedi / Diagnostic Questions (UK; ages ~6–16)**
- Definition: Eedi diagnostic questions are "multiple-choice questions designed in a way that each incorrect answer reveals a specific misconception" (citing Wylie & Wiliam 2006); the help centre also flags the risk, citing Marsh et al. (2007), that distractors "may become integrated into subjects' more general knowledge", while arguing "misconceptions usually exist before seeing the multiple choice question" — [Eedi: The research behind Eedi](https://help.eedi.co.uk/en/articles/7234087-the-research-behind-eedi).
- What the teacher sees: before assigning, historical bar charts from "thousands of students" show which wrong options are common; after a quiz, a results table shows per-question performance with indicators of whether misconceptions were "resolved" or "remain unresolved"; clicking an answer opens "the question and misconception explanations" — [Eedi: Understanding student misconceptions](https://help.eedi.co.uk/en/articles/6212339-understanding-student-misconceptions).
- Scale of data: the NeurIPS 2020 Education Challenge released Eedi data of "27613 questions, 118971 students, 15867850 answers" (tasks 1–2) plus "948 questions, 4918 students, 1382727 answers" (tasks 3–4), i.e., over 17 million answer records; each question has "four answers, exactly one of which is correct and where each of the three incorrect answers is chosen to highlight a common misconception"; the authors note that when teachers write items "these misconceptions are not labelled or linked between questions" — [Wang et al. 2020, arXiv:2007.12061 (PDF)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.12061).
- Misconception catalogue: in 2026 Eedi released a "Misconception Graph" of "more than 8,000 common maths misconceptions, informed by 200 million real student responses", under CC BY 4.0 via Learning Commons/Hugging Face, with "every wrong option [is] tied to a specific misconception" — [Eedi news: misconception graph is now a public good](https://www.eedi.com/news/eedis-misconception-graph-is-now-a-public-good). The free Diagnostic Questions bank holds "25,000 questions and quizzes" across maths, computing and science — [diagnosticquestions.com](https://diagnosticquestions.com/).
- How hard tagging is: the 2024 Kaggle competition "Eedi – Mining Misconceptions in Mathematics" asked models to map each distractor to one of "2,587 fine-grained math misconceptions"; a silver-medal (top-5%) solution scored MAP@25 of about 0.50 on the private leaderboard — [third-party solution write-up on GitHub](https://github.com/DaoyuanLi2816/Kaggle-Eedi-Mining-Misconceptions-in-Mathematics-Silver-Medal) (secondary source; the Kaggle page itself did not render).
- Confidence and hypercorrection: analysing 219,826 responses from 7,302 UK students aged 6–16 who rated their confidence on a 5-point scale, Foster, Woodhead, Barton and Clark-Wilson found confidence correlated with facility, boys more confident than girls, disadvantaged students less confident "even after accounting for facility", confidence lower for older students, and — because the platform re-presents "an analogous question about 3 weeks later" after an error — "the first evidence in an authentic school mathematics context for the hypercorrection effect", i.e., "errors made with higher confidence are more likely to be corrected" — [Foster et al. 2021, Educational Studies in Mathematics (Semantic Scholar record)](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/s10649-021-10084-7?fields=title,year,venue,authors,abstract,citationCount).
- The "correct answer trap": on 20,964 real Eedi responses, "automated feedback systems that rely on answer correctness will reinforce, rather than address, misconceptions when students reach the correct answer through flawed reasoning"; fine-tuned classifiers detected 57% and an open-weight reasoning model 84% of such cases, but "at realistic prevalence, false alarms outnumber genuine detections approximately 8:1"; the authors propose a detect–verify–escalate pipeline routing uncertain cases to follow-up diagnostic questions — [Imran & Bulathwela, arXiv:2606.23205 (June 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23205). The companion AIED'26 paper reports 71% of detection failures concentrated in two question types and "approximately four false positives for every genuine detection" — [Imran & Bulathwela, arXiv:2605.23925](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23925). (The two papers give different false-alarm ratios; both are reported here.)
- Causal evidence: an independent two-year RCT by WhatWorked Education (UK) started with ~3,450 pupils in 20 schools and ended with 9 schools and 1,192 pupils (Years 7–8), outcome measured on an NWEA standardised maths assessment: Cohen's d = 0.46 at 18 months and 0.30 at 24 months (p = 0.004, "approximately four months additional progress"); caveats: control schools mostly used another platform (MyMaths), attrition was selective on prior attainment, and control schools dropped out more (81% vs 62.5%) — [Eedi news: two-year RCT earns gold efficacy certificate from EduEvidence](https://www.eedi.com/news/two-year-rct-earns-eedi-a-gold-efficacy-certificate-from-eduevidence). Eedi's papers page also lists "A pilot impact study to evaluate the effectiveness of Eedi on raising attainment in mathematics at KS3" (Harrison, Brown, Higgins, 2023) under "Education Endowment Foundation Evaluation" — [Eedi research papers](https://www.eedi.com/papers) — but the EEF report itself could not be opened (see Gaps).
- Eedi's 2026 positioning: a "Diagnostic Engine" for third-party tools, "DQR" (a low-tech card-scanning quiz tool) and "Eedi Tutor"; the company claims use "across four continents" — [eedi.com](https://www.eedi.com/).

**ASSISTments (USA; grades 4–12, evaluated in grade 7)**
- Design: a web homework platform giving "immediate feedback and hints to students during homework time while simultaneously providing individualized feedback to students' teachers", with "Skillbuilders" for mastery-based practice; the IES award describes "cognitive diagnostic reports" for teachers — [Blueprints program page](https://www.blueprintsprograms.org/programs/1261999999/assistments/); [IES award R305A120125](https://nces.ed.gov/use-work/awards/efficacy-study-online-mathematics-homework-support-evaluation-assistments-formative-assessment-and).
- Evidence: Maine cluster RCT, grade 7, effect size g = 0.18 (t(20) = 2.992, p = 0.007) on the end-of-year state test — [SRI: How big is that?](https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/digital-learning-pubs/how-big-is-that-reporting-the-effect-size-and-cost-of-assistments-in-the-maine-homework-efficacy-study/). Blueprints reports 46 schools/3,035 students in Maine (0.18 overall, 0.29 for low-achievers) and a North Carolina replication of 63 schools/5,991 students with 0.10 one year later; WWC rating "Meets Standards Without Reservations – Positive Effect"; the platform is free, training $2,000 per 30 participants — [Blueprints](https://www.blueprintsprograms.org/programs/1261999999/assistments/). IES lists the analytic sample as 43 schools and 2,850 students — [IES](https://nces.ed.gov/use-work/awards/efficacy-study-online-mathematics-homework-support-evaluation-assistments-formative-assessment-and) (the two counts differ; likely randomised vs analytic samples).

**Carnegie Learning MATHia / Cognitive Tutor (USA; middle and high school)**
- RAND RCT: "147 school sites: 73 high schools and 74 middle schools in 51 school districts in seven states"; "Nearly 18,700 students in grades 9–12… Nearly 6,800 students in grades 6–8"; no significant effect in year 1; in year 2 at high school "approximately 0.20 standardized effect sizes — is equivalent to moving a student from the 50th percentile on the algebra posttest to the 58th percentile"; middle-school effects not significant — [RAND research brief RB-9746 (2013)](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_briefs/RB9746.html). A commentary stresses that the average "obscures variation" and that mechanisms were unclear — [Christensen Institute](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/blog/carnegies-randomized-controlled-study-take-a-victory-lap-then-continue-the-research-cycle/).
- Mastery in practice (log data from 18 treatment schools, 4,460 students): "about 85% of worked sections are mastered… about 13–15% of sections end in promotion" (the software moving a student on without mastery) and a few percent in teacher "reassignment"; the authors find "reassignment appears to lower posttest scores — a finding that is fairly resilient to confounding from omitted covariates" — [Israni et al., arXiv:1802.08616 (PDF)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.08616).

**ALEKS (USA; knowledge space theory)**
- Theory: knowledge is modelled as discrete "knowledge states"; for algebra "a domain of approximately 350 basic concepts, giving rise to a structure of millions of empirically feasible knowledge states"; an adaptive assessment "can gauge a student's knowledge state in a relatively small number of questions (approximately 25–30)" — [ALEKS: Knowledge Space Theory](https://www.aleks.com/about_aleks/knowledge_space_theory).
- What a wrong answer does: each response updates likelihoods over states; questioning stops when entropy is low (the worked example "took 24 questions which is close to the average for Arithmetic"); the "outer fringe" of the inferred state — problems that could be added to form another state — is "where progress is taking place", i.e., what the student is ready to learn; validity is monitored by an extra probe problem: "the correlation between predicted and observed answers hovers between .7 and .8" — [Falmagne, The Assessment of Knowledge, in Theory and in Practice (PDF)](https://www.aleks.com/about_aleks/Science_Behind_ALEKS.pdf).

**Educational Initiatives: Mindspark and ASSET (India)**
- Muralidharan, Singh and Ganimian, "Disrupting Education? Experimental Evidence on Technology-Aided Instruction in India", American Economic Review 109(4), April 2019, pp. 1426–60: lottery winners "scored 0.37σ higher in math and 0.23σ higher in Hindi over just a 4.5-month period"; IV estimates imply 90 days of attendance would raise scores 0.6σ and 0.39σ; "similar absolute test score gains for all students, but much greater relative gains for academically-weaker students" — [AER article page](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20171112). (The NBER working-paper version reports 0.36σ and 0.22σ — [NBER w22923](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22923).)
- Sample and baseline: 619 students recruited from Delhi public middle schools (305 control, 314 treatment; 97.5% in grades 6–9; 86% followed up); "The average grade 6 student is around 2.5 grade levels below grade 6 standards in Math; by grade 9, this deficit increases to 4.5 grade levels"; "Students enrolled in the same grade typically span five to six grade levels in their preparation" — [NBER working paper 22923 (PDF)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22923/w22923.pdf).
- How Mindspark diagnoses: adaptation "is dynamic, occurring both at the beginning based on a diagnostic assessment, and then with every subsequent activity completed"; the software analyses "patterns in student errors", and the paper notes "the emphasis on error analysis reflects EI's long experience in conducting similar analyses and providing diagnostic feedback to teachers based on paper-and-pen tests" — [NBER w22923 PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22923/w22923.pdf). J-PAL's summary: three centres in low-income Delhi neighbourhoods; 90-minute sessions of 45 minutes software plus 45 minutes small-group teaching; cost about INR 1,000 (US$15) a month versus INR 1,500 per public-school student — [J-PAL evaluation summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/disrupting-education-evidence-technology-aided-instruction-india).
- ASSET: Educational Initiatives' web pages (ei.study/assetnote, ei.study) did not render any body text when fetched, so no ASSET facts could be verified (see Gaps).

**Pratham ASER (India; ages 5–16, floor-level oral tool)**
- Tool design: "The same test is administered to all children. The highest level of reading tested corresponds to what is expected in Std 2 and the highest level of arithmetic tested corresponds to what is expected in Std 3 or 4"; "The aim of the tools is to mark the child on the highest level the child can comfortably do. Both the Language and the Arithmetic tools have 5 levels"; reading levels are Beginner, Letter, Word, Paragraph, Story (Story = "a Grade 2 level text… 7–10 sentences and approximately 60 words. Can make 2 to 3 mistakes"); arithmetic runs number recognition → 2-digit subtraction with regrouping (ASER's own word is "borrowing") → 3-digit by 1-digit division — [ASER Assessment and Survey Framework (PDF)](https://img.asercentre.org/docs/Bottom%20Panel/Key%20Docs/aserassessmentframeworkdocument.pdf); tools exist in 19 Indian languages — [ASER tools](https://asercentre.org/aser-tools/).
- 2024 results: the survey "reached 649,491 children in 17,997 villages across 605 rural districts"; Std III children able to do "at least subtraction" rose from 25.4% (2014) to 28.2% (2018), fell to 25.9% (2022) and reached 33.7% in 2024, "higher than we have seen in the last decade"; Std III children reading a Std II text: 27.1% in 2024; Std V children able to do division: 30.7% in 2024 (25.6% in 2022); government-school Std III subtraction went from 20.2% to 27.6% between 2022 and 2024 — [ASER 2024 full report (PDF)](https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/ASER_2024_Final-Report_13_2_24-1.pdf).
- Use for teaching: Teaching at the Right Level uses "five reading and arithmetic proficiency levels", groups children "according to their current learning levels either across grades or within the same class", re-groups them after "regular, time-bound assessments", and cites "Six randomised evaluations across seven states in India" with J-PAL — [Pratham: Teaching at the Right Level](https://www.pratham.org/about/teaching-at-the-right-level/).

### Inferences
- The misconception-tagged MCQ is powerful *after* a child can read fluently and hold four options in mind; the best evidence (Eedi RCT in Years 7–8, Mindspark in grades 6–9, ASSISTments grade 7, Cognitive Tutor grade 9) is all from ages 11+. Below age 8 the field uses oral one-to-one probes (ASER, DIBELS, BASE) and observed work, not distractor selection. Cornerstone's design — free-response paper work photographed and read, with wrong answers matched to a misconception by code — sits between the two and is more diagnostic than an MCQ at this age because it captures the child's own production, not a chosen distractor.
- Eedi's own 2026 research says a wrong-answer-only view misses "correct answer through flawed reasoning" cases and that detectors raise 4–8 false alarms per true detection; this is a direct argument for Cornerstone's three-signal rule (blank / wrong / wrong-with-working) and for treating a model's misconception read as a hypothesis that needs a second, code-verified instance before it is recorded as a fact.
- ALEKS and Cognitive Tutor show that ~24–30 well-chosen items can locate a state in a 350-concept space and that ~85% of sections reach machine-judged mastery; they also show what happens when adults override the criterion (lower post-tests). The comparable Cornerstone risk is an educator advancing a rung on a hunch.
- ASER's "highest level comfortably done, allow 2–3 mistakes" rule is the simplest published mastery criterion for this age group and is understood by every Indian education official; its ceiling (Grade 2 text, Grade 3–4 arithmetic) makes it a floor check, not a curriculum measure.

### Gaps
- The EEF-funded evaluation of Eedi (Harrison, Brown, Higgins 2023, listed on Eedi's papers page) could not be read (EEF pages returned 403; no ERIC record found); its effect size and security rating are therefore unreported here.
- No published inter-rater or test–retest reliability for *misconception labels* (as opposed to scores) was found for Eedi, Mindspark or ASSET; the Kaggle MAP@25 ≈ 0.5 result is the closest proxy and is a secondary source.
- ASSET (grades, format, reach, psychometrics) could not be verified because EI's pages rendered no text; the Mindspark paper's footnote on EI's paper-and-pen diagnostic history is the only opened source.
- ASER's framework document does not report inter-rater reliability of volunteer testers; the tools page mentions a "Reliability and validity of tools" document that was not opened.

## Key question 3 — Paper-based capture and AI grading (Gradescope, ZipGrade, GradeCam/Gradient, Plickers, Remark Office OMR, handwriting at scale): claimed accuracy and the human check

### Takeaway
No product opened publishes an accuracy figure for reading children's handwriting; the successful products either restrict the mark to something a machine reads near-perfectly (bubbles, rotated cards, barcodes/QR) or keep a mandatory human-in-the-loop for anything handwritten (Gradescope groups similar answers and "nothing is graded automatically without that check"; Remark flags "cross-outs, multiple marks" as exceptions). Cornerstone's per-child QR plus vision-model read plus human queue is the same architecture as Gradescope's, applied to a younger population where the handwriting problem is harder and unmeasured.

### Cited findings
- Gradescope: handles "scanned paper assignments", "bubble sheet assignments" with a predefined key, and "AI-Assisted answer grouping features" that "automatically group similar student answers that instructors can review and grade"; instructors then "grade student answers instead of grading each student answer one-by-one" with rubrics — [Gradescope: get started](https://www.gradescope.com/get_started). A third-party explainer describes the mechanism — student pages overlaid on the blank template to isolate ink, OCR of English handwriting and maths notation, near-identical responses clustered — and the rule that "Every suggested answer group must be reviewed and confirmed by an instructor. Nothing is graded automatically without that check"; open-ended essays "still require full human evaluation"; no accuracy percentages are given — [Apporto explainer](https://www.apporto.com/gradescopes-ai-assisted-grading-explained) (secondary; Gradescope's own help article returned 403).
- ZipGrade: phone-camera grading of bubble sheets in "20, 50, and 100 question sizes" plus custom forms supporting "multiple choice, true/false, matching, and gridded-numeric entry"; "Grade 100 papers FREE each month", unlimited for "$6.99 USD for one year"; "over 1 million teachers"; item analysis including discrimination and CSV export; no accuracy claims or lighting/mark-quality caveats on the page — [zipgrade.com](https://www.zipgrade.com/).
- GradeCam / Gradient: marketed as "Paper & digital assessments" with immediate scoring and gradebook transfer; "862K+ students, 345K+ educators", "40 states and 7 countries"; no handwriting-recognition or accuracy claims on the pages opened — [gradientk12.com](https://gradientk12.com/); [gradecam.com](https://gradecam.com/).
- Plickers: students hold up printed cards with rotated codes; the teacher scans the class with one phone; multiple-choice and true/false only; real-time results; free; "K-12 teachers in over 100 countries" — [get.plickers.com](https://get.plickers.com/). Eedi's DQR uses the same mechanism ("rotate it to answer between A, B, C, or D"; the teacher can "scan the entire classroom of students in seconds"), and the app returns "a breakdown of common misconceptions based on wrong answers"; it is explicitly for low-tech settings where "all a teacher needs is a smartphone" — [Eedi DQR](https://www.eedi.com/dqr).
- Remark Office OMR: plain-paper forms designed in any word processor, read with TWAIN scanners or from PDF/TIFF/JPG; recognises marked bubbles, barcodes, typewritten text (OCR) and handwriting "through Microsoft Computer Vision integration"; handles "cross-outs, multiple marks, damaged forms, and other anomalies" with an exception-review step where users "enter new data, or clear data for desired questions"; exports to Excel/SPSS/SQL; no accuracy percentage stated — [Remark Office OMR](https://remarksoftware.com/products/office-omr/).
- Handwriting recognition for children at scale: no product or study reporting a measured accuracy on young children's handwritten numerals or working was found among the sources that could be opened (see Gaps). The closest published caution is Eedi's finding that even a state-of-the-art model reading student *reasoning* yields "false alarms outnumber genuine detections approximately 8:1" at realistic prevalence — [arXiv:2606.23205](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23205).

### Inferences
- Every mature product either constrains the mark (bubble, card, QR) or grades by *grouping* rather than by *reading*: the AI's job is to cluster identical answers so one human decision covers many scripts. For a 12,500-item bank with computed answers, Cornerstone can do better than grouping — the vision model transcribes and code checks — but the human queue must be sized on the assumption that transcription confidence, not answer correctness, is what triggers review.
- Exception handling (blank, multiple marks, cross-outs) is a first-class feature in OMR products; it maps directly onto Cornerstone's "blank" signal and argues for a "struck-out / rewritten" sub-state so that a crossed-out wrong answer followed by a right one is not read as either.
- Because no vendor publishes handwriting accuracy for children, Cornerstone's own gold-set precision/recall (`engine eval read_cells`) is not just internal hygiene; it would be the only published number of its kind in the council's evidence base if the school ever chooses to share it.

### Gaps
- Gradescope's official help article on AI-assisted grading and its "handwriting of English-language text and of math notation" claim could not be opened (403); the claim appears only in search snippets and a secondary explainer.
- No accuracy figures for ZipGrade, GradeCam, Plickers or Remark were found on their sites.
- No peer-reviewed study of OCR/vision accuracy on children's (ages 5–9) handwritten numerals in school worksheets was located in this research (search budget exhausted before this could be pursued); this remains the largest evidence hole for Cornerstone's own architecture.

## Key question 4 — Formative assessment research (Black & Wiliam, Wiliam's later work, EEF feedback evidence, the case against over-assessing young children): what cadence is right for 5–9-year-olds

### Takeaway
The 1998 "0.4–0.7 standard deviations" claim has been revised down by meta-analysis to a weighted mean of about 0.20 (higher, ~0.30, for professional-development-led and computer-based formative systems), and the EEF's 2021 guidance says timing should be a teacher judgement, not a policy, and explicitly excludes under-5s for lack of evidence. The consistent message across the formative literature, the early-childhood critiques and India's NCF-SE is the same: for ages 3–8, assessment should be continuous observation of ordinary work with fast feedback, never a test event; from about age 8 (Preparatory Stage), short formative written checks plus one end-of-year summative are appropriate.

### Cited findings
- Black & Wiliam, "Inside the Black Box" (Phi Delta Kappan, October 1998): formative-assessment studies showed effect sizes "between 0.4 and 0.7", "larger than most of those found for educational interventions"; "if pupils are given only marks or grades, they do not benefit from the feedback"; "improved formative assessment helps low achievers more than other students and so reduces the range of achievement while raising achievement overall"; self-assessment "an essential component" — [Kappan reprint](https://kappanonline.org/inside-the-black-box-raising-standards-through-classroom-assessment/).
- Kingston & Nash (2011), Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice: of over 300 relevant studies "only 13 provided sufficient data", giving 42 effect sizes; "The median observed effect size was 0.25" and "a weighted mean effect size of 0.20"; by subject ELA 0.32, mathematics 0.17, science 0.09; professional-development-based and computer-based formative approaches averaged 0.30 and 0.28; "An effect size of about 0.70 (or 0.40–0.70) is often claimed for the efficacy of formative assessment, but is not supported by the existing research base" — [ERIC record EJ951173](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ951173).
- Feedback is not uniformly positive: Hattie & Timperley (2007) — "the type of feedback and the way it is given can be differentially effective", with "the timing of feedback and the effects of positive and negative feedback" among the "thorny issues" — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.3102/003465430298487?fields=title,year,venue,authors,abstract,citationCount). Kluger & DeNisi (1996), Psychological Bulletin, "The effects of feedback interventions on performance: a historical review, a meta-analysis, and a preliminary feedback intervention theory" — [CrossRef record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) (abstract not available through the API; the paper is the standard citation for feedback sometimes lowering performance).
- EEF, Teacher Feedback to Improve Pupil Learning (2021): six recommendations — "1 Lay the foundations for effective feedback; 2 Deliver appropriately timed feedback that focuses on moving learning forward; 3 Plan for how pupils will receive and use feedback; 4 Carefully consider how to use purposeful, and time-efficient, written feedback; 5 Carefully consider how to use purposeful verbal feedback; 6 Design a school feedback policy that prioritises and exemplifies the principles of effective feedback". On timing: "There is not one clear answer for when feedback should be provided. Rather, teachers should judge whether more immediate or delayed feedback is required, considering the characteristics of the task set, the individual pupil, and the collective understanding of the class." On scope: "recommendations on how to deliver feedback to 5- to 18-year-old learners. Early years is not included as the underpinning systematic review found little relevant evidence in the area." On policy: feedback policies that "explicitly specified the frequency of written feedback" show a "level of specificity [that] is likely unwarranted". It cites the Embedding Formative Assessment trial in 140 secondary schools: "two additional months' progress" in Attainment 8 with "a very high security rating" — [EEF guidance report, ERIC full text ED615988 (PDF)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED615988.pdf); catalogue record dated 16 Aug 2021 — [ERIC ED615988](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED615988).
- Against testing very young children: BERA (2018) — quick, simple assessments at reception age are age-confounded, of "dubious validity", and "hard to justify when [not] being used to support a child's learning" — [BERA](https://www.bera.ac.uk/publication/a-baseline-without-basis). Bassok, Latham & Rorem (2016), "Is Kindergarten the New First Grade?": comparing US public kindergartens in 1998 and 2010, teachers "devoted more time to advanced literacy and math content, teacher-directed instruction, and assessment and substantially less time to art, music, science, and child-selected activities" — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1177/2332858415616358?fields=title,year,venue,authors,abstract,citationCount). The US National Research Council's consensus report *Early Childhood Assessment: Why, What, and How* (National Academies Press, 2008) organises the field around purposes, validity/utility and "coordinated, thoughtful assessment systems rather than isolated testing" — [NAP record](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/12446/chapter/1) (only the front matter rendered; editors and specific recommendations not extractable).
- India's framework on cadence: NCF-SE 2023 §3.4.9.1 (Foundational Stage, ages 3–8): "Explicit tests and examinations are completely inappropriate assessment tools for this stage… two primary methods of assessment are appropriate. Observations of the child and analysing artefacts that the child has created"; "Assessment should not overly burden the teacher… autonomy to judiciously choose the appropriate tool… and the periodicity". §3.4.9.2 (Preparatory Stage, Grades 3–5): "a robust system of formative assessment is required to track progress of individual students… a variety of formative oral and written assessments should be introduced… At the end of each year… a comprehensive summative assessment of the student's readiness to enter the next grade" — [NCF-SE 2023 (PDF)](https://ncert.nic.in/pdf/NCFSE-2023-August_2023.pdf).
- What cadence the products themselves use: three benchmark screenings a year of one-minute probes (mCLASS/DIBELS) — [CDE](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ci/cl/mclassinfooverview.asp); MAP Growth "up to four times a year" of 25–40 minutes per subject for K–2 — [NWEA technical report](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/MAP-Growth-Technical-Report-2025.pdf); Eedi re-presents an analogous question about three weeks after an error (a spaced re-check) — [Foster et al. 2021](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/s10649-021-10084-7?fields=title,year,venue,authors,abstract,citationCount); Pratham re-groups children after "regular, time-bound assessments" — [Pratham TaRL](https://www.pratham.org/about/teaching-at-the-right-level/).

### Inferences
- A weekly paper worksheet that is *ordinary classwork*, photographed rather than administered, is consistent with every source above for ages 5–9: it is an artefact-based observation (NCF-SE), it is short, it produces feedback that can be acted on within days, and it never becomes a "test event". The design risk is the report, not the worksheet: if a weekly mastery verdict is shown to a child or parent as a grade, the Black & Wiliam finding on "marks only" and the BERA/Bassok concerns apply.
- Cadence guidance that survives scrutiny: continuous (weekly) evidence capture; feedback to the child within the same or next lesson; a re-check of any failed skill after a spaced interval of ~2–3 weeks (Eedi's operational choice, supported by the hypercorrection result); a summary to parents no more often than termly for ages 3–8; a single end-of-year summative from Grade 3 upward (NCF-SE). Nothing in the evidence supports quarterly 30–60-minute screen tests for 5–7-year-olds.
- Effect sizes of 0.2–0.3 are what good formative systems actually deliver at scale; Cornerstone should not promise more, and should expect the largest gains for the weakest children (Black & Wiliam; ASSISTments 0.29 for low achievers; Mindspark's larger relative gains for weaker students).

### Gaps
- Dylan Wiliam's short/medium/long-cycle formulation could not be sourced from an opened document (his papers page lists related titles only; the ERIC search found no record) — [dylanwiliam.org papers list](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers.html).
- The EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit "feedback" strand (months-progress estimate) and the EEF Eedi trial page were blocked (403) and are not cited.
- No study was found that directly tests assessment *frequency* (weekly vs monthly) as a variable for 5–9-year-olds; the cadence inference above is assembled from product practice and framework guidance, not from an experiment.

## Key question 5 — Measurement fundamentals a school system must respect (minimum evidence for mastery, reliability of short tests, IRT basics, difficulty vs complexity, standards-based grading, growth without a single scale)

### Takeaway
The fundamentals are unforgiving and well documented: reliability grows sub-linearly with test length (Spearman–Brown), a Rasch-calibrated item's difficulty is the point where half the children at that ability get it right, and information — hence precision — is concentrated where item difficulty matches the child. Mastery criteria in adaptive systems conflate "how much is known" with "how sure we are" (Pelánek), the conventional 0.95 BKT threshold is being revised upward (0.98 in 2025 field data), and the growth-model literature's clearest lesson is that gain scores need a vertical scale while conditional-status measures (student growth percentiles) do not — and neither should be compared across subjects. Grading research says a single composite grade is unreliable and multidimensional, and the standards-based fix is to separate product, process and progress.

### Cited findings
- Test length and reliability: Spearman–Brown, ρ'ₓₓ = nρₓₓ / [1 + (n−1)ρₓₓ]; "Test length must grow by increasingly larger values as the desired reliability approaches 1.0" — a 50-item test with reliability 0.80 needs about 238 items to reach 0.95; and "If the longer/shorter test is not parallel… the prediction will not be strictly accurate" — [Wikipedia: Spearman–Brown prediction formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spearman%E2%80%93Brown_prediction_formula).
- IRT basics: in the 1PL/Rasch model the difficulty b is the ability at which success probability is 50%; the 2PL adds discrimination a; the 3PL adds a guessing asymptote c ≈ 1/(number of options); item information is maximal near the item's difficulty, and SE(θ) = 1/√I(θ); the Rasch model's "specific objectivity" gives sample-independent item calibration that 2PL/3PL lose — [Wikipedia: Item response theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Item_response_theory). Applied statements of the same: "a student's RIT score… answers correctly about 50% of the time" — [NWEA blog](https://www.nwea.org/blog/2025/how-the-map-growth-rit-scale-offers-valuable-insights-into-student-growth/); "the high and low scores on any given test have the highest SEM" — [e-asTTle booklet](https://www.datocms-assets.com/56414/1726795170-e-asttle-support-booklet-24-25.pdf); "Error margins are larger for very high and very low scores" — [ACER](https://www.acer.org/files/PATM-Interpreting-Scores.pdf).
- What "reliable" costs in practice: 34 adaptive items give Star Math generic reliability 0.89–0.94 but alternate-form reliability of only 0.71 at kindergarten over ~100 days — [Star Math manual, Table 11](https://flfast.org/content/contentresources/en/SMRPTechnicalManual_FL.pdf); MAP Growth's instructional-area scores fall to 0.75 "for instructional areas with small numbers of items" — [NWEA technical report](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/MAP-Growth-Technical-Report-2025.pdf); one-minute kindergarten phonemic probes re-correlate at only .49 after a delay — [DIBELS 8 manual](https://dibels.uoregon.edu/sites/default/files/DIBELS8-TechnicalManual_04152020.pdf).
- Mastery criteria: Bayesian knowledge tracing originates in Corbett & Anderson, "Knowledge tracing: Modeling the acquisition of procedural knowledge", User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction (1995) — [CrossRef record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/BF01099821). Pelánek, "Conceptual Issues in Mastery Criteria: Differentiating Uncertainty and Degrees of Knowledge": "commonly used mastery criteria combine two different aspects of knowledge estimate in the comparison to this threshold: the degree of achieved knowledge and the uncertainty of the estimate"; the simplest criterion is "N consecutive correct", and "The choice of the threshold involves a trade-off between unnecessary over-practice and the risk of premature declaration of mastery"; interpreting a BKT 0.95 as "95% chance the learner has mastered" is "misleading. Unless the data correspond very closely to the strong assumptions of the BKT model, the interpretation is incorrect (possibly quite seriously)"; when observation noise is low, "even the basic N consecutive correct (NCC) criterion leads to good estimates" — [Pelánek (PDF)](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/mastery-modeling.pdf).
- Threshold evidence from the field: Zhang, Vanacore, Baker, Ch, Mills and Henkel (EDM 2025), on 5,039 micro-lessons, 543 students and 49,612 problems in the Rori WhatsApp maths tutor (seven African countries), compared eight mastery levels and found students at or above P(Ln) ≥ 0.98 "outperformed their peers across multiple learning metrics in the following lessons", so "P(Ln) > 0.98 may serve as a more effective threshold for defining mastery" than the customary 0.95 — [EDM 2025 short paper](https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2025/proceedings/2025.EDM.short-papers.4/index.html). In Cognitive Tutor Algebra I field data, ~85% of sections ended in machine-judged mastery and moving students on early lowered post-test scores — [arXiv:1802.08616](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.08616). ALEKS needs ~24–30 items to fix a knowledge state and validates itself with a held-out probe (r ≈ .7–.8) — [Falmagne PDF](https://www.aleks.com/about_aleks/Science_Behind_ALEKS.pdf).
- Difficulty versus complexity: IRT difficulty is an empirical pass-rate property of an item (the 50% point) — [Wikipedia: IRT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Item_response_theory) — whereas "cognitive complexity" is treated as a separate design criterion: CCSSO's 2013 criteria for high-quality mathematics assessments list "C.4 Requiring a range of cognitive demand" alongside content criteria, and Achieve built a framework to evaluate it — [Achieve, A Framework to Evaluate Cognitive Complexity in Mathematics Assessments (2019, ERIC ED603609 PDF)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED603609.pdf). (Hess's Cognitive Rigor Matrix, the usual source for the difficulty/complexity distinction, could not be opened; see Gaps.)
- Growth without a single scale: Castellano & Ho, *A Practitioner's Guide to Growth Models* (CCSSO 2013) cover seven models — "Gain Score • Trajectory • Categorical • Residual Gain • Projection • Student Growth Percentile • Multivariate"; "A vertical scale links the reporting test score scale across several grade levels… in many cases, vertical scales are not possible for the subject matter tested"; gain-based models require "a common, vertical scale"; "Part of the appeal of SGPs and other conditional status metrics is that they do not require test scores from multiple time points to share a common vertical scale", but SGPs need large samples — "A loose rule of thumb is to include at least 5,000 students"; categorical (proficiency-band transition) models carry "an implicit vertical scale" — [ERIC ED551292 (PDF)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED551292.pdf). Betebenner (2009) introduced SGPs as "a normative description of growth capable of accommodating criterion-referenced aims" — [CrossRef record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2009.00161.x). ACER keeps one scale per learning area and states reading and maths scores are not comparable — [ACER](https://schoolsupport.acer.org/hc/en-au/articles/22255011145241-Scale-scores-and-achievement-bands).
- Grades and standards-based grading: Brookhart et al. (2016), reviewing a century of research: "Early 20th-century studies generally condemned teachers' grades as unreliable", and grades "assess a multidimensional construct containing both cognitive and noncognitive factors" — [CrossRef record, Review of Educational Research](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3102/0034654316672069). Guskey, "Five Obstacles to Grading Reform": report cards still list "a single grade for each subject area"; teachers "combine aspects of students' achievement, attitude, responsibility, effort, and behavior into a single grade"; "Grades based on students' standing among classmates tell us nothing about how well students have learned"; the standards-based remedy is that "educators distinguish product, process, and progress learning criteria" and report them separately — [Guskey (PDF)](https://tguskey.com/wp-content/uploads/Grading-3-Five-Obstacles-to-Grading-Reform.pdf).

### Inferences
- For a per-skill mastery claim from paper worksheets, the arithmetic is stark: a skill checked by 3–4 items a week is far below the item counts that give 0.9 reliability; the honest unit of evidence is therefore *accumulated* across weeks, and the mastery rule should be an explicit, transparent count (N correct across ≥2 sittings, with the third signal — wrong-with-working — counted as evidence of the misconception, not as noise), exactly the "N consecutive correct" family Pelánek shows performs as well as BKT when observation noise is low. Code-verified answers make observation noise low.
- Whatever threshold is chosen, the 2025 field evidence points to erring strict (0.98 rather than 0.95, or one more consecutive success) because under-practice hurts the *next* skill.
- Rasch calibration of Cornerstone's items is feasible only per skill/rung with hundreds of responses per item; until then, the four difficulty levels are author-assigned complexity levels (a design property), not empirical difficulties, and reports should say so. The distinction matters because a "Hard" item that most children answer is not hard in the IRT sense.
- Growth reporting for a small school cannot use SGPs (≥5,000 students) or a vertical scale it does not own; the defensible options are criterion-referenced progress along the school's own rung ladder per subject, reported as movement between described bands (the ACER/e-asTTle pattern), and never a cross-subject composite.
- A single report-card grade is the thing the grading literature most consistently condemns; the standards-based practice of separate product/process/progress reports is compatible with, and gives vocabulary for, the HPC that India now requires (see Q6).

### Gaps
- Hess's Cognitive Rigor Matrix and Webb's DOK documentation could not be opened (404/403); the difficulty–complexity distinction is therefore supported here by the IRT definition and the CCSSO/Achieve criterion rather than by Hess's own text.
- Kluger & DeNisi's abstract and its "about one-third of feedback interventions reduced performance" figure were not retrievable through the APIs used (title and year only).
- No source was found that gives a minimum item count for a *classroom* mastery decision on a narrow skill; the products' 24–72 items are for whole-domain placement, not single-skill mastery.

## Key question 6 — India: PARAKH Holistic Progress Card, NCERT/NCF-SE competency-based assessment, CBSE's shift, PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan 2024 on early numeracy; what parents and future schools will expect

### Takeaway
Indian policy now describes, in some detail, the report format parents of a Nursery–Grade 4 child will meet elsewhere: a Holistic Progress Card with descriptive, disaggregated, competency-level entries and no single grade (NCF-SE 2023, PARAKH, CBSE's own 2025–26 curriculum which requires HPCs), assessment by observation and artefacts in the Foundational Stage, formative written work plus an end-of-year summative from Grade 3, and external competency-based examinations at Grades 3, 5 and 8. The 2024 national survey (Grade 3, 45 MCQs, 90 minutes) reports 65% of Grade 3 children proficient in mathematics and 57% in language — much better than 2021 — while ASER's household oral tests still find only one rural Std III child in three can do a two-digit subtraction with regrouping.

### Cited findings
- NCF-SE 2023 on the HPC (§3.4.10): "an individualised and comprehensive reporting of a student's progress in all domains or areas of learning on the basis of Competencies and Learning Outcomes"; "Unlike a single score or letter grade, an HPC provides disaggregated reporting… based on evidence of student work beyond performances on tests (e.g., assessment of writing samples, projects, portfolios, artwork, participation in games)"; "The comparison will be with the student's earlier report card and not with the performance of other students in the class." §3.4.11: "Students should not be detained from Grades 1 to 8"; "NEP 2020 (4.40) requires all students to take examinations in Grades 3, 5 and 8, conducted by an appropriate authority that tests competencies… These will be competency-based examinations… For Grades 3 and 5 the focus could be on FLN competencies." — [NCF-SE 2023 (PDF)](https://ncert.nic.in/pdf/NCFSE-2023-August_2023.pdf).
- PARAKH's HPC: covers Foundational (Bal Vatika to Grade 2), Preparatory (3–5), Middle (6–8) and Secondary stages; five domains (physical, socio-emotional, cognitive, language and literacy, aesthetic and cultural); the web page's rubrics use three performance levels named "Stream", "Mountain" and "Sky" (e.g., "A Stream Level student follows instructions/directions for two- or three- step tasks… A Sky Level student follows instructions/directions for tasks with more than five steps"), with self-assessment ("I liked doing this work"), peer assessment and a parent/caregiver form in "A 360-degree Holistic Progress Cards" — [PARAKH HPC page](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/hpc). PARAKH's blog stresses "Instead of grades or scores, it provides narrative and descriptive feedback" and "ongoing observation and teacher reflection" — [PARAKH: HPC Foundational Stage](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/blog/hpc-foundational-stage).
- Terminology is not yet stable across documents: PARAKH's "How to fill the HPC (Foundational Stage)" rates each activity on three abilities — Awareness, Sensitivity, Creativity — at "Beginner, Proficient, and Advanced", records "a graphical representation of the student's performance in this domain in the whole term", and has the parent section filled "by the teacher in consultation with the parents during the SMCs/PTA meetings" — [How to fill the HPC, Foundational Stage (PDF)](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/themes/parakh/hpc-files/how-to-fill-pdf/How-to-fill-the-HPC-(Foundational-Stage).pdf); CBSE's HPC teacher guide instead defines "Beginner: Tries to achieve the Competency… with a lot of support… Progressive: Achieves… with occasional/some support… Proficient: Achieves… on his/her own", defines competencies as "learning achievements that are observable and can be assessed systematically", and asks teachers "To identify misconceptions or alternative conceptions" — [CBSE Holistic Progress Card teacher guide (PDF)](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/web_material/Manuals/HPC_TeacherGuide.pdf).
- CBSE's 2025–26 curriculum (Secondary front matter): "schools are required to implement Holistic Progress Cards (HPCs) as formal communication tools between schools and families… HPCs should focus on individual progress and interests, providing disaggregated reporting to avoid comparisons with peers. Detailed guidelines and prototypes of HPC have been provided by CBSE for Foundational and Preparatory Stage"; Foundational-stage guidance says to "Prioritize observation of children and analysis of their created artifacts as primary assessment methods"; Board question papers "have more questions based on real-life situations requiring students to apply, analyse, evaluate and synthesize"; Class X grades use a 9-point octile system — [CBSE Curriculum 2025–26, Secondary (PDF)](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/web_material/CurriculumMain26/Sec/Curriculum_Sec_2025-26.pdf). The Class X Mathematics (Standard) paper design weights "Remembering/Understanding" at 43 marks (54%) and "Applying" at 19 marks (24%), with the remaining marks for analysing/evaluating/creating — [CBSE Mathematics IX–X curriculum 2025–26 (PDF)](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/web_material/CurriculumMain26/Sec/Maths_Sec_2025-26.pdf).
- PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan 2024 (successor to NAS): conducted 4 December 2024 in Classes 3, 6 and 9 across all 36 States/UTs; "Class 3: 45 questions in Language and Mathematics"; "At Grades 3 learners will be evaluated in foundational stage competencies and the assessment time will be 90 minutes"; items "reflect the learning outcomes developed by PARAKH" — [PARAKH PRS page](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/prs); [PRS reports page](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/prs-reports-2024). Headline results: "21 lakh students", "74,000+ schools", "781 districts"; Grade 3 mathematics 65% proficient "(compared to 42% in NAS 2021)"; Grade 3 language 57% "(up from 39% in NAS 2021)"; "Rural students edged ahead of urban peers in Grade 3"; persistent gaps in Grades 6 and 9 — [PARAKH blog: PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan 2024](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/blog/parakh-rashtriya-sarvekshan-2024). The national report was released on 11 July 2025 — [Careers360 news](https://news.careers360.com/parakh-rashtriya-sarvekshan-result-2024-top-schools-30-pc-lack-resource-teacher-subject-job-satisfaction-training-class-3-6-9) (secondary). A coaching-site summary reports competency-level findings such as "Only 55% of students can correctly arrange numbers up to 99 in ascending/descending order" — [Rau's IAS summary](https://compass.rauias.com/current-affairs/parakh-rashtriya-sarvekshan-2024/) (secondary; unverified against the primary report, which is a scanned PDF — [PRS 2024 Technical Report, 1,008 pages, image-only](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/themes/parakh/prs-files/PRS_2024_Technical_Report.pdf)).
- What the Grade 3 items look like: four-option multiple choice; language items are comprehension questions on a poster and a short passage; mathematics items include a skip-counting pattern ("6, 12, 18, ______, 30, 36") and reading a map ("where is Hina's house?… West of the hospital…") — [PRS 2024 Grade 3 sample items (PDF)](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/themes/parakh/prs-files/Grade3.pdf).
- The contrasting household picture: ASER 2024 (rural, oral, one-to-one) finds 33.7% of Std III children can do a two-digit subtraction with regrouping and 27.1% can read a Std II text — [ASER 2024 (PDF)](https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/ASER_2024_Final-Report_13_2_24-1.pdf).

### Inferences
- The two national numbers are not contradictory: PRS measures competencies with 45 MCQs in 90 minutes at school and reports IRT-style proficiency bands (the IRT claim comes only from search snippets and is unverified against the scanned report); ASER marks the highest level a child performs orally at home. A Pune parent will see both kinds of numbers in the press; Cornerstone's per-skill evidence lets it say precisely which of these a child can do.
- Parents and receiving schools will expect (a) an HPC-shaped report: descriptive, per competency, three-level attainment language (Beginner/Progressing/Proficient or Stream/Mountain/Sky — the vocabulary is still settling), self/peer/parent sections, no ranks; (b) from Grade 3, competency-based MCQ formats like PRS and, later, CBSE's typology weights; (c) no detention to Grade 8 and external competency exams at Grades 3, 5, 8. A school engine that exports its skill graph into HPC language, and that can generate PRS-style MCQs from its bank for Grade 3 practice, meets both expectations without adopting either as its internal truth.
- NCF-SE's "explicit tests… completely inappropriate" for ages 3–8 is a constraint on *format and framing*, not on evidence collection: observation and artefacts are exactly what a photographed worksheet is, provided the child never experiences it as a test and the record stays descriptive.

## Council position

Each bullet gives the recommendation, the exemplar to borrow from or avoid, a confidence level, and the evidence.

1. **Borrow the vertical, criterion-described ladder from ACER PAT and e-asTTle, and keep one scale per subject — never a cross-subject composite.** ACER states reading and maths units are not comparable and describes each band in words; Castellano & Ho show gain scores need a vertical scale while conditional-status metrics do not. Cornerstone's rungs R1–R14 per subject are the right shape; report movement between described bands, not a number. *Confidence: high.* — [ACER](https://schoolsupport.acer.org/hc/en-au/articles/22255011145241-Scale-scores-and-achievement-bands); [Castellano & Ho](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED551292.pdf); [e-asTTle](https://www.datocms-assets.com/56414/1726795170-e-asttle-support-booklet-24-25.pdf).
2. **Avoid the 30–60-minute screen test for children under 8 (MAP Growth K–2, i-Ready, Star as universal screeners).** Their kindergarten evidence is the weakest part of their own manuals (i-Ready K classification AUC 0.75 "partially convincing"; Star K alternate-form 0.71; MAP K–2 25–40 minutes per subject up to four times a year), and BERA/NCF-SE both rule out test events at this age. Keep evidence capture inside ordinary work. *Confidence: high.* — [NCII i-Ready](https://charts.intensiveintervention.org/screening/tool/?id=7534542c4f422f85); [Star manual](https://flfast.org/content/contentresources/en/SMRPTechnicalManual_FL.pdf); [NWEA technical report](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/MAP-Growth-Technical-Report-2025.pdf); [BERA](https://www.bera.ac.uk/publication/a-baseline-without-basis); [NCF-SE 2023](https://ncert.nic.in/pdf/NCFSE-2023-August_2023.pdf).
3. **Borrow the one-minute, one-to-one oral probe (DIBELS/mCLASS, Cambridge BASE, ASER) for pre-readers (Nursery–Grade 1) and for fluency facts, but treat any single probe as unstable.** DIBELS's own delayed alternate-form reliability for kindergarten phonemic segmentation is .49; the products compensate with three benchmark windows and progress monitoring. Cornerstone should log probes as evidence rows, never as a verdict from one sitting. *Confidence: high.* — [DIBELS 8 manual](https://dibels.uoregon.edu/sites/default/files/DIBELS8-TechnicalManual_04152020.pdf); [CDE mCLASS](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ci/cl/mclassinfooverview.asp); [CEM](https://www.cem.org/primary-assessments); [ASER framework](https://img.asercentre.org/docs/Bottom%20Panel/Key%20Docs/aserassessmentframeworkdocument.pdf).
4. **Borrow Eedi's discipline of naming every wrong answer, and its 3-week re-check, but do not borrow the multiple-choice format for ages 5–8.** Eedi's misconception graph (8,000+ misconceptions from 200M responses, CC BY 4.0) is worth mining for Cornerstone's `misconception` table; its RCT (d = 0.30 at 24 months) and Mindspark's (0.37σ) were both with 11–15-year-olds, and Eedi's own 2026 research shows correct answers can hide misconceptions and detectors raise 4–8 false alarms per hit. Cornerstone's free-response capture with three signals is the better instrument at this age; the re-check after ~3 weeks is the operational habit to copy. *Confidence: medium-high.* — [Misconception graph](https://www.eedi.com/news/eedis-misconception-graph-is-now-a-public-good); [Eedi RCT](https://www.eedi.com/news/two-year-rct-earns-eedi-a-gold-efficacy-certificate-from-eduevidence); [Foster et al.](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/s10649-021-10084-7?fields=title,year,venue,authors,abstract,citationCount); [arXiv:2606.23205](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23205); [AER Mindspark](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20171112).
5. **Make the mastery rule explicit, transparent and strict — a counted rule (N correct across at least two sittings), not a hidden probability — and err toward more evidence.** Pelánek shows BKT-style thresholds conflate knowledge and uncertainty and that N-consecutive-correct performs as well when observations are clean (code-verified answers are clean); 2025 field data favour 0.98 over 0.95; Cognitive Tutor logs show that moving children on early lowers later scores. Never let an educator's override silently rewrite the rule — record it as a superseding evidence row. *Confidence: high.* — [Pelánek](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/mastery-modeling.pdf); [EDM 2025](https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2025/proceedings/2025.EDM.short-papers.4/index.html); [arXiv:1802.08616](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.08616).
6. **Report per-skill results as bands with uncertainty, never as a reliable score, until items are calibrated.** Spearman–Brown and the vendors' own sub-score reliabilities (MAP instructional areas down to 0.75 with few items) mean a week's 3–4 items on one skill cannot carry a mastery claim alone; the four "Easy/Medium/Hard/Advance" levels are author-assigned complexity, not empirical difficulty, until hundreds of responses per item exist for Rasch calibration. *Confidence: high.* — [Spearman–Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spearman%E2%80%93Brown_prediction_formula); [IRT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Item_response_theory); [NWEA technical report](https://www.nwea.org/uploads/MAP-Growth-Technical-Report-2025.pdf).
7. **Borrow Gradescope's human-in-the-loop rule and Remark's exception handling for the photo pipeline: nothing handwritten is final without a check, and blank / multiple / crossed-out marks are first-class states.** No product opened publishes handwriting accuracy for children, so Cornerstone's gold-set evaluation (`engine eval read_cells`) is the only accuracy number it will have; size the human queue on transcription confidence, not on answer correctness. *Confidence: high on the rule, low on any expected accuracy figure.* — [Gradescope](https://www.gradescope.com/get_started); [Apporto](https://www.apporto.com/gradescopes-ai-assisted-grading-explained); [Remark](https://remarksoftware.com/products/office-omr/).
8. **Avoid the single grade; borrow standards-based separation of product, process and progress, and export the skill graph in HPC vocabulary.** A century of grading research condemns composite grades; NCF-SE, PARAKH and CBSE now require disaggregated, descriptive HPCs with self/peer/parent sections and comparison only with the child's own earlier card. Keep internal truth in `child_skill_state`; render it into the HPC's three-level language (Beginner/Progressing/Proficient or Stream/Mountain/Sky — track which PARAKH settles on). *Confidence: high.* — [Brookhart et al.](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3102/0034654316672069); [Guskey](https://tguskey.com/wp-content/uploads/Grading-3-Five-Obstacles-to-Grading-Reform.pdf); [NCF-SE 2023](https://ncert.nic.in/pdf/NCFSE-2023-August_2023.pdf); [PARAKH HPC](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/hpc); [CBSE Curriculum 2025–26](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/web_material/CurriculumMain26/Sec/Curriculum_Sec_2025-26.pdf).
9. **Set cadence by age: continuous artefact capture and same-week feedback for all; a spaced re-check of failed skills at ~2–3 weeks; a termly descriptive report to parents for ages 3–8; one end-of-year summative from Grade 3; nothing that the child experiences as a test before Grade 3.** This is the intersection of NCF-SE §3.4.9, the EEF's "no one clear answer on timing — teacher judgement", Eedi's re-ask interval and the products' three-to-four-window practice. *Confidence: medium (assembled from practice and frameworks; no experiment on frequency).* — [NCF-SE 2023](https://ncert.nic.in/pdf/NCFSE-2023-August_2023.pdf); [EEF feedback report](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED615988.pdf); [Foster et al.](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/s10649-021-10084-7?fields=title,year,venue,authors,abstract,citationCount).
10. **Promise formative-assessment effects of 0.2–0.3σ, concentrated in the weakest children, not 0.4–0.7.** Kingston & Nash's weighted mean is 0.20 (0.28–0.30 for computer-based/PD-led systems); ASSISTments 0.18 (0.29 for low achievers); Cognitive Tutor ~0.20 only in year two; Black & Wiliam and Mindspark both find the weakest gain most. Design the reports and the parent conversation around "this child moved from this rung to that rung", not around a headline effect. *Confidence: high.* — [Kingston & Nash](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ951173); [Blueprints ASSISTments](https://www.blueprintsprograms.org/programs/1261999999/assistments/); [RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_briefs/RB9746.html); [Kappan](https://kappanonline.org/inside-the-black-box-raising-standards-through-classroom-assessment/).
11. **Borrow ASER's floor check as an external anchor and PRS's item style as a Grade 3 rehearsal, but keep both outside the truth ring.** Once a year, an ASER-style one-to-one check (two-digit subtraction with regrouping; Std II text) gives a number every Indian parent and official understands and lets the school compare itself to the 33.7% / 27.1% rural baselines; from Grade 3, generate PRS-format four-option items from the bank so children meet the national format without the school adopting MCQs as its evidence base. *Confidence: medium-high.* — [ASER 2024](https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/ASER_2024_Final-Report_13_2_24-1.pdf); [PRS Grade 3 sample items](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/themes/parakh/prs-files/Grade3.pdf); [PARAKH PRS](https://parakh.ncert.gov.in/prs).
12. **What changes above Grade 4: adaptivity and misconception MCQs become the right tools.** From about age 10–11, 34–72-item computer-adaptive tests are reliable and quick (Star 34 items in <25 minutes; ALEKS locates a 350-concept state in ~25 questions), and misconception-tagged MCQs have their strongest causal evidence (Eedi Years 7–8; Mindspark grades 6–9); India adds external competency exams at Grades 5 and 8 and CBSE's typology-weighted papers at Grade 10. Cornerstone's bank and misconception table are the assets that make that transition cheap; the paper-first pipeline is the part to expect to change. *Confidence: medium-high.* — [Star manual](https://flfast.org/content/contentresources/en/SMRPTechnicalManual_FL.pdf); [Falmagne/ALEKS](https://www.aleks.com/about_aleks/Science_Behind_ALEKS.pdf); [Eedi RCT](https://www.eedi.com/news/two-year-rct-earns-eedi-a-gold-efficacy-certificate-from-eduevidence); [NCF-SE 2023](https://ncert.nic.in/pdf/NCFSE-2023-August_2023.pdf); [CBSE Maths 2025–26](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/web_material/CurriculumMain26/Sec/Maths_Sec_2025-26.pdf).
