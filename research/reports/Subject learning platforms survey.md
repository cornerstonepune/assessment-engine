# Buy the exports, keep the judgement

Of roughly seventy child-facing platforms checked across six subject areas, only a handful let a per-child, per-skill result leave the vendor in a form Cornerstone's engine can ingest, and that single test settles most of the buying. For mathematics the recommendation is **IXL** first (CSV export from every analytics report, skills already organised by Indian class, no student-facing generative AI), **Khan Academy** as the free NCERT-mapped practice layer with a Districts enquiry for its nightly All Skills CSV, and **Mindspark** only if Educational Initiatives contracts to deliver the per-child export it does not publish today; for coding, **Code.org**, which is free, pseudonymous by design and exports a level-by-level CSV, with Scratch as a creative complement whose evidence is teacher-entered. For English, **Lexia Core5/PowerUp** is the only reading product with a nightly per-activity feed (SFTP CSV, OneRoster 1.2 and LTI 1.3 certified) and **Quill Premium** the only grammar product with a per-skill report and raw CSV at a published price ($115 per teacher per year); **Microsoft Reading Progress** is the free tool for teacher-run oral reading checks in English, Hindi and Marathi, and **No More Marking** measures writing quality from the scanned scripts the school already captures. For Hindi and Marathi no purchasable product carries a per-child feed; the path is Reading Progress now and a partnership approach to IIT Bombay's TARA. In science the right purchases are content-only — **PhET**, one of **Mystery Science** or **Generation Genius**, and **Arduino Science Journal** with kits — because the teacher marks the investigation and the school's own capture path records it, so nothing needs exporting. Social science should not run on adaptive item practice at all: every strong Grades 3–8 result comes from knowledge-rich sequences, document-based reading, argument-writing apprenticeship and projects, all teacher-delivered and rubric-assessed; platforms are confined to the NCERT/DIKSHA spine, open geography and economics data, and a thin nickname-mode **Kahoot!** retrieval quiz returned over LTI 1.3. No vendor anywhere offers xAPI, Caliper or OneRoster gradebook return, so every integration is a file drop or an LTI grade passback into a school-built adapter with a school-owned crosswalk table; every imported result is a score or mastery level that must be labelled score-only because none carries the school's three signals; and every vendor holding child data needs a DPDP-compliant processing agreement and verifiable parental consent before the children's provisions of the DPDP Rules commence on **13 May 2027** — no vendor page opened mentions the Act. The publicly priced parts of the shortlist come to roughly **US$11,000–13,000 a year for 200 children**, before quotes from IXL, Mindspark and No More Marking.

## Only seven products let a per-skill result leave the vendor

The market's plumbing runs one way. Rostering standards — Clever, ClassLink and OneRoster — are near-universal, but they move the class list from the school into the vendor and return nothing; not one maths, literacy or science platform documents OneRoster Gradebook, xAPI or Caliper for sending results back. Khan Academy syncs with Clever and ClassLink "every weeknight" ([Khan Academy Help Center](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/360031158952-How-does-automated-rostering-work-for-Khan-Academy-Districts-accounts)), DreamBox insists that "All new students should be managed via the data sync with Clever or ClassLink" ([DreamBox Learning Help](https://dreamboxlearning.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/27281407686163-DreamBox-Math-Managing-Classroom-Rosters-and-Logins-if-your-District-Uses-Clever-or-ClassLink-Educators)), and Mathspace's Clever link is an SSO plus nightly roster sync in which "ALL class management and rostering ... [is] managed by the district" ([Mathspace Help Center](https://help.mathspace.co/en/articles/1135697-mathspace-and-clever-integration)). The two return paths that exist are a downloadable file and LTI 1.3 Assignment and Grade Services, and the second returns a score per assignment rather than per skill unless the school assigns one skill per task.

Measured against Cornerstone's first rule, seven products clear the bar in some form. Khan Academy's Districts tier publishes the richest dictionary: an "All Skills CSV" whose columns carry "Skill name", "Current skill mastery level (Attempted, Familiar, Proficient, or Mastered)", "Skills Mastery Points", "Learning Time on Skill" and "Standards (Common Core and/or applicable state standards linked to this skill)" per student, updated nightly ([Khan Academy Help Center](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/17167578991629-CSV-Data-Dictionary-for-Administrator-Reports)) — but only for Districts customers with "a minimum of 250 licenses" ([Khan Academy Blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/becoming-a-khan-academy-districts-partner/)). IXL lets an administrator "click the 'export' button on any report to download a shareable .CSV file" from Skill Usage and Skill Proficiency reports that show questions answered, time and a SmartScore per skill ([IXL Official Blog](https://blog.ixl.com/2023/04/22/ixl-district-analytics-two-new-reports/)), and states that it "is IMS Global LTI Advantage certified and uses LTI v1.3" with grade passback ([IXL Canvas integration sheet](https://www.ixl.com/materials/us/Canvas_integration.pdf)). Code.org now lets teachers "download your students' progress as a CSV file ... the complete dataset with level-by-level details" ([Code.org Support](https://support.code.org/hc/en-us/articles/25195525766669-Getting-Started-New-Progress-View)). Lexia's automated export delivers, "nightly via CSV files uploaded to a secure FTP site", one row per student per activity with `reading_focus`, `accuracy`, `rate`, `current_unit`, `levelname` and Skill Check percentages ([Lexia Automated Data Exports](https://www.lexialearningresources.com/lrtraining/documentation/MyLexia_AutomatedExport.pdf)), and myLexia holds active 1EdTech certificates for OneRoster 1.2 REST rostering, OneRoster 1.1 CSV import and LTI 1.3 ([1EdTech](https://site.imsglobal.org/certifications/lexia-learning-systems/mylexia)). Quill Premium adds a Skills Report and a "Data Export — Raw student data in CSV format" ([Quill Support](https://support.quill.org/en/articles/5578691-what-data-reports-does-quill-offer)). Kahoot! is 1EdTech-certified for "LTI 1.3", "LTI Advantage", "LTI Deep Linking 2.0" and "LTI Assessment and Grade Services 2.0" with "automatic grade passback" ([Kahoot blog](https://kahoot.com/blog/2024/07/23/1edtech-certifies-kahoot-interoperability/)). No More Marking returns "Pupil-level scaled scores / marks" as "CSV-downloadable data" ([No More Marking](https://www.nomoremarking.com/)) — a holistic writing measure, not per-skill mastery, but one the school can store per child.

Everything else is dashboard-only or unverifiable. Eedi's export downloads "overall class average scores and misconceptions stats" — class level, not child level ([Eedi Help Centre](https://help.eedi.co.uk/en/articles/4397506-managing-classes-students-and-teachers)). Mindspark's public footprint describes a student-facing "topic map" with progress and accuracy and no school export or API ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.mindspark.edicine&hl=en_IN)). Microsoft's own moderator confirms that Reading Progress data "can export data to Excel ... Class wise only" with no API ([Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/5226320/how-do-you-get-the-reading-fluency-insight-data-ou)). Google's Read Along in Classroom help page describes rich per-student accuracy, comprehension and speed views and no export at all ([Read Along in Google Classroom help](https://support.google.com/readalong/answer/14174515?hl=en)). Math Academy, Synthesis Tutor, Scratch, Cuemath, Bhanzu and Codingal export nothing per skill; Math Academy's teacher feature is an "oversight panel" to "share a student's progress" ([Math Academy FAQ](https://www.mathacademy.com/faq)), and Scratch's policy describes teacher accounts that create student accounts but no reporting function ([Scratch Privacy Policy](https://mitscratch.freshdesk.com/en/support/solutions/articles/4000219339-privacy-policy)).

Two consequences shape every recommendation below. First, the export gate is also the pseudonymity gate: a file keyed by the school's own `child_id` used as the vendor username is the only way a result comes home without a name ever leaving the building, so any vendor whose account model demands a legal name and date of birth — DreamBox requires "name, date of birth, classroom name, class year" ([DreamBox Privacy](https://www.dreambox.com/privacy)), Synthesis Teams "first and last name and date of birth" ([Synthesis Privacy Policy](https://www.synthesis.com/privacy)) — fails two rules at once. Second, none of the export dictionaries opened (Khan, IXL, Code.org, Lexia, Quill) carries anything resembling Cornerstone's three signals; every imported row is a score, a mastery level or a completion flag, and the evidence record must mark it as such rather than fold it into counts of blank, wrong and wrong-with-working.

## Maths and coding: IXL and Code.org clear the gate; Khan and Mindspark need contracts

Scores below use one scale for every subject: **2** = documented and fits the rule, **1** = exists with a material limitation, **0** = absent or contradicts the rule, **?** = not verifiable on any page opened (counted as 0). Columns are the eight buying criteria: per-skill export (Exp), child-facing AI limits (AI), skill map and grade coverage for an Indian school (Map), independent efficacy (Evid), data protection (Priv), teacher view and assignment control (Tchr), public price (Cost) and practicalities (Prac). The export column is a gate, not a weight: a 0 there means practice-only use at most, whatever the total.

IXL wins on the criterion that matters because its export needs no district-scale contract and its content is already cut to Indian classes: IXL India runs "LKG through Class XII", states that "All of IXL's skills correspond to the Indian national curriculum" and publishes skill lists per class, from 55 skills at LKG to 300 at Class XI ([IXL India](https://in.ixl.com/)), which is exactly what a crosswalk to Cornerstone's skill ids needs. Its evidence is real but modest: a cluster-randomised trial in Holland Public Schools (grades 3–5, 263 treatment and 282 control students) found "approximately 10-point larger gains on the Renaissance Star Math assessment", an effect size of **0.13 SD**, with no sponsor ([ERIC ED663581](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED663581)). IXL shows no student-facing generative AI on any page opened, which satisfies the AI rule by absence. The unknowns are all contractual: IXL's children's privacy policy renders only by JavaScript, its landing page says student data is "governed by our agreements with the School and/or District" and that "IXL does not permit children under the age of 13 to create an account" for parent accounts ([IXL Privacy Policy](https://www.ixl.com/privacypolicy)), and neither the school price nor whether a classroom licence includes CSV export could be confirmed. Those three points are the purchase conditions.

Khan Academy is the strongest free layer and the most awkward purchase. Its maths is "mapped to India's NCERT curriculum" in Hindi as well as English ([Khan Academy Blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/khan-academy-in-hindi/)), and its per-skill export is the best documented in the market — but only in the Districts tier, which requires 250 licences and Clever or ClassLink rostering, and whose student Khanmigo is for "US-based districts only" ([Khan Academy Blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/becoming-a-khan-academy-districts-partner/)). That last restriction is a benefit under Cornerstone's AI rule: in Pune, children on Khan Academy see no generative tutor unless a parent buys the consumer plan at "$4/month" or "$44/year" ([Khanmigo pricing](https://www.khanmigo.ai/pricing)), and Khanmigo is in any case built to "never give you the answer" ([Khanmigo for learners](https://www.khanmigo.ai/learners)). Its efficacy numbers are the vendor's own: an internal quasi-experimental study of about 350,000 students found 30-plus minutes a week "associated with ~20% greater-than-expected learning gains" on MAP Growth ([Khan Academy Blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/khan-academy-efficacy-results-november-2024/)), and a difference-in-differences study of 181,000 students found recommended use associated with "0.26 standard deviations higher" growth ([ERIC ED624104](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED624104)) — dosage-conditional associations, not causal estimates. Privacy is mixed: Districts accounts come with "Khan Academy does not allow large language models (LLMs) to train their models using student data" ([Khan Academy Help Center](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/36808726375693-How-is-my-child-s-privacy-and-security-protected-in-a-Khan-Academy-Districts-partnership)), yet Common Sense rates the service 78% with a "Warning" because user information "is used to track and target advertisements on other third-party websites" ([Common Sense Privacy](https://privacy.commonsense.org/evaluation/Khan-Academy)). The recommendation is to use the free tier as practice whose evidence enters only by teacher entry, and to ask Khan Academy in writing whether it will sell Districts, without Khanmigo, to an Indian school below 250 licences; Districts is priced at "$15 per student" with Khanmigo and "$10 per student" for MAP Learning Paths without ([Khan Academy Blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/becoming-a-khan-academy-districts-partner/)).

Mindspark is the only candidate with an Indian randomised trial, Indian curriculum alignment for every grade the school will run and an Indian data controller, and it still cannot be bought as it stands. The AER lottery study of after-school centres in Delhi found maths scores "0.37 sigma higher" and Hindi "0.23 sigma higher" after 4.5 months, with "much greater relative gains for academically-weaker students" ([American Economic Review](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20171112)) — the outlier in a field whose US effects cluster near 0.1 SD, though it was a centre programme with 45 minutes of software and 45 minutes of small-group teaching per session at about INR 1,000 per child per month ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/disrupting-education-evidence-technology-aided-instruction-india)), not in-school tablet use. The product is "aligned to CBSE, ICSE and IGCSE syllabus" for grades 1–10 ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.mindspark.edicine&hl=en_IN)) and EdTech Tulna lists it for Grades 3–5 and 6–8 as CBSE, English-medium adaptive learning ([EdTech Tulna](https://www.edtechtulna.org/ei-mindspark-english-maths-cbse-6-8)). Against that, no export or API is documented anywhere public, and Educational Initiatives' privacy policy rests on "Section 43A of the Information Technology Act, 2000", collects "name, age, gender, phone number and email address", and permits transfer to a body corporate "located in India or any other country" with no mention of DPDP ([Ei Study Privacy Policy](https://ei-study.com/privacy_policy/)). The condition is therefore a contract, not a checkbox: a weekly per-child, per-topic export (child_id, topic or unit id, attempts, accuracy, mastery state, date) by CSV or JSON over SFTP or a signed URL, pseudonymous rostering, hosting fixed in India, and a DPA that names DPDP obligations. Until EI signs, Mindspark is home practice whose results are not evidence.

Two reserves deserve a sentence each. Mathspace has the market's only documented per-task AI off-switch — "Your teacher may choose to turn off Milo Chat when assigning tasks to you" — with every chat visible to teachers, Azure OpenAI as the model service ([Mathspace Help Center](https://help.mathspace.co/en/articles/8303305-mathspace-personal-tutor)), a moderation ladder that escalates bans and notifies teachers with transcripts ([Mathspace Blog](https://blog.mathspace.co/miloai-just-got-safer-how-our-new-chat-moderation-system-keeps-students-protected/)), India among its eight curriculum regions ([Mathspace](https://mathspace.co/textbooks/syllabuses/select)) and transparent pricing at "$8 Classes" or "$15 Schools" per student per year ([Mathspace Premium plans](https://mathspace.co/us/about-premium)); it scores highest overall and still sits behind IXL because its return path is Canvas LTI grade passback plus in-product Skills and Mastery reports with no CSV or API ([Mathspace Help Center](https://help.mathspace.co/lms-integrations)), and its efficacy page could not be opened. Eedi is the opposite profile: a two-year independent RCT in Years 7–8 reporting "d = 0.30 (p = 0.004)" at 24 months, with a sample that shrank from about 3,450 to 1,192 pupils ([Eedi news](https://www.eedi.com/news/two-year-rct-earns-eedi-a-gold-efficacy-certificate-from-eduevidence)), a Socratic tutor that "activates after specific errors" ([Eedi](https://eedi.com)), and a misconception map of "more than 8,000 common maths misconceptions" released under CC BY 4.0 ([Eedi](https://eedi.com)) that could be mounted directly onto Cornerstone's misconception table; but its export is class averages, so it feeds the school's item bank, not its evidence record.

The exclusions are principled. DreamBox has two RCTs and an Evidence for ESSA "strong" rating at +0.10 ([Evidence for ESSA](https://www.evidenceforessa.org/program/dreambox-learning/)) but requires name and date of birth. Prodigy's own educator page describes free access funded by "optional parent memberships" ([Prodigy for Educators](https://www.prodigygame.com/main-en/teachers)), the Johns Hopkins evaluation was correlational and estimated "roughly 888 questions in the game" per one-point assessment gain ([Prodigy evaluation](https://marketing.prodigygame.com/Prodigy-Evaluation-Key-Findings.pdf)), and Fairplay counted "16 ads for membership and only 4 math problems" in 19 minutes ([Fairplay](https://fairplayforkids.org/pf/prodigy/)). Zearn's RAND RCT (+0.11 SD on MAP in grades 3–5) is good evidence ([Zearn](https://about.zearn.org/research)), but its objectives are US K–8 and its "Export Center" fields are undocumented. Matific's headline "34%" comes from a Western Sydney University case study "sponsored by Slate Science" whose authors say "it is not possible to claim whether or how much the use of Matific influenced this outcome" ([Matific research booklet](https://www.matific.com/home/resources/media/documents/Booklet-Academic_Research_by_Western_Sydney_University.pdf)). Cuemath at ₹610–900 per class plus platform fee ([Cuemath Pricing](https://www.cuemath.com/en-in/pricing/)) and Bhanzu at "$160 per month" ([Bhanzu](https://bhanzu.com/news/bhanzu-launches-monthly-math-plans-for-us-families)) are tutoring services an order of magnitude dearer per child than any platform, and a tutor's notes are not a data feed.

**Integration pattern, maths.** IXL: roster by CSV upload with `child_id` as the username and no real names or dates of birth; an administrator downloads the Skill Proficiency or Skill Usage CSV on a fixed cadence (there is no API); an n8n flow hands the file to an engine importer that maps IXL skill ids to Cornerstone skill ids through a seeded crosswalk table and appends one evidence row per skill with source, SmartScore and questions answered, flagged score-only. Khan Academy Districts, if sold: rostering must go through Clever or ClassLink, so the roster pushed there carries `child_id` display names only; the nightly All Skills CSV is the return path, mapped by Khan skill name and Common Core code, because course mastery does not sync to any LMS. Mindspark: the pattern is whatever the contract specifies, and nothing is built until it does. Mathspace or IXL over LTI 1.3 grade passback returns one score per task, so the school would have to assign one skill per task and run an LTI platform of its own — a build that is not worth making for maths alone.

**Cost, maths.** Public prices are Mathspace at $8 or $15 per student per year with one free class of 30 ([Mathspace Premium plans](https://mathspace.co/us/about-premium)), Khan Academy free with Districts at $10–15 per student under a 250-licence floor, Eedi's free tier ([Eedi for Teachers](https://www.eedischool.com/us)) and Mindspark at an unexplained "₹1,000-5,000" ([CSRBOX](https://www.csrbox.org/India_CSR_products_Mindspark_55)); IXL's school and India prices render only by JavaScript and were not obtained.

**Gaps, maths.** IXL's and Khan Academy's full privacy policies (hosting, username-only accounts, AI-training clauses); whether Khan Districts is sold in India at all; Mindspark's dashboard, export, price and skill list; 1EdTech certification pages for IXL, Mathspace and ALEKS, which render by JavaScript, so IXL's LTI 1.3 claim rests on IXL's own sheet; and whether any maths export distinguishes blank from wrong — none opened does.

Code.org is the only coding platform that satisfies the export rule, and it satisfies the pseudonymity and AI rules almost by accident of geography. Teachers create accounts "with picture passwords or secret word passwords", the service collects a "Display Name (e.g., 'Cool Coder' or 'John')" and an age rather than a birthdate, runs on US servers, "does not display targeted advertising" and, as a nonprofit, exceeds COPPA without being bound by it ([Code.org Privacy Policy](https://code.org/privacy)). Its AI Tutor is Socratic — "asking guiding questions rather than giving away answers" — but "In Web Lab ... it can also generate code directly", is restricted so that "Only users in the United States have access", is "not available to students under 11", and its interactions are "automatically deleted after 90 days" and never used to train third-party models ([Code.org AI Tutor FAQ](https://support.code.org/hc/en-us/articles/40542019587213-AI-Tutor-FAQ)). For a school in Pune that means no child-facing generative AI at all, which is the outcome the rule wants, achieved by default rather than by a switch the school controls. The export is per lesson and level rather than per skill: the CSV offers "a lesson summary showing completion percentages or the complete dataset with level-by-level details", with time spent and last update per level ([Code.org Support](https://support.code.org/hc/en-us/articles/25195525766669-Getting-Started-New-Progress-View)). Because Code.org courses are fixed sequences, a course–lesson–level to Cornerstone-coding-skill crosswalk is buildable once; its CSTA alignment page was not opened, so the school should express its coding skill map in CSTA terms to reuse whatever mapping Code.org publishes. The weakness is evidence: the platform's evaluation URL now serves its general about page, and the only independent study found is a correlational paper on 20 primary pupils ([arXiv 2412.14180](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14180)). No coding platform in this survey has a randomised trial, so the choice rests on data return, privacy and curriculum fit.

CodeMonkey is the structured, auto-graded alternative from Pre-K to Grade 8 with username-and-password student accounts, a PRIVO COPPA safe-harbour certificate, AWS hosting in an unstated region, and an AI widget whose images "are not collected or stored" ([CodeMonkey Privacy Policy](https://www.codemonkey.com/privacy-policy/)); every teacher plan includes a classroom dashboard and automatic grading, but School and District plans are "Custom Plan" with pricing on request ([CodeMonkey Pricing](https://app.codemonkey.com/plans)) and no export is documented. The condition is blunt: write a per-student CSV export into the purchase order or do not buy. Scratch stays as the creative complement it has always been. It costs nothing, has an offline editor, and ScratchJr is a tablet app for ages 5–7 ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/scratchjr/id895485086)); but teacher-created student accounts need "username, password, birthdate, gender, and country of residence", and the Foundation "may use AI ... to train AI models" ([Scratch Privacy Policy](https://mitscratch.freshdesk.com/en/support/solutions/articles/4000219339-privacy-policy)). There is no progress data to export, so Scratch evidence is a project rubric entered by the educator against the school's own coding skills — Pattern A below.

Tynker and CodeCombat fail on the rules rather than on quality. Tynker Copilot lets children aged 6–12 "convert their ideas into block code" and "receive block code outputs with step-by-step instructions" on a fine-tuned Llama 2 model ([Tynker press release](https://www.tynker.com/about/press/2023/10-introducing-tynker-copilot-the-first-ever-llm-powered-coding-companion-for-young-coders)) — generated code, not hints, with no school off-switch described — and Common Sense Privacy's April 2026 evaluation finds "Personal information is sold or rented to third parties" ([Common Sense Privacy](https://privacy.commonsense.org/evaluation/tynker)). CodeCombat allows "your first name or pseudonym, last initial" ([CodeCombat Privacy](https://codecombat.com/privacy)) and shows student code in its dashboard ([CodeCombat Teacher Dashboard](https://codecombat.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/4405250882327-Teacher-Dashboard-Overview)), but carries a Common Sense "Warning" with six "unclear" findings ([Common Sense Privacy](https://privacy.commonsense.org/evaluation/CodeCombat)), and its AI HackStack has students "play and prompt from the very start" ([CodeCombat](https://codecombat.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/38195349234199-AI-HackStack-Getting-Started-Guide)). Kodable's game "does not collect any personally identifiable information from children" ([Kodable Privacy Center](https://www.kodable.com/privacy)) and its site licences are transparent at "$1750/year", "$2500/year" and "$3500/year" ([Kodable Plans](https://www.kodable.com/plans)), but it stops at Grade 5. Codingal sells live one-to-one courses with a "Master dashboard" and no named standard ([Codingal School Partnership](https://www.codingal.com/en-us/school-partnership/)).

**Integration pattern, coding.** Code.org: the teacher creates a section per class; children log in with pictures or secret words and their display name is the school's `child_id`; the level-by-level CSV per section is downloaded on a cadence and mapped by a seeded course–lesson–level crosswalk; a level Code.org marks as a "Spotlighted assessment opportunit[y]" ([Code.org Support](https://support.code.org/hc/en-us/articles/25195525766669-Getting-Started-New-Progress-View)) becomes an evidence row only after the educator has looked at the submitted project and approved it — completion is not mastery. CodeMonkey: username-only accounts; return path does not exist until contracted. Scratch: no integration; rubric entry.

**Cost, coding.** Code.org and Scratch are free; Kodable's site licences are public but out of grade range; CodeMonkey, Tynker, CodeCombat and Codingal quote privately.

**Gaps, coding.** Code.org's CSTA alignment page and Common Sense rating; CodeCombat's price, CSV export and Clever scope; Tynker's and Kodable's AI off-switches; CodeMonkey's per-student price and any export; anything about Tekie beyond its acquisition by Uolo ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/buzz/b2b-edtech-startup-uolo-acquires-online-learning-platform-tekie/)).

Lexia is the one reading product whose feed can populate a per-skill record without a teacher retyping anything. Its Core5 Detailed Student Export carries, per student per activity, `activity_name`, `reading_focus` (the component of reading), `struggling_indicator`, `accuracy`, `rate`, `percent_completed`, `current_unit`, `levelname` (1–21), `grade_level_material` and `Activity_Skill_Check` percent correct, keyed by `lexia_id`, `username` and a district-supplied `studentrefid`, delivered nightly by SFTP and kept for six days ([Lexia Automated Data Exports](https://www.lexialearningresources.com/lrtraining/documentation/MyLexia_AutomatedExport.pdf)). Evidence for ESSA rates Core5 "Moderate" across four studies and 17,281 students with an average effect of **+0.16**, the largest a matched comparison of 6,000-plus pupils in grades 3–5 at +0.11 on NWEA MAP ([Evidence for ESSA](https://www.evidenceforessa.org/program/lexia-core5-reading/)); a cluster RCT of 115 K–5 students with reading disabilities met WWC standards with reservations and showed an improvement index of **+8 percentile points** on MAP ([WWC Study 91258](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/91258)); and PowerUp, the Grades 6–12 product, has a cluster-randomised trial of 155 below-35th-percentile middle-schoolers at **+0.36** on STAR Reading ([Evidence for ESSA](https://www.evidenceforessa.org/program/lexia-powerup-literacy/)). It is adaptive without open chat and no generative AI is documented. Public pricing exists because Louisiana publishes its contract: Core5 at **$40 per student** (1–249), $35 (250–499), $30 (500+) or a **$9,500 site licence**, with persistent internet at about 0.4 Mbps per student and Chrome OS, Windows, macOS or iPad — no Android ([Louisiana DOE price list](https://doe.louisiana.gov/docs/default-source/curricular-resources/price-list---lexia-learning-systems-llc---core-reading---ela-intensive-reading-intervention-grades-3-5.pdf?sfvrsn=3c2b6218_0)). Three conditions stand between this and a purchase: Lexia's international page names partners only for the UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand, with India appearing only in a contact-form list ([Lexia international](https://www.lexialearning.com/company/international)); its privacy policy places data "on servers located in the United States", lists minimum student fields of "first name, last name, username, grade, school, and instructional language" and reserves de-identified data for "product improvement or development ... and educational research" ([Lexia Application Privacy Policy](https://legal.lexialearning.com/legal/application-privacy.html)) — the name fields are field labels, not a legal-name requirement, so filling them with school pseudonyms is plausible but must be confirmed in contract; and a DPA must name DPDP with US hosting acknowledged. Its levels and six reading components are US-standard, so the crosswalk to Cornerstone's reading skills is the school's to build and maintain.

It reports accuracy rate and correct words per minute, categorises errors as omissions, insertions, mispronunciations, repetitions and self-corrections, and lets a teacher overrule every marked error; its language auto-detect is "a PREVIEW feature" for 70-plus languages and Microsoft warns that pronunciation detection "may not recognize accents and dialects well" ([Microsoft Reading Progress](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/education/learning-accelerators/getting-started-with-reading-progress-in-teams)), with Hindi, Marathi and English (India) in the auto-detect list ([Microsoft auto-detect settings](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/topic/ensure-auto-detect-preview-in-reading-progress-uses-your-intended-language-7e4b5cca-77ae-4748-8f52-8b654c720d58)). Reading Coach, the child-facing companion, processes voice "only on a user's device", stores nothing with Microsoft ([Microsoft Reading Coach](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/topic/getting-started-with-reading-coach-4bb004e9-2eda-4ee5-b3d6-ca10bc42f6cb)), lists hi-IN, mr-IN and en-IN among its 81 locales ([Reading Coach language settings](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/topic/reading-coach-language-settings-9ae2fd8f-46cc-424b-b1da-79b017cf0387)), and its generative "Create a Story" can be disabled tenant-wide by an administrator ([Reading Coach IT documentation](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/topic/it-documentation-for-reading-coach-d56960aa-6598-49df-98a4-9022e243e139)) — the cleanest off-switch in the language market. The evidence is vendor-commissioned: a matched comparison of 1,404 Fresno students moved i-Ready scores from the 50th to the 54th percentile ([Microsoft Education blog](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/education/blog/2024/08/new-study-shows-improved-reading-scores-by-using-reading-progress/)). Its data path is a weekly per-class Excel export by a teacher, not an API, which is workable as a watched file drop.

Google Read Along is the only free child-facing reading buddy in Marathi and Hindi — its language list runs Arabic, Bangla, English, Gujarati, Hindi, Marathi, Portuguese, Spanish, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu ([Read Along languages](https://support.google.com/readalong/answer/12281788?hl=en&co=GENIE.Platform%3DAndroid)) — and its Classroom dashboards show per-student accuracy, the "top 10 words that the selected student struggled with", comprehension and speed, with cross-assignment tracking gated to a paid Workspace edition ([Read Along in Google Classroom help](https://support.google.com/readalong/answer/14174515?hl=en)). Its 2019 Bolo pilot with ASER Centre in 200 Uttar Pradesh villages reported that "64 percent of children who used the app showed an improvement in reading proficiency in three months" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/03/06/google-introduces-educational-app-bolo-to-improve-childrens-literacy-rates-in-india/)), a Google-run pilot rather than a peer-reviewed trial. With no export, its results cannot flow back, so it is optional practice whose evidence is nil. Epic's 45% Common Sense "Warning" for personalised advertising and tracking ([Common Sense Privacy](https://privacy.commonsense.org/evaluation/Epic---Kids-Books--Reading)) excludes it.

Quill is the best fit on the decisive criterion at the lowest cost. ### Automatic speech recognition on Indian children's voices is unvalidated, so scores enter as provisional

No reading-tutor vendor in the survey publishes an accuracy figure for children's speech, and the independent benchmarks that exist point one way. AI4Bharat's Svarah set shows Indian-accented adult English roughly tripling Whisper large's error rate — **7.2% WER against 2.7%** on LibriSpeech clean — with Azure's Indian-English model at 21.3% and Google's at 20.7% ([Svarah](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15760)). The one peer-reviewed study of Indian children aged 5–15 with Hindi or Marathi as first language reached "accuracy of above 95%" only with a custom model and pronunciation dictionary ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10209-025-01225-3)), and the ASER reading-level dataset of 81,330 clips from 5,301 children in Hindi, Marathi and English supported an 86%-accurate English reading-level classifier ([arXiv 1912.04381](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.04381)). Even human raters agreed at only 0.76 on comprehensibility ratings of 10–14-year-old Indian readers in IIT Bombay's corpus ([arXiv 2405.19426](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19426)), which is the ceiling any automatic scorer is measured against. The design consequence follows Cornerstone's own rule that the graph reads only confirmed evidence: every speech-scored result from Reading Progress, Read Along or any Indian ORF tool enters as provisional until a teacher confirms it, and before any vendor's oral-reading numbers count as evidence the school runs its own validation of 20–30 children per grade against teacher-timed words correct per minute — a check no vendor supplies for Indian children.

**Integration pattern, English.** Lexia: the school pushes a roster as OneRoster 1.1 CSV with pseudonymous names and `studentrefid` = `child_id`; an n8n flow pulls the nightly SFTP files; an engine adapter crosswalks `activity_id`, `reading_focus` and `levelname` to Cornerstone reading skills through a seeded table and appends one evidence row per completed activity or Skill Check. Microsoft Reading Progress: the teacher exports the Insights Excel per class weekly into a watched Drive folder; the engine parses accuracy, words correct per minute and error categories into provisional rows that a teacher confirms. Quill: pseudonymous accounts rostered from Google Classroom; a monthly Skills Report plus Data Export CSV, concept-to-skill crosswalk, evidence rows flagged score-only. No More Marking: scripts already captured by the school's reading workflow are uploaded; the scaled-score CSV is stored as one holistic writing measure per child per window. Read Along: no integration; not evidence. CommonLit or Writable: per-assignment CSV, assignment-level only.

**Cost, English.** Lexia $30–40 per student or $9,500 per site (US list); Microsoft Reading Progress and Coach free within Teams for Education; Read Along free on Workspace for Education Fundamentals; Quill $115 per teacher per year; CommonLit free or $4,000 per school; No More Marking, Writable and NoRedInk undisclosed; Amira $20 per student for the suite on California's list ([CDE Amira overview](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ci/cl/amirainfooverview.asp)).

**Gaps, English.** Lexia's India route, pseudonymous fields and a sample export; Amira's export and API; Reading Eggs, Raz-Plus, NoRedInk, IXL ELA and Freadom export formats and prices (pages blocked or JavaScript-only); No More Marking's price, privacy policy and reliability paper; Quill's school price, AI off-switch and concept ids; any vendor accuracy figure for Indian children's speech.

Common Sense rates PhET "Pass, 92%" because "The service can be used without collecting any personal information from children or students" ([Common Sense Privacy](https://privacy.commonsense.org/evaluation/PhET-Interactive-Simulations)), and its research page cites team and third-party studies without effect sizes ([PhET Research](https://phet.colorado.edu/en/research)). The NCERT crosswalk is straightforward for Curiosity's chapters on magnets, states of water, circuits, heat transfer, light, forces, pressure, particulate matter, solutions and the Earth–Moon–Sun system ([Tiwari Academy Class 6 Science](https://www.tiwariacademy.com/ncert-solutions/class-6/science/); [Class 7](https://www.tiwariacademy.com/ncert-solutions/class-7/science/); [Class 8](https://www.tiwariacademy.com/ncert-solutions/class-8/science/)), though those chapter lists come from a secondary site and must be checked against the NCERT PDFs before the crosswalk is published.

For Grades 3–5, roughly half of "Our Wondrous World" is natural science — plants, animals, water, food, health, "How Things Work", "How Things are Made", "Our Sky", "Energy" ([Tiwari Academy Class 4 EVS](https://www.tiwariacademy.com/ncert-solutions/class-4/evs/); [Class 5](https://www.tiwariacademy.com/ncert-solutions/class-5/evs/)) — and either Mystery Science or Generation Genius can supply the phenomenon, video and investigation while the teacher runs it. Mystery Science sells a school membership at **"Science Only $2199"** or "$2999" with writing for K–5 with "hands-on investigations" and "written & 3D assessments" ([Mystery Science pricing](https://mysteryscience.com/pricing)); it "does not permit students to create user accounts and does not collect personal information from students", though it "retains the information it collects for an unrestricted period" and states no hosting region ([Discovery Education privacy](https://www.discoveryeducation.com/privacy-policy/)). Generation Genius charges **"$2395/yr"** for Science + Math + ELA with unlimited teachers and student links, or $250 for a single science classroom ([Generation Genius pricing](https://www.generationgenius.com/pricing/)), and its privacy URL now redirects to Newsela's US-hosted policy. Neither has an independent efficacy study, both are US-English, and the other half of EVS — family, mela, festivals, neighbourhood, "Our Vibrant Country" ([Tiwari Academy Class 3 EVS](https://www.tiwariacademy.com/ncert-solutions/class-3/evs/)) — is Indian and social, where only DIKSHA and school-made material apply. Run one of the two for a term against the chapter list and keep the one that covers more.

For the making and measurement chapters, Arduino Science Journal is "free, open-source", uses a phone's own magnetometer, sound and light sensors, and stores experiments locally unless a child signs in — Google Drive sync "is available to all users over the age of 14" ([Arduino Science Journal](https://www.arduino.cc/education/science-journal); [Arduino blog](https://blog.arduino.cc/2021/06/03/sync-google-drive-with-arduino-science-journal-projects/)) — so the exported data file is the child's own artefact and no vendor identity is created. Tinkercad carries a Common Sense "Pass, 78%" with no sale of personal information ([Common Sense Privacy](https://privacy.commonsense.org/evaluation/Tinkercad)). Smartivity's retail kits are priced in rupees — a "Mega Science Kit | 6-10 Years" at INR 899 and "My First Science Kit" at INR 549 ([Smartivity](https://www.smartivity.in/collections/science-experiment-kits/products.json?limit=12)) — and are materials, not a programme.

The reserves hold child data and therefore carry conditions. BrainPOP's Classroom plan is "$330/year" for Grades 3–8 with NGSS-aligned science and Clever, ClassLink, Google and Schoology rostering ([BrainPOP plans](https://www.brainpop.com/discover/plans)); its accounts require "full name, class, graduation year/grade, username, password" on "Servers located in the United States", it promises to "never share Personally Identifiable student data or use Student Records to train any third-party AI models" ([BrainPOP privacy](https://www.brainpop.com/about/privacy_policy/)), and its LTI product "doesn't have any active certifications" ([1EdTech](https://site.imsglobal.org/certifications/brainpop/brainpop-lti)). Stile is a full NGSS middle-school course with lab kits whose AI is teacher-facing draft feedback ([Stile](https://www.stileeducation.com/)); its policy requires "verifiable parental or guardian consent for every Student, regardless of age", bars its providers from training LLMs on student data and keeps Australian customers' data in Australia, but holds names and emails and publishes no price ([Stile privacy](https://www.curriculumassociates.com/privacy/stile)). Labster's LTI 1.3 passes back "final grade only" ([Labster help](https://help.labster.com/en/articles/6221165-schoology-lti-1-3-how-to-add-labster-simulations-to-your-course-as-an-educator)), its evidence is almost entirely undergraduate, and engagements "typically begin at $5,000 USD" ([Labster pricing](https://www.labster.com/pricing)) — the wrong level for Grades 3–8. Legends of Learning allows "first and last name or a nickname" and records right and wrong answers on US servers ([Legends privacy](https://www.legendsoflearning.com/privacy-policy/)) at $4,450 per school ([Legends pricing](https://www.legendsoflearning.com/pricing/)), but its export is unverified and its flagship study lists the company's founder among the authors. Tata ClassEdge claims 5,000-plus schools and CBSE, ICSE and state-board alignment with no pricing, languages, export or evidence ([Tata ClassEdge](https://www.tataclassedge.com/)); LEAD sells "curriculum, pedagogy, and technology" as one bundle ([LEAD](https://leadschool.in/)) and would compete with, rather than feed, Cornerstone's own engine; Byju's "is under insolvency proceedings in India" and its Android app was delisted in May 2025 over unpaid AWS bills ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byju%27s)). Gizmos could not be verified on any criterion because the vendor's sites returned 403 throughout.

**Integration pattern, science.** Pattern A throughout the shortlist: no vendor account; the child's worksheet or photograph from a PhET or investigation task goes through the school's existing read-mark-graph path and is recorded against the school's skill id; a Science Journal CSV or Tinkercad design image is attached to the child's evidence row by the teacher as an artefact. Process skills — fair-test design, measurement technique, safe handling — stay observation-checklist events the teacher records. BrainPOP or Stile, if ever adopted, are Pattern C: pseudonymous roster, CSV parsed by an adapter, verifiable parental consent per vendor because named account fields are mandatory.

**Cost, science.** PhET, Oak, Arduino Science Journal and Tinkercad are free; Mystery Science $2,199 or Generation Genius $2,395 per school per year; BrainPOP $330 per classroom; Legends $4,450 per school; Labster from $5,000; Smartivity INR 549–899 per kit; Stile, Gizmos, Tata ClassEdge and LEAD undisclosed.

**Gaps, science.** Everything about Gizmos; PhET's Hindi and Marathi translations and PhET-iO terms; any independent efficacy study for Mystery Science, Generation Genius, Stile or BrainPOP; Stile's per-student price and rostering; Legends' export format; Tata ClassEdge's and LEAD's data, hosting and AI safeguards.

## Social science: no evidence supports adaptive practice, so the model changes

The founders' suspicion is confirmed by the evidence and by the market. No Grades 3–8 randomised trial of a social-science software platform with achievement outcomes was found, the What Works Clearinghouse has no social-studies topic area at all ([WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/)), and every strong result in the subject is teacher-delivered and rubric-assessed. A kindergarten lottery into nine Core Knowledge charter schools produced reading gains in grades 3–6 of "ITT (0.241***) and TOT (0.473***)" and a grade-5 science effect of "ITT (0.15*)" ([EdWorkingPapers](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai23-755)). A cluster RCT of project-based second-grade social studies across 48 teachers in low-income districts found "higher growth in social studies and informational reading" ([ERIC EJ1284043](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1284043)), reported as "a 63-percent gain in social studies learning, translating to five to six months" ([Lucas Education Research](https://www.lucasedresearch.org/publication/the-impact-of-project-based-learning-on-social-studies-and-literacy-learning-in-low-income-schools/)). Reading Like a Historian, a document-based curriculum used for 42–72% of instruction time with 236 eleventh-graders, produced significant effects on historical thinking (partial η² = .09), transfer, factual knowledge and general reading comprehension ([Stanford](https://stacks.stanford.edu/file/druid:by786ht6640/a%20reisman_ReadingLikeaHistorian_CognitionandInstruction.pdf)). An 18-day Grade 8 apprenticeship in historical argument writing gave basic readers effects of **0.45** on historical writing, **0.32** on quality and **0.60** on length ([ERIC EJ1124455](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1124455)), and Johnson and Johnson's meta-analytic review of constructive controversy finds that "intellectual conflict can have important and positive effects on student learning" ([ERIC EJ883319](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ883319)). The one civics-game study is a 2011 pre/post design in which fourth-graders gained most ([ERIC EJ991517](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ991517)) and its 2024–25 successor reports attitudes only ([iCivics](https://vision.icivics.org/civics-isnt-a-given-and-other-exciting-takeaways-from-our-research/)). The outcomes that matter — extended writing, oral argument, source analysis — are judgement work, which under Cornerstone's own constitution belongs to the teacher with model-assisted reading at most.

NCF-SE says the same thing in its own words. Social science is assessed for "sourcing and interpreting evidence, tracing continuity and change, recognising spatial patterns", and "Assessment of engagement with current affairs must never be only on the basis of general knowledge" but "on specific situations, issues, or caselets" ([NCF-SE 2023](https://ncert.nic.in/pdf/NCFSE-2023-August_2023.pdf)). The market, meanwhile, offers content and quizzes, not adaptive skill engines: the tools that hold results (Kahoot, Newsela, BrainPOP) hold quiz and reading-check scores, and the country lock-in is severe — Oak and BBC Bitesize teach England's history, iCivics teaches the US constitution, Newsela publishes US texts — so only geography, economics and generic skills such as map reading, timelines and data interpretation transfer to "Exploring Society: India and Beyond".

### Where adaptive practice must not be used, and what stays with the teacher and the engine

Adaptive item practice has no place in the history and governance chapters — the beginnings of Indian civilisation, the Gupta era, the Marathas, the colonial era, panchayats, universal franchise, the parliamentary system — nor in the social half of Grades 3–5 EVS (family, mela, festivals, neighbourhood, "Our Vibrant Country", "Some Unique Places"), nor anywhere the outcome is reasoning with evidence, a caselet response, a debate or a project. No foreign platform covers that content, no platform anywhere assesses sourcing, contextualising or corroborating, and the risk of a quiz engine reducing "Exploring Society" to general knowledge is precisely what NCF-SE forbids. What a platform may do is narrow and named: deliver sequenced content and sources (NCERT/DIKSHA, Oak geography, Gapminder, Google Earth, QGIS); run low-stakes spaced retrieval on dates, places, terms and map features (Kahoot in nickname mode); and supply real data for investigations the teacher sets.

Everything else stays with the teacher: the document-based reading protocol with feedback on how a child sourced and corroborated; the modelled-then-faded apprenticeship in argument writing; structured controversy and debate; project units with an authentic audience; and the rubric judgement on each of these. The school's own curriculum engine holds the NCERT five-theme sequence and its skill map, the source packs and argument tasks as items, the rubrics, the retrieval schedule that Kahoot executes, and the evidence record itself — where the three-signal discipline generalises cleanly from maths to "no claim / claim without evidence / claim with evidence", each row confirmed by a teacher before the graph reads it.

**Integration pattern, social science.** Pattern A for the spine and data layers: no vendor account; the map, timeline, caselet or essay enters the school's capture path and is recorded against the school's skill id. Pattern B for Kahoot: the school authors quizzes whose question-to-skill tags live in its own item table, children join with a `child_id` alias as nickname, and results return either per activity over LTI 1.3 AGS (which requires the engine to act as an LTI platform) or per question through the downloadable report; an adapter maps nickname to `child_id` and appends evidence rows, and the report must be checked to confirm that an unanswered question is preserved as distinct from a wrong one before any of it counts.

**Cost, social science.** NCERT/DIKSHA, Oak, Gapminder, Dollar Street, QGIS free; Google Earth shows no charge but no price page was opened; Kahoot free to $228 per teacher per year; Newsela undisclosed; BrainPOP $330 per classroom.

**Gaps, social science.** Whether Kahoot reports preserve "unanswered"; Newsela's price and 1EdTech certifications; iCivics' privacy and account model (404); BBC's statement on non-UK access; Dollar Street's India coverage; the Class 7 chapter titles and Class 8 Part 2 list (only theme summaries opened); primary-source effect sizes for project-based learning and constructive controversy, which reached this survey only as snippets.

## What the school must build: three patterns, one crosswalk per vendor, consent per platform

Every shortlisted tool falls into one of three integration patterns, and the school's build is bounded accordingly. **Pattern A, content-only and teacher-assessed**, needs nothing new: PhET, Oak, DIKSHA, Google Earth, Gapminder, QGIS, Mystery Science, Generation Genius, Tinkercad, Science Journal, Scratch, Read Along and StoryWeaver create no child data at a vendor, so the worksheet, photograph, map or project rubric enters the existing read-mark-graph path against the school's own skill id, and DPDP exposure at the vendor is zero. **Pattern B, pseudonymous roster with LTI 1.3 grade passback**, is verified only for Kahoot's Assessment and Grade Services ([Kahoot blog](https://kahoot.com/blog/2024/07/23/1edtech-certifies-kahoot-interoperability/)) and, on the vendor's own word, for IXL ([IXL Canvas integration sheet](https://www.ixl.com/materials/us/Canvas_integration.pdf)) and Mathspace via Canvas ([Mathspace Help Center](https://help.mathspace.co/lms-integrations)); it requires the engine to stand up as an LTI platform — login initiation, key set, deep linking, line items and results — and returns one score per activity, so it is worth building only if Kahoot's downloadable report proves insufficient or a second vendor would share the endpoint. **Pattern C, pseudonymous roster with a file export**, is the workhorse and comes in three variants: a vendor push (Lexia's nightly SFTP CSV), a scheduled manual download by an administrator or teacher (IXL's report CSV, Khan Districts' All Skills CSV, Code.org's level CSV, Quill's Data Export, Microsoft's per-class Excel, No More Marking's scaled scores, Writable's and CommonLit's per-assignment files) and a contracted feed that does not yet exist (Mindspark, CodeMonkey).

Pattern C dictates the engineering. For each vendor the school builds one adapter that parses and validates the file and imports idempotently on vendor row id, `child_id` and date; one seeded crosswalk table from the vendor's skill, level or activity identifier to a Cornerstone skill id, carrying provenance and a version so that a vendor renaming a skill cannot silently re-point evidence; and one flow that watches a Drive folder or polls the SFTP site, hands the file to the engine's import endpoint and notifies on failure, with no logic of its own. Evidence rows carry the source, the vendor's own score fields and a score-only flag, because no export dictionary opened — Khan's ([Khan Academy Help Center](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/17167578991629-CSV-Data-Dictionary-for-Administrator-Reports)), IXL's, Code.org's, Lexia's ([Lexia Automated Data Exports](https://www.lexialearningresources.com/lrtraining/documentation/MyLexia_AutomatedExport.pdf)) or Quill's — distinguishes a blank from a wrong answer; the graph may read such rows as "practised" and "level reached" but never as counts of blank, wrong and wrong-with-working. Speech-scored rows from Reading Progress enter provisional and are confirmed by a teacher. Rostering always runs school to vendor: a CSV upload — OneRoster 1.1 CSV where the vendor accepts it, as Lexia does ([1EdTech](https://site.imsglobal.org/certifications/lexia-learning-systems/mylexia)) — with `child_id` as username and display name, class labels only, no legal name and no date of birth. The vendor is never the source of truth for who is in the class.

The legal frame turns these findings into contract clauses. The DPDP Rules were notified on 14 November 2025 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Personal_Data_Protection_Rules,_2025)) with an 18-month phase-in for "consent notices, purpose limitation ... children's data requirements, retention/erasure workflows, and security safeguards" ([India Briefing](https://www.india-briefing.com/news/dpdp-rules-2025-india-data-protection-law-compliance-40769.html/)), which the earlier council work dated to **13 May 2027** ([Rule 1](https://dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule1.html)). The Act requires verifiable parental consent before processing any under-18's data and forbids "tracking or behavioural monitoring of children" ([Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/98869575/)); Rule 12 lifts those two duties for the classes of fiduciary in Part A of the Fourth Schedule, which includes educational institutions, for the purposes in Part B ([dpdpa.com Rule 12](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule12.html)), and the Schedule confines that relief to monitoring "for the educational activities of such institution; or in the interests of safety of children enrolled" ([Fourth Schedule](https://dpdpa.com/schedule/schedule4.html)) — read on a mirror because the ministry's site was unreachable. Cornerstone's own record, keyed by `child_id` with names held apart, sits inside that exemption; a foreign vendor holding accounts for the same children does not, and a pseudonymous username tied to a persistent identifier is still personal data. The Supreme Court did not wait for 2027 when it barred sharing APAAR data with private entities in September 2026 and applied DPDP provisions immediately ([Medianama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/09/223-indias-apaar-student-parents/)). Four clauses therefore go into every purchase: a per-platform consent recorded per child under the Rule 10 flow ([Rule 10](https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule10.html)), with the lawful basis tagged on each data element; a processing agreement that names the DPDP Act and states the hosting region; pseudonymous rostering written into the account model; and a specified export with format, fields, cadence and transport. No vendor page opened mentions the Act — the two Indian vendors' policies rest on the IT Act 2000, and Educational Initiatives' permits transfer to "any other country" ([Ei Study Privacy Policy](https://ei-study.com/privacy_policy/)) — and penalties for breaching the children's obligations reach ₹200 crore ([PRS](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/digital-personal-data-protection-bill-2023)).

Some gaps run across every subject and could not be closed from public pages. No vendor mentions India's DPDP Act. No vendor offers xAPI, Caliper or OneRoster gradebook return, so any future claim of one should be checked in the 1EdTech directory, whose pages for IXL, Mathspace, ALEKS and Newsela render only by JavaScript and could not be read. Khan Academy's and IXL's full privacy policies were unreadable for the same reason, leaving hosting and username-only accounts unconfirmed. No accuracy figure exists for automatic scoring of Indian children's speech in any language. Gizmos could not be assessed at all. The NCERT chapter lists used for the crosswalks come from a secondary listing site and must be verified against the NCERT PDFs. And no import from any vendor will ever carry the school's three signals.

## Conclusion

The survey changes what a platform purchase is. Once the export gate is applied, a market of seventy products collapses to seven, and the same gate that protects the evidence record turns out to protect the children: a result can only come home keyed by the school's own identifier, so pseudonymity, data minimisation and DPDP consent stop being separate compliance tasks and become properties of the one integration design. The decisions that remain are contract clauses rather than feature comparisons — a specified export, pseudonymous account fields, a processing agreement that names the Act and the hosting region, and a written AI off-switch — and the school should draft that data annex once and attach it to every purchase, including the free ones. The engineering burden is deliberately small: one adapter, one versioned crosswalk and one file-watching flow per vendor, all reading files rather than APIs, which leaves the real work where it belongs, in the skill map the crosswalks point at.

Two implications reach beyond this year's buying. The geo-fencing that currently keeps Khanmigo and Code.org's tutor off children's screens in Pune is a vendor decision, not a school control, and it will erode as those tutors expand; the off-switch has to be contractual now, while the school still holds the leverage of a purchase. And the survey's only genuine white space is Indian-language literacy: nothing purchasable scores Marathi reading with a per-child feed, the government-scale assessors are not products, and the one validated Indian tool, IIT Bombay's TARA, is reachable only by partnership — which makes that approach, together with an in-house validation of speech scores against teacher-timed reading, a better use of effort than waiting for a vendor. Social science, finally, states the general rule that governs all of it: adaptive practice earns its place where an answer can be computed and verified, and where the answer is an argument the platform's job ends at content and retrieval, and the teacher's begins.
