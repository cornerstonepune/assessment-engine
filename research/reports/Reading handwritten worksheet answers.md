# Draw the answer box before reading it

Systems that reliably read handwritten answers on paper do not use a better reader than this engine. They avoid the engine's hardest job. Gradescope, Crowdmark, exam-board on-screen marking, census scanning and every credible 2025–26 research grader decide **where** each answer sits before any reading happens. They know this from a template: either a box drawn once on the blank sheet, or a box printed at fixed coordinates and located through corner marks or a QR code. Only then do they read one small answer on its own, and anything doubtful goes to a person. None of them works out the layout from the ink, which is what the engine's geometry rules do. That difference explains most of the engine's trouble: **94 of its 198 logged failures are about location, not reading**, and all three of this week's experiments changed the reading or the confidence floor rather than the location step. The accuracy evidence is thinner than vendors suggest. Forms designed for machines reach about 99% on handwritten digits and 99.85% on tick boxes, but only for adult writers and only after people re-key every doubtful field. University graders that read fixed answer boxes reach 88–95%. A phone-photo homework system with free-layout answers read 91% fully right. No independent study measures Grade 2–4 children. The targets of ≥95–97% agreement with teachers and ≤1% confidently wrong are therefore realistic for the **learning record**, meaning machine reading plus teacher review of what the machine routes. They are not realistic for the machine alone on legacy papers. The real trade-off is teacher minutes, not accuracy. For the 16 legacy layouts: draw every answer box once per layout, line each photo up with that template, and remove the teacher's red ink. Then read each box with two independent readers that never see the question, accept only when they agree, and have teachers confirm the rest in groups. For engine-printed sheets: add corner marks beside the QR code, one box per digit, printed option boxes for every non-numeric answer, and a separate working area. Today's 83 checked answers cannot prove either plan. What turns these estimates into facts is about 500 answers transcribed independently by two people, plus a permanent random audit of the answers the engine accepts on its own.

## Every working system fixes location before it reads; the engine does it the other way round

The engine works like a marker handed a page and told "the answers are somewhere on here". Amazon Textract's basic reader, DetectDocumentText, returns every word on the page with its position, printed and handwritten alike. It has no idea what a question or an answer slot is. Home-made geometry rules then guess which printed question each handwritten number belongs to, and the failure log shows where that breaks. The four largest failure classes hold 198 failures: **74** answers bound to the wrong question or sub-part, **74** readings below the 70% confidence floor, **30** answer types the reader was never built for (even/odd, "<", True/Not true ticks, orderings), and **20** questions not found.

That makes **94 location failures**: the 74 bound to the wrong slot plus the 20 questions never found.

This week's experiments fit the same picture. Re-reading flagged crops at 500 dpi recovered only 7 of 83, so image sharpness is not the main limit. Dropping the floor to 60 or 50 turned fragments into silent errors (a "72" read as "2"). The floor is a dial that trades teacher work for errors, not a guarantee. Finally, the team built a clean "blank" page by taking the typical value of each pixel across the children's copies. It exposed neat child digits that Textract had tagged PRINTED. But the extra visible ink let the geometry rules pick working instead of answers, which added 5 silent errors. Better reading made the guessing step worse.

The baseline is also less certain than it looks. 81.9% exact on 83 answers fits a true accuracy anywhere from **about 72% to 89%**. One confidently-wrong answer in 83 fits a true rate anywhere from **0.03% to 6.5%**. Both ranges are 95% intervals computed for this report with the Wilson and exact binomial methods.

**Gradescope**, the most widely used paper-exam grading platform in universities, never infers layout. The instructor uploads the blank worksheet and draws a box around each question once, and every scan is compared against that template ([Singh et al., 2017](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~pabbeel/papers/2017-LaS-gradescope.pdf)). Its current AI lays each submission over the blank template and keeps only the difference, which should be just the student's handwriting ([Gradescope Guides](https://guides.gradescope.com/hc/en-us/articles/24838908062093-AI-assisted-grading-and-answer-groups)). The AI then reads only short answers written on a single line, and only to **group identical answers**. A person confirms each group and grades it once; anything ungrouped is graded one by one. Gradescope requires the answer to sit on one line, "most easily enforced by providing a clear box or underscored area in the assignment template" ([Gradescope formatting guide](https://guides.gradescope.com/hc/en-us/articles/22238602932621-Formatting-Guide-for-AI-Assisted-Grading)). It publishes no accuracy figure for this reading, and it never awards a mark from handwriting without a person. One original design detail is telling: matching scripts to student names was deliberately kept manual but fast. The authors wrote that this "leads to a far lower error rate in name assignment than that shown by some of the automated systems" ([Singh et al., 2017](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~pabbeel/papers/2017-LaS-gradescope.pdf)).

**Crowdmark** prints a QR code on every page, so page identity and order are never guessed. Its AI reads only names written one character per box and multiple-choice bubbles. The company states that it "does not seek to replace educators by grading with AI" ([Crowdmark blog](https://www.crowdmark.com/blog/how-does-crowdmark-use-artificial-intelligence/); [Crowdmark matching](https://www.crowdmark.com/help/using-automated-matching/)).

**Exam boards** go further in the same direction. Pearson's ePEN scans scripts and cuts them into per-item "clips" using the known geometry of the answer booklet ([Pearson glossary](https://www1.edexcel.org.uk/ePEN-training/content/Glossary%20of%20Online%20Marking%20Terms%20Version%207.pdf)). A response that lies outside its clip is flagged "Out of Clip", and a senior examiner sees the whole script. Multi-part questions are split into separately marked "traits". Every marker is silently tested with pre-marked "validity items" at **1 in every 25 items**.

RM Assessor, used by many boards, lets machines mark only objective items such as multiple choice, and people mark every written answer ([RM](https://www.rm.com/assessment/collect-collate-and-mark)).

India's own large-scale test is a warning about a different failure. CBSE's first full on-screen marking in 2026 covered **98,66,622 answer books and about 40 crore scanned pages**, and it went to full scale without a phased pilot. It failed on capture and identity, not marking logic: blurred scans, missing pages, and students receiving another student's sheets. There were 4,04,319 applications for scanned copies, and the board's Chairman and Secretary were removed ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_CBSE_On-Screen_Marking_controversy); [The Probe](https://theprobe.in/education/cbse-osm-result-2026-examination-failure-india-11891206)).

The **research systems** that graded real handwritten maths in 2025–26 converge on the same recipe. UC Irvine ran its calculus pipeline for three terms on more than 1,000 students. Its answer sheet "separates each problem into a solution region and a final-answer region", and each region is read separately "to reduce cross-problem interference and hallucination". Over 90% of its scores landed within one point of the teaching assistants', but only 79.8% were fully correct ([Yu et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.00895)). At UIUC, Gemini 3 Flash reached 89–99% accuracy on individual rubric items in photographed work, and **87% of its errors came from misreading, not misjudging** ([Levine et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.19043)).

Blank boxes are a named hazard in these systems. UC Irvine's model wrote "full solutions" into empty boxes until the words "Solution" and "Final Answer" were printed inside them. A Ljubljana team added an "answer presence guardrail" after its model invented content for blank regions, which the authors called "operationally unacceptable" ([Yu et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.00895); [Perš et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.00730)).

None of these systems reaches human-level reliability without routing a large share to people. (R², used below, measures how closely two sets of scores move together; 1.0 is perfect.) Kortemeyer's calculus grader auto-accepted 81% of items when its scores tracked the teaching assistants' at an R² of 0.89, but only about 30% when the bar was raised to 0.95 ([Kortemeyer et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05162v2)). Ljubljana sent about 17% to manual review ([Perš et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.00730)). A Brussels study found "no stable threshold" that could safely skip human review, so instructors checked everything and still saved 23% of their time ([VUB, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.13083)). Pensieve, the one vendor-authored paper, has been used on more than 300,000 responses. It reports 95.4% accuracy on its high-confidence grades, but its time savings are modelled, not measured ([Pensieve, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.01431)).

Every one of these studies used university students.

Products for young children mostly avoid reading free handwriting at all. Kumon moved its worksheets onto tablets with a stylus, and instructors mark them ([Kumon](https://www.kumon.com/resources/how-kumon-connect-works/)). GradeCam reads handwritten digits only in a dedicated numeric question type, sold as an add-on ([Cult of Pedagogy](https://www.cultofpedagogy.com/gradecam/)).

A newer wave of photo-based graders claims 95–99%+. GradingPal claims "95%+ OCR accuracy" on early elementary handwriting, GradeLab "99%+", and Eklavvya in India "95%+" ([GradingPal](https://www.gradingpal.com/blog/how-gradingpal-s-ocr-technology-grades-handwritten-math-and-science-worksheets); [GradeLab](https://gradelab.io/ai-handwritten-grading); [Eklavvya](https://www.eklavvya.com/ai-answersheet-evaluation-school/)). None publishes its method or test data. These figures are usually per-character or per-response reading rates, not the end-to-end, per-answer-slot measure the engine reports.

Mathpix, the best-known commercial reader of handwritten maths, also publishes no accuracy percentage. In the one independent comparison, on hard university calculus solutions, a prompted GPT-4.1-mini produced acceptable transcriptions 84% of the time against Mathpix's 55%. The difference came mainly from the language model using the problem's context ([Mathpix](https://mathpix.com/handwriting-recognition); [Yu et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.00895)). As the third section shows, that same context is what makes a model rewrite a child's wrong answer. Mathpix's edge on advanced notation buys little for Grade 2–4 digits.

## Cloud form-readers can learn a layout, but none says how well it reads a child's digit

"Managed document AI" means renting a pre-trained reader from Amazon, Microsoft or Google and paying per page. Each now offers a way to teach it one layout. You upload a few filled-in examples and draw a box around each field, and it learns to return what is written in each box. That is exactly the binding step the engine lacks, so these services deserve a careful look.

**The legacy papers are too thin to train on.** Each of the 16 legacy layouts was sat by only 1–6 children. Azure needs about five copies per layout and Google needs six, so many legacy layouts cannot be trained at all. Microsoft also warns that synthetic values "can affect model performance", so padding the set with made-up copies is not a documented way out. Every new layout would need fresh labelling and retraining.

**For engine-printed papers the problem inverts.** The engine knows every box's coordinates because it drew them. Paying a service to *learn* a layout the engine already knows is wasted effort. Cutting out boxes at known coordinates is exact and free.

**No vendor publishes handwriting accuracy for digits.** AWS's own claim is that an adapter raised field-extraction F1 (a combined measure of what was found and what was missed) from 68% to 92% on bank cheques, in a 2023 blog post ([AWS ML blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/customize-amazon-textract-with-business-specific-documents-using-custom-queries)). The only independent head-to-head found used one legible cursive paragraph scanned at 300 dpi, and it was run by a company selling a competing product. It measured word error rates of **Azure 8.67%, Textract 10.5% and Google Document AI 23.3%** ([handwritingocr.com](https://www.handwritingocr.com/blog/best-ai-handwriting-ocr)). That is prose, where surrounding language helps the reader, so it says nothing reliable about a "43" written in pencil.

**Human review is left to the school.** Textract's confidence comes with no claim that it matches real accuracy. Azure's "19 out of 20" wording is a vendor claim, and Azure itself says to combine the field-position confidence with the word-reading confidence. Amazon's human-review service, A2I, "is no longer open to new customers", and it never natively covered DetectDocumentText or Queries ([AWS A2I](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/sagemaker/latest/dg/a2i-textract-task-type.html)). Whichever vendor is used, the school's own approval screen is the human loop.

**Teacher ticks are not covered.** All three services detect *printed* checkboxes. Textract does this only through its $0.05-per-page AnalyzeDocument call, not the $0.0015 call the engine uses now, and Google charges a $6-per-1,000-pages add-on. None of them documents reading a teacher's free-hand red tick.

**The services are built for whole pages, not crops.** Textract needs text at least 15 pixels tall and Azure at least 12 ([AWS quotas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/textract/latest/dg/limits-document.html); [Azure Read](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/document-intelligence/prebuilt/read?view=doc-intel-4.0.0)). A tight answer crop can fall below that, which fits the engine's crop misreads. In practice, keep one whole-page call and assign its words to drawn boxes by position. When a crop is read on its own, pad it and enlarge it.

**Cost is small at school volume, with one exception.** 10,000 pages cost about $15 for plain reading and $250–300 on any trained option. The one real fixed cost is Google's hosting fee, which could reach about $7,000 a year if each of the 16 layouts needed its own deployed version.

**Data can stay in India for the relevant features.** All three services allow this, except Google's generative extractor. By default, Textract "may store and use document and image inputs" to improve itself unless the AWS account opts out ([Textract FAQ](https://aws.amazon.com/textract/faqs/)). Because these are children's pages, the account should opt out.

**The verdict: do not adopt a managed template model now.** Keep Textract's cheap whole-page read as one of two readers, and replace the geometry rules with boxes the school draws itself. If a head-to-head is wanted later, Azure's custom template is the one to try on the layouts with five or more copies. It trains free in minutes and makes an explicit calibration claim that the school can test against its own answers.

## A reader that can see the question will "correct" the child

Reading a single cropped answer is close to solved, but only by a reader trained on the right population. One team fine-tuned TrOCR, a transformer-based handwriting reader, on handwritten 3–5-digit numbers cropped from old property cards. Its exact-match accuracy rose with the amount of training data ([Bhaskar et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.24676)). It reached **95%** with 500 training crops, **97.2%** with 5,000 and **98.7%** with 7,000 plus cheque digits, and rejecting its least-confident outputs pushed exact match to **99.4%**.

The same model trained only on bank-cheque digits scored **4.9%**. Digits from somewhere else do not transfer. GPT-4o, reading single cropped cells, "matches or exceeds" the fine-tuned model. On multi-cell regions it was "hit-or-miss" and sometimes hallucinated.

On 3,141 handwritten exam-answer letters, Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite read **97.6%** correctly with no training. It even recovered letters written outside their cells and answers that were crossed out and rewritten ([Grabowski, 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.11477)).

The evidence on children is thin. One 2025 study reports roughly double the character error rate on elementary-school handwriting compared with adult handwriting (3.48% vs 1.75%). It says current models "often autocorrect or misinterpret" children's letter forms, but only its abstract could be checked ([Springer, 2025](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-04614-7_7)). No public dataset of Grade 2–4 handwritten digits with published results was found.

The bigger danger is not misreading but **rewriting**. A vision-language model is an AI such as Claude, Gemini or GPT that looks at an image and writes text. It reads partly by seeing and partly by predicting the most probable text. When the question is visible, the most probable text in the answer slot is the correct answer, so a child's 397 gets pulled toward 387. The literature documents this directly. **Most models overwrite errors.** On 2,244 handwritten maths solutions with planted errors, **42–66% of transcriptions from 15 models overwrote at least one of the student's errors**. Larger models in a family did it more. "Don't correct" instructions gave only a "marginal reduction" while degrading transcription ([Seong et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.22774v2)). **Models fail by expectation more than people do.** On 500 real handwritten documents, **63–91% of model errors were "prior-driven"**, meaning the model wrote what it expected rather than what was there. The figure for humans was 49%; they stay "conservative on illegible content" ([WildHandBench, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22959)). **Older Claude models were the weakest readers of children's arithmetic.** On 288 handwritten answers from Ghanaian middle-school arithmetic worksheets, Claude 3.5 Sonnet read 79% correctly, Claude 3.7 read 76%, and Gemini 2.5 Pro read 89%. Claude 3.7 *graded* 84% correctly, better than it read. The authors warn this means a model may "use mathematical context to compensate for imperfect visual interpretation" ([Henkel et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.05538)). **Hinting the expected answer is worse still.** On the 3,141 exam answers, being told the expected letters raised false acceptances (a wrong answer read as the right one) from 1 to 193 for Grok-4.3, from 5 to 80 for GPT-5.2, and from 1 to 11 for Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite ([Grabowski, 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.11477)).

The engine's 397→387 incident had all three conditions the literature ties to rewriting: the question visible, a short number, and a strong model. It is not a one-off.

Models also invent answers for empty slots, and fixed settings do not stop it. On real handwritten medical forms read at temperature 0 (the setting that makes output deterministic) into a fixed JSON template, hallucination rates were still **6–23%**. When models cannot read a field they "do not flag uncertainty" but "infer the closest plausible alternative, or hallucinate some output entirely" ([Pather et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.16504)). The same study found Claude Sonnet 4.6 best on numbers and dates, at 77% with a 12% hallucination rate.

Two independent studies favour the Gemini Flash family for faithfulness. Gemini-3-Flash rewrote **0%** of planted scrambled words in printed documents, while some open models rewrote over 50% ([Lee et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.21617)). In Seong et al.'s faithfulness ranking, Gemini 2.5 Flash moved from 10th to 1st. Neither study used children's digit crops, so the model should be chosen by the school's own test, described below.

The measured evidence ranks the defences. **First, never show the reader the question, its numbers, or the expected answer.** Show only the crop of the answer box: the reader transcribes, and ordinary code then compares the transcription with the answer key. **Second, tell the reader the answer's format, not its value** (digits only; one of <, > or =; even or odd), which removes letter/digit confusions such as 0/O and 1/l that fine-tuning on digits also fixed ([Bhaskar et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.24676)).

**Third, use two readers of different kinds and accept a reading only when they agree exactly.** Across several vision models, **disagreement between different models was a better error flag than asking one model to judge itself or to re-sample**: routing the 7.3% most-disputed items to a stronger model gained 8.2 points on a reading benchmark, against 2.7 for one model sampled three times ([Zhang et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11101v4)). Older evidence agrees. Three-way voting among OCR runs removes **20–50% of errors** ([Lopresti & Zhou, 1997](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1077314296905020)), and double data entry beats a single keyer who visually checks their own work ([Barchard & Pace, 2011](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563211000707)). One extra tripwire follows from the Henkel result; it is an inference, not a published technique. If the vision AI's reading equals the arithmetically correct answer while the other reader disagrees, route it to a teacher. **Fourth, detect blanks in code.** Count pencil pixels in the box before any model is asked, so no reader can invent an answer for an empty slot and "blank" becomes a first-class result.

"Don't correct" prompts help only partly. One study found they cut rewriting by 33–47%, at a small cost in accuracy on clean text ([Lee et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.21617)). UC Irvine reports autocorrection under 2% with such an instruction ([Yu et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.00895)), while the maths-error study found only a "marginal" effect. The design should not depend on the prompt.

Ticks and symbols are a different problem. Measuring the ink in a printed box at a known position is very reliable: automated forms processing made **0.46 errors per 1,000 checkbox fields**, as good as two people keying independently ([Paulsen et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3320865/)). Vision AIs asked about checkboxes do worse. The best scored 83.2 against a human 97.5 on a checkbox benchmark ([CheckboxQA, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10419)). They also "infer a selected option despite the absence of a clear mark, effectively treating the visual layout itself as a signal" ([Pather et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.16504)).

People set the benchmark, and they fail differently. On clear arithmetic answers, trained raters' agreement "approached 100%" ([Henkel et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.05538)). Keying hand-printed numbers once produced **67 errors per 1,000 fields**, and double keying halved that ([Paulsen et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3320865/)). "Illegible" should therefore be a valid output that goes to a teacher, exactly as a careful human reader would say "can't read it".

Per-child handwriting profiles are not worth building yet. Adapting a reader to one writer cut character errors by about 5% relative with one line of that writer's text, 10–20% with 2–8 lines, and 20–45% with 16–256 lines ([Kohút & Hradiš, 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.06308)). Training on the school's whole population of writers is the big lever, as the TrOCR curve shows.

## Designed sheets remove location errors and halve reading errors, but cannot fix a seven-year-old's handwriting

Census bureaus and ICR vendors (ICR, "intelligent character recognition", is the industry name for reading handwritten characters in boxes) settled long ago on a standard set of form-design rules ([ABBYY, 2009](https://static1.abbyy.com/abbyycommedia/4673/abbyy-flexicapture-80-form-creation-guide.pdf); [UN Statistics Division, 2009](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/demographic-social/census/documents/CensusDataCaptureMethodology.pdf)). They put **one character in each separate box**, print the boxes in a light **"dropout" colour** that software removes so only the handwriting is left, place **four or five solid black registration squares** near the corners (one off-pattern, so the page's orientation is unambiguous), give every sheet **a machine-readable ID**, keep clear space between printed text and the answer fields, and print a worked example of how to fill in the form.

Legacy worksheets ask for answers as text on a line, after an "=" or on an underline. ABBYY's guide calls this layout "not recommended for forms requiring a high degree of recognition accuracy", and adds that "recognition is worst in the case of forms completed by pencil" ([ABBYY, 2009](https://static1.abbyy.com/abbyycommedia/4673/abbyy-flexicapture-80-form-creation-guide.pdf)). The school's papers have both problems.

The payoff from good design is well measured, but only for adults. **Separate boxes beat shared ones.** UN indicative figures put handwritten digits in separate boxes at **98% per character**, against 95–96% when several digits share one box. Handwritten letters score 75–90% ([UN, 2009](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/demographic-social/census/documents/CensusDataCaptureMethodology.pdf)). **Ticks read best.** In the England and Wales 2011 census, after people keyed every field the machine could not read confidently, tick marks were right **99.85%** of the time, numbers **98.93%** and mixed letters-and-numbers **97.58%** ([ONS, 2011](https://www.ons.gov.uk/file?uri=%2Fcensus%2F2011census%2Fhowourcensusworks%2Fhowdidwedoin2011%2F2011censusgeneralreport%2F2011censusgeneralreportforenglandandwaleschapter5_tcm77-384969.pdf)). **Automated reading cut staffing.** The US 2000 census read handwriting at 99.4–99.7% and cut keying staff "by as much as 75 percent". These are the vendor's figures ([Lockheed Martin, 2001](https://news.lockheedmartin.com/2001-01-08-Lockheed-Martins-Data-Capture-System-Processes-148-Million-Census-Forms-With-Record-Speed-and-Accuracy)).

Per-character figures compound. At 98% per digit, a three-digit answer is entirely right only about 94% of the time (0.98 × 0.98 × 0.98).

Free-layout school work reads worse. BAGS, a smartphone homework-grading system with answers on underlines, read **91%** of 6,068 answer areas fully right ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.03767)). It needed 9,000 labelled images just to learn where the answers were. Its 274 failures broke down as: **52** from failing to find the answer, **68** from misreading, and **154** from "ambiguous handwriting or low-quality images".

No controlled study compares the same children's answers on a designed sheet and a legacy sheet, so the gap has to be assembled from the pieces above. **Location failures disappear.** Design removes nearly all of them; they were 19% of BAGS's failures and about half of the engine's. **Reading errors roughly halve.** Per-character error falls from about 4–5% to about 2%. **Legibility does not change.** Grade 2 numerals were judged legible only **89%** of the time by trained raters in one handwriting test. That figure comes from a search-result abstract whose authors and sample could not be confirmed ([ETCH study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/336601860_Handwriting_Performance_of_Typical_Second-Grade_Students_as_Measured_by_the_Evaluation_Tool_of_Children's_Handwriting_-_Manuscript_and_Teacher_Perceptions_of_Legibility)).

That legibility ceiling is why children's sheets will always route some answers to a teacher.

Lining up a phone photo with the printed layout, called **registration**, works like laying a tracing over the page. The software finds known marks, computes the stretch that un-tilts the photo, and then every box sits at its printed coordinates. The evidence on how to do it. **Use four to six markers.** A print-science study found two markers "insufficient"; four to six gave consistent alignment, and larger markers were more reliable ([Štampfl & Ahtik, 2022](https://doi.org/10.14622/JPMTR-2216)). **Don't rely on the QR code alone.** It identifies the sheet but should not be the only anchor: in a 2026 phone-capture dataset, 11 of 780 photos (about 1.4%) had unreadable QR codes ([Wild-OMR, 2026](https://zenodo.org/records/21710005)). **Phones lose accuracy.** One open-source mark reader's developer claims "nearly 100%" on scans and "about 90%" on phone photos ([OMRChecker](https://github.com/Udayraj123/OMRChecker)). **Capture tools help.** Google's ML Kit Document Scanner captures automatically once it detects the page, and corrects edges and rotation ([ML Kit](https://developers.google.com/ml-kit/vision/doc-scanner)). Crowdmark flags a missing page with "a red band and a warning" at upload ([Crowdmark](https://www.crowdmark.com/help/fixing-errors-in-assessment-upload/)).

CBSE's failures are the case for checking every photo while the sheet is still in hand.

Two design choices need local testing because no study covers them. The first is box size for 7–9-year-olds. The adult standard is 4×5 mm, and boxes that are too large encourage "abnormally large letters" ([ABBYY, 2009](https://static1.abbyy.com/abbyycommedia/4673/abbyy-flexicapture-80-form-creation-guide.pdf)). Second-graders' writing size varies widely, and larger writing goes with worse quality ([Coradinho et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10047900/)).

The second is box colour. ABBYY prefers red-orange boxes, but those would collide with the teacher's red pen. Grey pencil, red teacher ink and light-green boxes can be separated by colour on a phone photo. That is an inference to verify, not a published result. Images should stay in greyscale or colour, never pure black-and-white, because a black-and-white cut-off erases faint pencil first.

## Eighty-three checked answers cannot prove 97%; about five hundred can

The engine does two different jobs, and each needs its own number. **Reading** is measured as **exact match**: the share of answer boxes where the engine's transcription equals a trusted human transcription character for character. Per-character scores are only for diagnosis, because one wrong digit makes the answer wrong. **Marking** is measured as **agreement with the teacher's tick or cross**. That checks the whole chain, reading plus answer key, so it cannot tell a reading error from a key error or a teacher slip. The trusted transcriptions (the "gold" set) should be double-keyed: two adults transcribe independently and a third settles any mismatch. Otherwise the gold itself carries errors about as large as the 1% target.

Small samples cannot prove high accuracy. A **lower bound** is the worst true accuracy the data can rule out with 95% confidence. Even if all 83 current answers had been right, that lower bound would be only **95.6%**. Proving that at most 1% of accepted answers are confidently wrong takes **299 auto-accepted answers with zero errors**. This is the statistical "rule of three", familiar from the example that 300 parachutes that all open show a failure rate under 1% ([Hanley & Lippman-Hand, 1983](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/385438)). The sample sizes below are exact binomial arithmetic, taken from the research notes and this report's own checks.

| Claim to prove | Errors observed | Answers needed |
|---|---|---|
| Confidently wrong ≤ 1% (among auto-accepted) | 0 / 1 / 2 / 3 | 299 / 473 / 628 / 773 accepted answers |
| Confidently wrong ≤ 3% | 0 / 1 / 2 | 99 / 157 / 208 |
| Reading accuracy ≥ 97% (80% chance of succeeding) | true accuracy 98.5% / 99% | about 652 / 332 |
| Teacher agreement, lower bound ≥ 95% | observed 97% / 98% | about 500 / 300 |
| Teacher crosses caught ≥ 95% | 0 missed | about 60 teacher crosses |

At today's 74% auto-accept rate, 299 accepted answers means about **400 checked answers in total**, or about 640 if one error turns up. These counts apply separately to every group that will run unattended: phone photos versus scans, each grade, numeric versus tick answers. A good overall average can hide one group that fails.

**A 95% agreement gate on its own is weak.** Suppose 10% of answers are wrong and the engine's only mistakes are missed crosses. It could then agree with the teacher 95% of the time while marking **half of all wrong answers as right**. Kappa measures agreement after removing what luck alone would produce, and in that case it would be only about 0.64. Educational measurement guards against this with three criteria for automated scoring ([ACT, 2021](https://www.act.org/content/dam/act/unsecured/documents/R2100-auto-scoring-standards-2021-07.pdf)): kappa of at least **0.70**; kappa no more than **0.10 below** the agreement between two humans marking the same answers; and exact agreement no more than about **5 points below** the human–human figure.

The engine's gate should also set a floor on the share of teacher crosses it catches, say ≥95%. It should trip on the lower bound, not on the observed rate.

**The 70% confidence floor guarantees nothing.** A confidence score is only useful if it is calibrated, meaning an answer scored "70% sure" is right about 7 times in 10. Textract makes no calibration claim. An industrial handwriting study found "substantial overlap between distributions of confident and erroneous readings" (paywalled; summary only) ([IJIDeM, 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12008-026-02513-9)). When GPT-4 graded handwritten university maths, about 27% of the grades it was "confident" about were wrong ([arXiv 2408.11728](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.11728v1)).

The auditable replacement is a **risk–coverage rule**. It works on a set of answers that people have already checked. Try candidate thresholds from strict to lenient, and at each one count how many answers would be auto-accepted and how many of those are wrong. Then pick the most lenient threshold whose upper bound on errors stays at or below 1%.

This is the practical form of "selective classification with guaranteed risk" and "Learn then Test" ([Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500); [Angelopoulos et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.01052v5)). The score being thresholded can combine OCR confidence, whether the two readers agree, and a format check. With 83 labels the rule will correctly answer "no threshold qualifies yet". It must be re-run for each group, because a threshold set on scans says nothing about phone photos.

**The two kinds of mistake may deserve different limits.** Marking a wrong answer right hides a learning gap; marking a right answer wrong is unfair to the child. One autograder, tested on about 10 million answers, found that demanding at most 2% wrong-marked-right and zero right-marked-wrong left only **about 10% of questions** gradable by machine ([Schneider et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.03425)).

**Teachers' own ticks are the right continuous check, but they are a reference, not gold.** Human error on repetitive grading runs at **0.5–6%** ([Schneider et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.03425)). Even exam boards' "definitive" marks are sometimes wrong. Maths is the most consistent subject: the chance of receiving the definitive grade is 0.96, against 0.52 in English literature ([Ofqual, 2018](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5bfbfd70e5274a0fb775cca3/Marking_consistency_metrics_-_an_update_-_FINAL64492.pdf)).

So every engine–teacher disagreement should go to a second adult who sees neither mark and decides what it was: a reading error, a key error or a teacher slip. A local teacher–teacher baseline, from 200–300 answers marked twice, is the only fair yardstick for "no worse than humans". No published figure exists for Grade 2–4 classroom items.

On legacy pages the red marks share the image with the child's pencil. They must be stripped before reading, or the reader can learn to key off them. The teacher's tick or cross is then read separately and used as the validation label. No published study compares an automated reader against teachers' own classroom ticks on primary worksheets, so this design is assembled by analogy.

**Operational systems also never stop sampling.** Exam boards slip pre-marked seed items to markers at about 5%, and Pearson uses 1 in 25 ([Ofqual, 2018](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5bfbfd70e5274a0fb775cca3/Marking_consistency_metrics_-_an_update_-_FINAL64492.pdf); [Pearson glossary](https://www1.edexcel.org.uk/ePEN-training/content/Glossary%20of%20Online%20Marking%20Terms%20Version%207.pdf)). Massachusetts has humans re-score **10%** of machine-scored essays in grades 3–8 ([MA DESE, 2024](https://www.doe.mass.edu/mcas/student/2024/scoring.html)). Smarter Balanced sends 20–40% of responses to humans, counting both low-confidence answers and a monitoring sample ([Smarter Balanced FAQ](https://smarterbalanced.alohahsap.org/content/contentresources/en/Smarter_Interim_Automated_Scoring_FAQ.pdf)).

The detail that matters most is this. The answers routed to teachers are the hard ones by design, so they cannot estimate live accuracy. Only a **random audit of auto-accepted answers** can. Every teacher confirmation should therefore be labelled "routed" or "audit", and the two should never be pooled.

**High-stakes programmes switch on automation in stages**, with pass criteria fixed before any results are seen ([ACT, 2021](https://www.act.org/content/dam/act/unsecured/documents/R2100-auto-scoring-standards-2021-07.pdf); [ETS, 2014](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1109327.pdf); [GAO, 2000](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-AIMD-00-324R/html/GAOREPORTS-AIMD-00-324R.htm)). **Shadow.** The machine marks, but its verdict stays hidden while humans mark as usual. **Check-score.** A human's mark stands, and the machine only flags disagreements. **Machine-first with human read-behind.** The machine marks, and humans re-score a sample.

If the audit's upper bound crosses the limit, the system falls back automatically to check-score.

## Two pipelines: rescue the sixteen legacy layouts, design the sheets the engine prints

The approaches compare as follows. The teacher-share figures are extrapolations. They combine today's 26%, the 17–40% that research and operational systems route, and the per-character figures above. Treat them as planning numbers until the school's own checked answers replace them.

| Approach | How it knows where the answer is | Machine reads right | Can ≤1% confidently wrong be enforced? | Share to teacher | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Today: whole-page Textract + geometry rules + 70% floor | Guessed from the ink | 81.9% (true value plausibly 72–89%) | No; the floor is not calibrated | 26% | School's own data, 83 answers |
| B. Whole-page vision AI with the question visible | The model reasons about the page | 76–89% on children's arithmetic worksheets | No; 42–66% of transcriptions overwrite a student error | Unknown | Independent, but older models and other ages |
| C. Managed template model (Azure or Google) | Learned from 3–5+ labelled copies per layout | Unpublished for handwritten digits | Only after the school calibrates it | Unknown | Vendor documents only |
| D. Legacy: school-drawn boxes, photo alignment, red-ink removal, two readers who must agree, grouped teacher confirmation | Drawn once per layout | About 90–95% (91% on free-layout phone homework; 89% best reader on children's worksheets) | Yes, by routing disagreements; provable after about 400–650 checked answers | About 20–35% (estimate) | Adjacent evidence; no Grade 2–4 study |
| E. Engine-printed sheet: QR, corner marks, one box per digit, option boxes, same two readers, school-trained digit reader later | Printed coordinates | Ticked options about 99.8% (adult census); digits 98% per character, so about 94–96% per multi-digit answer for one reader | Yes, with a smaller queue | About 10–20% of digit answers at first, falling as the school's reader learns; near zero for ticked options (estimate) | Adult census data plus in-domain training curves; children unmeasured |

Under D and E, suppose auto-accepted answers are at most 1% wrong and teachers resolve the rest with a few percent error. The **learning record** is then roughly **97–99% right**, and the difference between the two pipelines is mainly teacher time.

### Legacy papers: stop guessing where the answer is

For each of the 16 layouts, take one clean image of the page. A blank scan works, and so does the median-averaged image the team already built, which suits this purpose well. On it, draw one box per sub-part answer slot, and tag each box with its answer type: a number, even/odd, <, > or =, a tick option, or an ordering. This is Gradescope's outline and the exam boards' clip map, done once per layout by a person, with no training data.

Each photo then goes through these steps. **Line it up with its template.** Legacy sheets have no corner marks, so use the printed text Textract already finds as the anchor points. If the fit is poor, send the page to a teacher rather than guess. **Remove the teacher's red ink by colour** before any reading. Kortemeyer's team removed teaching assistants' marks this way ([Kortemeyer et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05162v2)). The removed strokes are then read separately as that question's tick or cross. **Subtract the template** so only pencil remains, as Gradescope's overlay does. **Check each box for pencil**, so a blank box is recorded as blank. **Apply Pearson's "out of clip" rule.** If pencil crosses a box edge, or sits just outside an empty box, that region of the page goes to the teacher. This rule replaces the geometry heuristics that picked working over answers. **Read each box twice, independently.** One reading is the Textract words that fall inside the box, from the whole-page call already paid for. The other is a vision AI that sees only the padded crop and is told the answer's format and nothing about the question. **Accept only exact agreement.** Send disagreements to the teacher, plus any case where the vision AI's reading is the correct answer but Textract's is not.

Non-numeric answers get their own handling. For printed tick options, compare the ink in each option box against the other options in the same question, not against a fixed cut-off. Answers like even/odd and "<" are read against their small allowed vocabulary. They are then shown to the teacher as **groups of identical-looking crops to approve once**. That is Gradescope's answer grouping, and it fits the engine's "engine prepares, person approves" principle.

Marking stays in code: compare the confirmed reading with the key. The legacy set is small and finite. Its most valuable output is not a perfect read but the **checked-answer set** that calibrates everything else. So double-key all 867 existing answers first, before tuning anything.

### Engine-printed papers: design the reading problem away

The engine already puts a QR code on each sheet, and the rest of the design follows from the evidence above. Add four solid black **corner squares** of about 5–8 mm, at least 8 mm from the page edge, and set the QR beside one corner so it doubles as the orientation mark. BAGS placed its ID next to a correction corner for the same reason: distortion is smallest there. Print every answer box at **coordinates the engine records at render time**, so no template model is needed and binding errors disappear by construction. Give numeric answers **one box per digit**. Turn every non-numeric answer into **printed options to tick**: <, > and =, even/odd, True/Not true, and orderings as numbered boxes. Tick marks are the most reliably read field type in every census. Keep a **separate working area** well away from the answer boxes. Add a small printed label at each answer box (UC Irvine printed "Final Answer" inside its boxes to stop readers inventing answers for blanks) and one worked example at the top of the sheet. Print the boxes in light green or grey, never red (the teacher's pen) and never blue (it merges with blue ink). Finally, **pilot two or three box sizes**, for example 7, 9 and 11 mm, with one class, and measure how often children write over the edge before fixing the size.

At upload, reject a photo while the sheet is still in hand unless three checks pass: the QR decodes, all four corners are found, and a sharpness score clears a level tuned on the school's own photos.

Reading uses the same two-reader agreement as the legacy pipeline. Once teacher confirmations have produced a few thousand labelled crops, fine-tune a small digit reader on them as a third, school-specific reader. The published training curve is the reason to expect the teacher queue to shrink over the first terms: 95% exact match at 500 crops, 97.2% at 5,000, and 99.4% when the least-confident readings are rejected.

### What to measure, and when it counts as proved

| Measure | Plain meaning | Gate | Sample |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact reading accuracy, by group | Engine's transcription equals the double-keyed transcription | Lower bound ≥ 97% for any group that runs unattended | About 330–650 per group |
| Confidently-wrong rate | Share of auto-accepted answers read wrong | Upper bound ≤ 1% | 299 accepted with 0 errors, or 473 with 1 |
| Coverage | Share accepted without a teacher | Reported, not gated; this is the teacher-workload number | Every batch |
| Agreement with teacher ticks | Engine's right/wrong matches the teacher's | Lower bound ≥ 95%, kappa ≥ 0.70, crosses caught ≥ 95% | About 300–500 answers; about 60 crosses |
| Teacher–teacher baseline | Two teachers marking the same answers | Engine within 0.10 kappa of it | 200–300 double-marked answers |
| Faithfulness test | On crops the teacher marked wrong, how often a reader outputs the correct answer instead of what was written | Zero from at least 300 wrong-answer crops | Run before choosing a vision model |
| Live audit | Random re-check of auto-accepted answers | 5–10% indefinitely; automatic fallback to teacher review if the upper bound passes 1% | Continuous |

Report each group separately: phone photos versus scans, grade, layout, answer type, and blank versus written.

Two experiments should come first because no published study answers them. The first gives the same vision AI the same crops twice, once with the question visible and once with the crop alone. The second is the faithfulness test above, run on every candidate reader, with the model chosen on its result rather than on published rankings.

Three open questions sit outside this research and must be checked before building. Where does each candidate vision AI process images, and does that keep children's work in India? What does each cost per crop? And how often do two different readers agree on the same *wrong* digit? Only local data can answer the last one.

## Conclusion

The founder's target is really a **routing** target dressed as a reading target. No reader in the published evidence, human or machine, reads unconstrained children's handwriting at ≥97% exact while being confidently wrong on at most 1% of everything. Every system that meets targets like these does it the same way: it knows where each answer is before reading, accepts only readings it can cross-check, routes the rest to a person, and measures its silent-error rate with a random audit rather than trusting a confidence score.

The engine's accuracy problem is therefore mostly an engineering and paper-design problem that others have already solved. The open question can be answered only from the school's own data: how many teacher minutes the honest version costs on legacy papers, and how far designed sheets shrink them.

Two lessons cut against intuition. First, a smarter reader is a liability here. The more capable the model and the more context it sees, the more confidently it writes what *should* be there instead of what the child wrote. A narrow reader that sees only the crop, checked against a second reader of a different kind, is the safer design. Second, the teachers' red ticks already on every legacy page are the school's most valuable asset for this problem. They give a free, continuous validation signal that most edtech products lack. That only holds if three conditions are met: the reader never sees the ticks, a third adult settles disagreements instead of counting them against the engine, and the numbers are reported as bounds rather than point estimates.
