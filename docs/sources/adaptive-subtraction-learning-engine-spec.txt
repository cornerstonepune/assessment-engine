ADAPTIVE LEARNING ENGINE
Subtraction: From Meaning to Mastery
Comprehensive Product, Pedagogy, Curriculum, AI &amp; Engineering Specification
Purpose: a build-ready specification for an original personalized learning engine inspired by publicly described mastery-learning patterns, not a copy of any proprietary school or software.
Version 1.0 | September 2026
1. Executive Summary
This document specifies a narrow but complete prototype of an adaptive learning engine for subtraction. The system should take a learner from the most primitive question — “What does subtraction mean?” — through mental strategies, number relationships, place value, regrouping, multiple written methods, and accurate three-digit subtraction, while continually adapting to the learner’s demonstrated knowledge, misconceptions, pace, confidence, and preferred representations.
The goal is not to build a chatbot that explains subtraction. The goal is to build a closed learning loop: diagnose → model the learner → choose the next best learning experience → interact → assess reasoning → update the learner model → decide what happens next. This is the central design principle.
Core design decision:  The LLM is a component, not the educational architecture. The architecture must own the curriculum graph, mastery rules, misconception taxonomy, item selection, safety constraints, evidence collection, and progression logic.
Why subtraction is an ideal pilot:  Subtraction exposes conceptual gaps in quantity, comparison, part-whole thinking, number line reasoning, place value, regrouping, notation, and procedural fluency. It is therefore an unusually good stress test for true personalization.

3. Product Thesis
3.1 What we are trying to prove
A learner can begin at an unknown point and the system can rapidly locate the highest-value missing prerequisite without forcing the child through a fixed grade-level sequence.
The same curriculum can produce materially different learning paths for different learners because the engine uses evidence rather than age/grade as the primary determinant of next steps.
The system can teach conceptual meaning before procedural shortcuts, then connect concepts to strategies and algorithms.
The system can detect not just wrong answers but likely misconceptions and choose a useful repair interaction.
The system can make learning feel purposeful by repeatedly connecting subtraction to decisions, quantities, trade-offs, measurement, money, distance and everyday situations.
The system can verify mastery through a combination of accuracy, explanation/reasoning, transfer and delayed retrieval — not a single quiz score.
3.2 What this prototype is not
It is not a generic ChatGPT math tutor.
It is not a collection of video lessons followed by worksheets.
It is not a fixed linear course where every learner sees the same sequence.
It is not a system that equates 90% accuracy on one screen with deep understanding.
It is not a clone of Alpha School, TimeBack or another proprietary platform.
4. Design Principles
Principle
Implication
Meaning before mechanics
Students first construct the idea of taking away, comparing, finding a difference, and finding an unknown part. Written procedures come later.
Concrete → visual → symbolic
Use manipulable quantities, visual representations and symbolic notation as connected views of the same idea.
Multiple strategies
Students should learn more than one sensible strategy and eventually choose an efficient method.
Context before abstraction, then abstraction
Application motivates and reveals the need for the mathematics; the engine then deliberately abstracts the underlying structure.
Reasoning is observable
The engine must collect evidence about how a child thinks, not only whether the final number is correct.
Adaptive, not chaotic
Personalization must remain inside a controlled curriculum graph with explicit prerequisites and validated outcomes.
Mastery is multidimensional
Accuracy, consistency, explanation, transfer, retention and sensible method selection all matter.
Struggle is useful; confusion is not
The system should challenge students, but avoid needless cognitive load, random difficulty spikes or repeated failure without diagnostic purpose.
Technology where it adds leverage
Use AI for adaptation, explanation, generation, simulation and analysis; preserve human guidance for motivation, relationships and judgment.
Everything is instrumented
Every response, hint, attempt, representation switch, confidence estimate and misconception signal should create useful learning data.
Original content and safety
Do not reproduce copyrighted/proprietary lessons. Generate or author original examples, visuals and wording under controlled schemas.

8. Adaptive Learning Loop
Every interaction should move through the same control loop. The “next best activity” engine should be deterministic where safety and curriculum integrity matter, and generative where wording/examples can be personalized.
1. Observe: capture answer, path, time, hints, representation, explanation, confidence.
2. Interpret: classify outcome + likely misconception + evidence strength.
3. Update learner state.
4. Identify eligible concepts from prerequisite graph.
5. Score candidate activities.
6. Select next action.
7. Render the experience.
8. Reassess pivotal knowledge.
9. Update mastery and schedule retention checks.
8.1 Candidate scoring
candidate_score =
0.30 * learning_need
+ 0.20 * prerequisite_priority
+ 0.15 * transfer_value
+ 0.10 * retention_due
+ 0.10 * confidence_mismatch
+ 0.05 * strategy_diversification
+ 0.05 * learner_interest_fit
+ 0.05 * engagement_recovery
- penalties_for_recent_repetition
- penalties_for_excessive_difficulty
These weights are starting defaults, not sacred constants. The prototype should log them so they can be experimentally tuned.

10. Detailed Curriculum Specification
10.1 What subtraction means
Learning objective: Understand subtraction as a relationship between quantities: taking away, finding what remains, comparing quantities, and finding an unknown part.
Prerequisites: Counting and one-to-one correspondence.
Experience design: Use physical/digital objects such as blocks, fruit, toys, seats or coins. The learner physically/remotely removes some objects and predicts the remainder. Then introduce “−” only after the relationship is experienced.
Mastery evidence: Learner can explain in words what subtraction is finding and can match a story, model and expression.
Common errors to detect: “Minus means make the number smaller” with no attention to context; reversing start and amount taken; treating subtraction as a symbol-matching exercise.
Application opportunities: Items removed from a collection, snacks eaten, people leaving a queue, seats occupied vs. empty.
Representative items / interactions:
Start with 7 stars. Remove 2. “What changed? What stayed the same?”
Show 9 counters with 4 hidden. “How many are hidden?”
Present 8 − 3 and ask the learner to build a matching story rather than solve it first.
10.2 Subtraction as missing part
Learning objective: Understand that subtraction can find an unknown part of a known whole and is therefore linked to addition.
Prerequisites: What subtraction means; part-whole representations.
Experience design: Use 10-frame, bar model and “known whole / known part / unknown part” stories. Ask learner to decide whether to subtract or add and explain why.
Mastery evidence: Learner can solve unknown-part problems and explain the addition/subtraction relationship.
Common errors to detect: Confusing total with missing part; always “taking away” even when the story is comparison or missing-part.
Application opportunities: Inventory packed vs. remaining, children on a bus, pieces completed in a project.
Representative items / interactions:
10 total, 6 red. How many blue?
You need 12 cups and have 7. How many more?
Write two related equations for the same situation.
10.3 Difference and comparison
Learning objective: Interpret subtraction as distance/difference between two quantities.
Prerequisites: Part-whole understanding.
Experience design: Use two rows of objects, horizontal bar models and a number line. Move from “how many more?” to a symbolic difference.
Mastery evidence: Learner solves compare situations correctly and can articulate that the difference is the amount that bridges two quantities.
Common errors to detect: Always subtract smaller from larger without interpreting the story; confusing difference with larger quantity.
Application opportunities: Price differences, age gaps, heights, scores, distances.
Representative items / interactions:
Mira has 13 points and Leo has 9. How many more points?
Which number is farther from 20: 14 or 17? Why?
Show 18 and 11 on a number line and find the distance.
10.4 Number line and counting strategies
Learning objective: Use counting back, counting up, and jumps as efficient strategies for subtraction.
Prerequisites: Difference and comparison; reliable counting.
Experience design: Interactive number line where jumps are visible. Compare counting back from the minuend with counting up from the subtrahend. Let the learner choose the efficient route.
Mastery evidence: Learner can solve small-subtraction problems using a meaningful counting strategy and choose between methods.
Common errors to detect: Counting one-by-one when a larger jump is available; losing track of inclusivity; confusing direction.
Application opportunities: Walking distance, scores, floors in a building, temperature changes.
Representative items / interactions:
17 − 9: count back or count up. Which feels shorter?
Solve 32 − 29 on a number line without subtracting ones one-by-one.
Explain why counting up works for a difference problem.
10.5 Flexible mental strategies
Learning objective: Develop a repertoire including compensation, decomposition, near tens, and use of known addition facts.
Prerequisites: Counting strategies; basic number relationships.
Experience design: Present the same problem with two or three possible strategies and ask the learner to compare efficiency. Example: 32 − 19 can become 32 − 20 + 1.
Mastery evidence: Learner can produce at least two valid strategies for representative problems and choose a sensible one.
Common errors to detect: Rigid dependence on one method; applying compensation in the wrong direction; losing track of the adjustment.
Application opportunities: Quick calculations in shopping, scoring, packing, travel, budgeting.
Representative items / interactions:
32 − 19
50 − 27
71 − 38
Ask: “Which would you rather calculate mentally, 52−49 or 52−27? Why?”
10.6 Place value as units
Learning objective: Understand that multi-digit numbers are composed of units: ones, tens, hundreds, and that units can be composed/decomposed while preserving total value.
Prerequisites: Number sense; tens/ones.
Experience design: Base-ten blocks, bundled straws, coins or digital manipulatives. Explicitly connect 1 ten = 10 ones and 1 hundred = 10 tens.
Mastery evidence: Learner can represent, decompose and recompose numbers flexibly and explain equivalence.
Common errors to detect: Treating digits independently; thinking “4” in 43 means four ones; believing a trade changes the number.
Application opportunities: Money denominations, inventory bundles, packaging, distance in hundreds/tens/meters.
Representative items / interactions:
Build 43 in at least two ways.
What can you trade one ten for?
Are 43 and 30+13 equivalent? Explain.
10.7 Two-digit subtraction without regrouping
Learning objective: Connect place-value structure to two-digit subtraction when each place can be subtracted independently.
Prerequisites: Place value; subtraction meaning.
Experience design: Use a place-value mat: subtract tens from tens and ones from ones. Move between concrete, expanded and column notation.
Mastery evidence: Accurate solution plus correct alignment/explanation of place values.
Common errors to detect: Subtracting digits without regard to place; misalignment; incorrect ordering.
Application opportunities: Prices, distances, scores, quantities of items.
Representative items / interactions:
54−21 using blocks
54−21 using expanded form 50−20 and 4−1
54−21 in columns; explain why the columns line up.
10.8 Regrouping: composing and decomposing a ten
Learning objective: Understand why a ten can be exchanged for ten ones when the ones place lacks enough units to subtract.
Prerequisites: Place value; 1 ten = 10 ones.
Experience design: Use explicit trade interaction: represent 42 as 4 tens and 2 ones; for 42−17, trade one ten for ten ones; observe 3 tens and 12 ones; subtract 7 ones, then 1 ten.
Mastery evidence: Learner can perform and explain regrouping with a model and connect the trade to the symbolic notation.
Common errors to detect: “Borrowing” as arbitrary; forgetting to reduce the tens; thinking the total value changed; subtracting 7 from 2 and recording 5.
Application opportunities: Change-making, exchanging rupees, converting a 10-item bundle, meter-to-centimeter analogy.
Representative items / interactions:
42−17 with blocks
42−17 using a drawn place-value chart
Ask: “Why are we allowed to change 4 tens 2 ones into 3 tens 12 ones?”
10.9 Alternative strategies for multi-digit subtraction
Learning objective: Understand that multi-digit subtraction can be solved via decomposition, compensation, counting-up and partial differences.
Prerequisites: Place value; regrouping; mental strategies.
Experience design: Show multiple methods for the same problem. Ask learner to estimate which is efficient before solving.
Mastery evidence: Learner can correctly execute at least two strategies, compare them, and recognize when a method is useful.
Common errors to detect: Compensation adjustment errors; partial differences sign errors; inappropriate method selection.
Application opportunities: Budget difference, distances, population changes, inventory.
Representative items / interactions:
83−47 by breaking 47 into 40+7.
83−47 by adding up from 47 to 83.
83−47 using 83−50+3.
Compare three solution paths and rank by ease.
10.10 Column method / standard algorithm
Learning objective: Accurately execute the standard column algorithm and understand every symbolic transformation as a place-value operation.
Prerequisites: Place value, regrouping concept, two-digit subtraction.
Experience design: Introduce the compact algorithm only after the learner can do the same problem with a place-value model. Animate each written mark: “cross out 4 tens, rewrite as 3 tens; 2 ones becomes 12 ones.”
Mastery evidence: High accuracy on mixed examples, correct regrouping sequence, ability to explain at least one regrouping step and check the answer.
Common errors to detect: “Borrow from the next digit” without value interpretation; crossing out incorrectly; failing to propagate across zero.
Application opportunities: Written calculation used for larger quantities, budgeting, inventory and measurement.
Representative items / interactions:
57−29
64−38
90−47
Ask learner to debug a deliberately incorrect column solution.
10.11 Three-digit subtraction
Learning objective: Extend the same unitizing and regrouping logic to hundreds, tens and ones, including multiple regrouping and zero cases.
Prerequisites: Column method; hundreds place value.
Experience design: Use dynamic base-ten blocks and then symbolic column layout. Explicitly test 3-digit problems without regrouping, with one regroup, with several regroupings, and with zeros.
Mastery evidence: Learner can solve a representative mixed set, explain regrouping across places, estimate before solving and check after solving.
Common errors to detect: Failure to regroup through zeros; subtracting smaller digit from larger regardless of top/bottom; place-value drift.
Application opportunities: Money, population, stock, travel distance, event attendance, project quantities.
Representative items / interactions:
583−241
583−267
704−286
900−457
1,000−368 as optional extension.
10.12 Estimation, checking and error detection
Learning objective: Use estimation, inverse operations and reasoning to detect implausible results.
Prerequisites: Fluent subtraction and addition relationships.
Experience design: Give a proposed answer and ask “Could this possibly be right?” before calculating. Then verify by addition or an alternative strategy.
Mastery evidence: Learner regularly estimates/checks and can identify a plausible vs. implausible result.
Common errors to detect: Assuming calculator-like exactness is always enough; checking only after being told an answer is wrong.
Application opportunities: Shopping totals, travel distances, score changes, quantities used.
Representative items / interactions:
“583−267=416. Does that seem reasonable?”
Estimate to the nearest ten before solving.
Find and explain the error in 704−286=582.
10.13 Transfer and multi-step problems
Learning objective: Choose subtraction appropriately in novel situations and combine it with other reasoning without being told the operation.
Prerequisites: Prior subtraction concepts and sufficient number fluency.
Experience design: Present realistic scenarios with irrelevant information, missing data, multiple steps or unfamiliar representations. Require the learner to decide what the question is asking.
Mastery evidence: Learner identifies when subtraction is needed, chooses a method, computes accurately, explains and checks.
Common errors to detect: Keyword matching (“less” = subtract) without semantic reasoning; using every number in the prompt; premature calculation.
Application opportunities: Receipts, schedules, journey planning, inventory, sports analytics, school events, project budgets.
Representative items / interactions:
A store has 240 notebooks. It sells 87 in the morning and 63 in the afternoon. How many remain?
A trip is 318 km. You have traveled 179 km. How far remains?
Create your own subtraction problem from a given data table.

14. AI Tutor Specification
14.1 Role
The AI tutor is an adaptive instructional component operating inside a bounded curriculum. It should be warm, concise, curious and demanding, but should not invent curriculum facts or override the engine’s progression decisions.
14.2 System prompt skeleton
You are the instructional dialogue component inside an adaptive math learning engine.
You are not the curriculum engine. You do not decide which concept is next unless the
controller explicitly asks you to recommend a candidate.
INPUTS YOU RECEIVE:

- prerequisite status
- student mastery estimates
- active misconception hypotheses
- previous attempts
- preferred/previously successful representations

20. Acceptance Criteria / Definition of Done
Two students with different diagnostic states receive different starting points and different next activities.
The engine can correctly route a student from a regrouping error to a place-value repair activity.
A learner can solve the same subtraction using two methods and compare them.
The student is explicitly shown the equivalence between a concrete trade and the symbolic regrouping notation.
The system can handle 3-digit subtraction including cases with zeros.
The system can distinguish routine computation mastery from transfer mastery.
The teacher dashboard can explain at least the top 3 reasons a learner is not advancing.
Every interaction is logged with enough information to reconstruct why the engine chose the next action.
The same underlying concept can be rendered in multiple contexts without changing the mathematics.
The coding agent can run automated tests for arithmetic, mastery gates, prerequisite routing and state transitions.

22. Research Basis, Alpha Analogy &amp; Caveats
This product concept is informed by three evidence families: (1) Alpha School’s public description of its mastery-based operating model, (2) established mathematics education guidance on conceptual understanding, representations and flexible strategies, and (3) learning-science guidance on spacing, retrieval and connecting concrete and abstract representations.
22.1 What we can responsibly borrow from Alpha’s public model
Alpha publicly describes personalized dashboards, individual pacing, adaptive apps and high mastery thresholds. citeturn451398search0turn451398search1
Alpha publicly describes TimeBack as a technology operating system with real-time skill tracking, goals, motivation and adaptive tutoring. citeturn451398search5
Alpha’s public materials emphasize that the human guide remains responsible for motivation and relationships rather than acting as a traditional lecturer. citeturn451398search4turn451398search7
Alpha has described its current learning engine as involving diagnostic mapping, dynamic lesson generation, feedback loops, verification and mastery tracking. citeturn451398search2
22.2 What we should not assume
Public claims about extremely high learning compression are not themselves independent proof of causality. The prototype should measure its own outcomes.
A high mastery threshold does not automatically equal durable learning; retention and transfer must be separately tested.
An AI-generated explanation is not automatically better than a teacher explanation. The engine should use evidence and experimentation to find out.
Gamification can increase engagement but can also distort incentives. Learning objectives must remain primary.
22.3 Mathematics education basis
The progression in this specification deliberately follows a meaning → representation → strategy → algorithm arc. NCTM’s guidance states that conceptual understanding should precede and coincide with procedural instruction and that students need a repertoire of strategies rather than being limited to one method. citeturn451398search54 The National Academies describe children moving from intuitive/concrete methods toward more abstract, problem-independent methods and note that multi-digit subtraction algorithms can be learned meaningfully when connected to appropriate experiences. citeturn909795search5turn909795search14 The U.S. What Works Clearinghouse recommends using visual representations and connecting concrete and abstract representations, as well as spacing learning over time and using retrieval practice. citeturn909795search0turn909795search2

23. Final Design Position
The first prototype should be judged by whether it can make a child’s path through subtraction genuinely different because of evidence gathered from that child — not because the AI changed the wording of the same worksheet. The strongest signal of success will be a system that notices a learner’s specific misconception, creates a targeted experience, verifies repair, and later proves retention and transfer.
Build philosophy:  Build the learning engine first, then make the experience beautiful. A polished interface wrapped around a weak student model is an ed-tech demo. A strong learner model coupled to a controlled curriculum and thoughtful interaction loop is the beginning of a product.
